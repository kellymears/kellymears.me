**An infographic** combines charts, icons, illustrations, maps, and brief text into a single graphic meant to explain a subject quickly. Where a chart shows one set of numbers, an infographic tells a short story, often with a headline, a sequence, and a conclusion.

Statistical graphics in the nineteenth century set the pattern. Florence Nightingale's 1858 diagrams showed that most British soldiers in the Crimean War died of preventable disease rather than wounds, and helped her argue for sanitary reform. Charles Minard's 1869 map of Napoleon's march on Moscow shows the army's shrinking size, its route, and the temperature on the retreat in a single image, and it's often called one of the best statistical graphics ever drawn.

In 1920s Vienna, the philosopher and economist Otto Neurath and a team of collaborators developed Isotype, a system of standardized pictograms in which each icon stands for a fixed quantity, so five figures mean five times as many people as one. The aim was to make social statistics readable by anyone, regardless of language or education; see [[Iconography]]. Modern signage and much of today's chart iconography descend from it.

Newspapers and magazines made infographics routine. USA Today, launched in 1982, put a small illustrated chart on the front of each section. The designer Nigel Holmes made playful, illustrated charts for Time magazine. The statistician Edward Tufte criticized that style as chartjunk, decoration that adds nothing to the data; see [[Data-Ink Ratio]]. A 2010 study found that people remembered embellished charts better after a delay without reading them less accurately, which complicated the argument without settling it.

Around 2010, infographics became a marketing format. Companies commissioned tall, scrolling graphics on any subject because other sites linked to them, which improved search rankings; see [[Search Engine Optimization]] and [[Backlink]]. Many had weak sources and arbitrary chart choices, and the format's reputation suffered.

Good infographics share a few habits:

- **One message.** A clear headline says what the reader should take away; see [[Visual Hierarchy]].
- **A reading path.** Layout leads the eye through the argument in order, usually top to bottom; see [[Grid System]].
- **Honest encoding.** Pictogram sizes and chart scales match the numbers. Scaling an icon in both directions to show doubling quadruples its area.
- **Plain words.** Labels and captions are short and specific; see [[Plain Language]].
- **Sources.** The data's origin is stated, so readers can check it.

Newsrooms now also build interactive and scrolling versions, combining reporting with maps and charts; see [[Data Journalism]] and [[Choropleth Map]]. Diagrams of networks, like the subway map, are a related tradition; see [[Transit Map]].
