**Helvetica** is a sans-serif typeface designed in 1957 by Max Miedinger with Eduard Hoffmann at the Haas type foundry near Basel, Switzerland. It was released as Neue Haas Grotesk and renamed in 1960 by the German foundry Stempel, which licensed it internationally; the new name came from Helvetia, the Latin name for Switzerland, chosen to sell its Swiss origins abroad.

The design refined an older model. Akzidenz-Grotesk, a German sans-serif from the 1890s, was the favored face of Swiss designers, and Helvetica set out to make something smoother and more uniform. It belongs to the style called **neo-grotesque**: strokes of nearly even thickness, horizontal ends on curved strokes such as the c and s, a large x-height that makes lowercase letters look big for their size, and tight spacing that makes words read as solid shapes; see [[Typeface]] and [[Kerning]].

It arrived at the right moment. Swiss designers were developing a rational approach built on grids, flush-left text, and objective presentation, and Helvetica became its signature typeface; see [[Swiss Style]] and [[Grid System]]. As multinational companies adopted modernist identities in the 1960s and 1970s, many chose it, and a long list of airline, retail, and technology logos were set in it; see [[Logo]]. Its apparent lack of personality was the point: it seemed to let content speak without decoration, in line with the modernist conviction that form should follow purpose; see [[Form Follows Function]].

Public spaces spread it further. Transit systems and governments used it on signs, and New York's subway, after years of mixed lettering, standardized on Helvetica in its sign manual by the late 1980s; see [[Wayfinding]] and [[Transit Map]]. Because it came bundled with personal computers and laser printers, it also became a default for ordinary documents. Microsoft's Arial, designed in 1982 to match Helvetica's character widths without licensing it, appears in its place on countless screens, and most readers can't tell them apart.

Familiarity bred backlash. Critics called it bland, overused, and the typeface of bureaucracy and corporate power; some designers deliberately avoided it, while others defended it as clear and honest. A 2007 documentary devoted entirely to the typeface captured both sides. On screens, its tight spacing and similar letter shapes, such as a capital I and lowercase l, hurt legibility at small sizes, one reason Apple replaced it as the iPhone's interface font in 2015 with a typeface designed for screens; see [[Variable Fonts]].

Helvetica has been redrawn several times, notably as Neue Helvetica in 1983 and Helvetica Now in 2019. Its ubiquity shows how defaults spread: a typeface bundled with tools gets chosen without anyone choosing it; see [[Default effect]].
