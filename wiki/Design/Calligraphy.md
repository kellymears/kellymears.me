**Calligraphy** is writing done as an art, where the shape, rhythm and spacing of the letters matter as much as the words. Every major writing tradition has one, and in several cultures it has been regarded as the highest of the visual arts.

In China, calligraphy has been prized for more than two thousand years. Written with a flexible brush and ink, each character is built from strokes in a fixed order, and the pressure, speed and dryness of the brush show in the result, so a master's hand is recognizable and the work is valued as an expression of character. The tradition spread with Chinese characters to Japan, where it is called shodō, and to Korea and Vietnam. In the Islamic world, where religious art largely avoided depicting figures, calligraphy became the central decorative art. Scripts such as the angular Kufic and the flowing Naskh and Thuluth cover mosques, manuscripts, tiles and coins, governed by proportional systems based on the dot made by the pen.

Western calligraphy is shaped by the broad-edged pen. Held at a constant angle, it makes thick strokes in one direction and thin ones in another, the contrast still visible in most serif typefaces. Scripts evolved over centuries, from Roman capitals to rounded uncials to the compressed letters of the late Middle Ages; see [[Blackletter]]. Italian humanists revived an older, clearer hand that became the model for roman and italic type; see [[Movable Type]] and [[Typeface]]. Later, pointed flexible pens produced the looping copperplate scripts of commercial correspondence and penmanship lessons.

Printing and then the typewriter reduced handwriting to a practical skill. The craft was revived in the early twentieth century by Edward Johnston, who studied medieval manuscripts to rediscover how the letters had been made; see [[Arts and Crafts Movement]]. A later story connects it to computing: Apple's cofounder told a 2005 commencement audience that a college calligraphy class inspired the Macintosh's range of proportional fonts.

Calligraphy remains embedded in digital type. Fonts for Arabic and other connected scripts must change each letter's shape depending on its neighbors, as a pen would, which requires sophisticated font technology and support for right-to-left text; see [[OpenType Features]] and [[Internationalization]]. Type designers sketch with pens and brushes before drawing outlines, and some fonts vary their stroke contrast along adjustable axes; see [[Variable Fonts]] and [[Bézier Curve]]. Software that reads handwriting faces the same variability that makes calligraphy expressive; see [[Optical Character Recognition]].

A modern revival of brush and hand lettering, spread widely through social media, has made it a popular hobby. Like other fine motor skills, it improves through slow, deliberate repetition until the movements become automatic; see [[Motor Learning]]. Lettering also remains common in logos, signage and packaging; see [[Logo]].
