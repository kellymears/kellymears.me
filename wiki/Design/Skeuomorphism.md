**Skeuomorphism** is the practice of styling a digital interface element to resemble the physical object it replaces — a notes app textured like yellow legal paper, a calculator with beveled plastic-looking keys, a bookshelf UI with wood grain and page-turn animations. The word predates computing (it originally described ornamental features on manufactured objects that were structural in an earlier material), but in software it names a specific design era, roughly the 2000s through early 2010s, epitomized by early iOS and its skeuomorphic default apps.

The design logic was onboarding by analogy: a generation with no mental model for "tapping a rectangle deletes a file" already had a rock-solid mental model for "dragging paper to a trash can," so borrowing the physical object's appearance transferred the [[Affordance]] for free. That logic was sound when the target audience genuinely lacked digital priors. It became a liability once they didn't — by the mid-2010s, users had more practice with flat digital buttons than with the physical objects being imitated, and the ornamental detail (the wood grain, the leather stitching) was pure visual cost with no remaining affordance benefit.

The reaction was flat design (iOS 7, Material Design's first version), which stripped ornamental realism down to color, typography, and a much smaller set of abstract signifiers — shadow, elevation, tint — to carry the same cues more cheaply. That correction has itself been going through course corrections since (Material's later versions and neumorphism both reintroduced some depth and softness), which is the general shape of design pendulum swings: an idea overcorrects, gets criticized, and settles somewhere between its origin and its opposite.

Skeuomorphism is the clearest historical case study for [[Affordance]] because it makes the mechanism visible — it borrowed a physical object's affordance on purpose, then had to give it up once the borrowed familiarity stopped paying rent.
