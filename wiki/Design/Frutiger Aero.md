**Frutiger Aero** is a name for the visual style of consumer technology from about the mid-2000s to the early 2010s: glossy buttons, translucent glass panels, water droplets, bubbles, lens flares, blue skies, green fields, auroras, and tropical fish. The label was coined online in the late 2010s by people cataloguing recent consumer aesthetics, and it combines the name of the Swiss type designer Adrian Frutiger, whose clean sans-serif faces suited the look, with Windows Aero, Microsoft's interface of the period; see [[Typeface]].

The style grew out of interface design. Apple introduced its **Aqua** interface with Mac OS X, unveiled in 2000, with translucent, candy-like buttons that Steve Jobs said looked good enough to lick, pinstriped backgrounds, and soft shadows. Microsoft's Windows Vista, released in 2007, introduced Aero, with window borders of blurred, frosted glass that showed the desktop behind them; see [[Glassmorphism]]. These effects were possible because graphics hardware had become capable of compositing transparent windows in real time; see [[Compositor]].

Interfaces of the period leaned on realistic texture and depth. Buttons looked like beads of gel, icons were rendered like photographed objects, and reflections suggested a glossy surface; see [[Skeuomorphism]] and [[Iconography]]. The same imagery filled desktop wallpapers, phone advertising, music players, and corporate presentations. Advertising for phones, laptops, and game consoles used the same palette of glowing blues and greens, often with abstract swooshes of light.

The look carried an optimistic message. Technology was presented as clean, natural, and harmonious, with screens of grass and water rather than circuitry, at a time of rapid adoption of broadband, smartphones, and social networks. Environmental themes, with bubbles and leaves suggesting freshness, appeared alongside sleek electronics.

It ended abruptly. Microsoft's Windows Phone and Windows 8, around 2010 to 2012, introduced flat tiles of solid color, and Apple's iOS 7 in 2013 stripped out textures and gloss; see [[Flat Design]]. Flat design was easier to scale across screen densities, looked cleaner on high-resolution displays, and suited responsive layouts. Within a few years, glossy interfaces looked dated.

The style's revival is part of a wider pattern. Young people who grew up with those interfaces began collecting and celebrating them online around 2020, assigning names to the half-remembered looks of their childhoods; see [[Nostalgia]]. Nostalgia tends to arrive on a cycle of about twenty to thirty years, as people return to the culture of their childhood once they're old enough to buy things; compare the earlier revivals of [[Art Deco]] and [[Memphis Group]].

The visual effects themselves came back sooner. Translucent, blurred panels returned in mainstream interfaces in the late 2010s and 2020s, now restrained and flat-colored, showing that design fashions rarely vanish entirely; see [[Desktop Metaphor]].
