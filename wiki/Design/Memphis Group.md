The **Memphis Group** was a collective of designers and architects in Milan, active from 1981 to 1987, who made furniture, lighting, ceramics, and textiles in deliberately loud colors and clashing patterns. Its work rejected the restrained good taste of modernist design and became one of the defining looks of the 1980s.

The group formed around the Italian designer Ettore Sottsass, who had already spent decades designing for the office equipment maker Olivetti. At a gathering in December 1980, a Bob Dylan song, "Stuck Inside of Mobile with the Memphis Blues Again," played repeatedly, and the group took Memphis as its name, enjoying its double reference to American popular culture and ancient Egypt. Members included Michele De Lucchi, Nathalie Du Pasquier, George Sowden, Martine Bedin, and Matteo Thun, with contributions from designers in several countries. Its first collection debuted at the Milan furniture fair in 1981 and drew huge crowds.

Memphis objects were built to provoke. Sottsass's Carlton bookcase, a room divider of angled shelves in multicolored plastic laminate, looks more like a totem or a toy than storage. Designers used cheap laminates, previously confined to kitchens and diners, alongside expensive materials, and covered surfaces with squiggles, dots, and abstract patterns. Pieces were bright, asymmetrical, and often impractical.

The target was modernism's moral seriousness. The Bauhaus and later functionalist designers held that form should follow purpose and that ornament was a kind of dishonesty; see [[Bauhaus]] and [[Form Follows Function]]. A generation of manufacturers had turned that into tasteful, neutral products, epitomized by restrained industrial design; see [[Dieter Rams]]. Memphis treated design as communication and pleasure, an idea aligned with postmodern architecture's rejection of minimalism, summed up in the architect Robert Venturi's quip "less is a bore."

The pieces sold in small numbers, but the look spread widely through fashion, graphic design, music videos, and television sets of the 1980s. The group disbanded in 1987, and its work later became collectible; an auction of a large Memphis collection owned by David Bowie in 2016 renewed interest.

The name returned in an unexpected form. In the late 2010s, critics began calling the flat, cheerful illustration style used across technology company websites, with its bendy-limbed figures in bright, unrealistic skin tones, **Corporate Memphis**, after its colors and geometric shapes; see [[Flat Design]]. The style was cheap to produce and offended no one, the opposite of what the original group intended, and the name was partly a joke about that.

Memphis shows how design movements cycle. One generation's ugliness becomes the next's nostalgia; see [[Nostalgia]]. It also shows that a look can be adopted while its argument is dropped; see [[Art Deco]].
