**Blackletter** is a family of heavy, angular letterforms that dominated writing in western Europe from about the twelfth to the fifteenth century. The name describes the effect: written with a broad pen held at a steep angle, tightly packed, with thick vertical strokes and narrow gaps between them, a page of blackletter looks dark. Many people now call it Gothic script or Old English, though it had nothing to do with the Goths.

It grew out of the rounder writing of earlier centuries as scribes compressed letters to fit more text on expensive parchment and to write faster; see [[Calligraphy]]. Regional varieties developed. **Textura**, the most formal, looks like a woven fabric of vertical strokes. Rotunda, rounder and more open, was favored in Italy and Spain. Later German varieties, Schwabacher and then **Fraktur** with its broken curves, became the everyday script of German-speaking Europe.

Printing froze the forms. When movable type arrived in Mainz around 1450, its first great book, the 42-line Bible, imitated textura so closely that it was meant to rival handwritten manuscripts; see [[Movable Type]]. Italian printers soon turned to roman typefaces based on humanist handwriting, which spread through most of Europe. German-speaking lands, Scandinavia and the Baltics kept blackletter for centuries. Fraktur relied on many joined letters and a long form of the s; see [[Ligature]].

The choice between the two became political. In the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, Germans argued about whether Fraktur was a national treasure or a barrier to international readers, a long-running quarrel known as the **Antiqua-Fraktur dispute**; see [[Imagined Community]]. The Nazi regime at first promoted blackletter as authentically German, then in 1941 abruptly banned it, falsely claiming it was of Jewish origin, probably because roman type was more readable in occupied countries. After the war, roman type stayed standard.

Its readability is largely a matter of familiarity. Readers raised on Fraktur read it fluently; most readers today stumble, especially over capitals, which can be hard to tell apart; see [[Legibility]]. In the early twentieth century, the Arts and Crafts revival of medieval printing brought it back for decorative use; see [[Arts and Crafts Movement]].

Today blackletter is a signal more than a text face. It marks tradition and authority on newspaper mastheads, diplomas and certificates, and heritage on beer labels and bakery signs; see [[Logo]]. It also carries menace and rebellion, used across heavy metal, hip-hop and tattoo culture, and its associations with nationalist movements make it a fraught choice in some contexts. Mathematicians use Fraktur letters as symbols, which is why they appear in [[Unicode]]. Digital revivals rebuild the old faces for screens; see [[Typeface]] and [[Font Rendering]].
