The **Arts and Crafts movement** was a reaction against what industrialization had done to everyday objects. By the mid-nineteenth century, factories could stamp out furniture, wallpaper and ornaments by the thousand, often badly made and covered in imitation decoration. Reformers in Britain argued that this cheapened both the goods and the people who made them, who had become machine operators with no stake in the result.

The critic John Ruskin supplied the philosophy: good work required the worker's own judgment and pleasure, and medieval craftsmanship, imperfect but alive, was better than flawless mechanical repetition. William Morris put it into practice. The firm Morris and partners founded in 1861 produced wallpapers, textiles, stained glass and furniture with patterns of interlacing leaves, flowers and birds, made with natural dyes and traditional methods. One of Morris's maxims is still widely quoted: have nothing in your houses that you do not know to be useful, or believe to be beautiful. The movement took its name from an exhibition society founded in 1887.

Book design was a late and lasting passion. In 1891 Morris founded the **Kelmscott Press**, cutting typefaces modeled on fifteenth-century printing, including a heavy gothic face, and producing books with dense decorative borders and ornamented initials. Its edition of Chaucer, published in 1896, is often called one of the most beautiful books ever printed; see [[Movable Type]], [[Blackletter]] and [[Drop Cap]]. The private-press movement that followed raised standards in commercial printing too; see [[Typeface]].

The movement had a built-in contradiction. Handmade goods cost far more than factory ones, so the reformers who wanted to elevate ordinary work mostly sold to the wealthy. Morris, a socialist, acknowledged the problem. Later designers concluded that the answer was not to reject machines but to design well for them.

That conclusion carried its ideas forward. The style spread to the United States, where Gustav Stickley's sturdy oak furniture and the Roycroft community popularized it in the early 1900s. In continental Europe, it fed the flowing decoration of the 1890s; see [[Art Nouveau]]. German design reformers adopted its belief in honest materials and the unity of art and craft, and the founders of the Bauhaus took it up while embracing industrial production; see [[Bauhaus]] and [[Form Follows Function]]. The calligrapher Edward Johnston, who revived broad-pen lettering within the movement's circle, later designed the London Underground's lettering; see [[Calligraphy]] and [[Wayfinding]].

Its values persist in modern craft culture and in the conviction that things should be built to last and repaired rather than thrown away. People also tend to value objects more when they've made them, which helps explain the appeal of handwork; see [[IKEA Effect]] and [[Dieter Rams]].
