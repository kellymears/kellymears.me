**De Stijl**, Dutch for "the style," was a group of artists, architects and designers active in the Netherlands from 1917 until the early 1930s. They believed art should be built from the most basic elements: horizontal and vertical lines, rectangles, the three primary colors, and black, white and gray. Everything else, including curves, diagonals, texture and representation of the visible world, was to be stripped away.

The painter and writer Theo van Doesburg founded the group and its magazine, and the painter Piet Mondrian gave it its theory, which Mondrian called **Neoplasticism**. The Netherlands stayed neutral in the First World War, and the movement's founders saw their pure, universal language of form as an antidote to the nationalism and chaos around them. Mondrian's mature paintings, black grids enclosing a few rectangles of red, yellow and blue on a white field, are its best-known images. Their balance is asymmetric: large white areas weigh against small blocks of intense color, so the composition holds without a center; see [[Visual Hierarchy]] and [[White Space]].

The architect and furniture maker Gerrit Rietveld turned the ideas into objects. The Red and Blue Chair of 1918 is a lattice of square-section wooden bars and two flat planes, painted so the parts seem to float apart. The Schröder House in Utrecht, built in 1924, applies the same logic to a home, with sliding walls that reconfigure the upper floor and colored planes that extend past the corners; it is now a World Heritage Site.

The movement split over a diagonal. In the mid-1920s van Doesburg began admitting tilted lines, arguing they added dynamism, and Mondrian, who regarded the strict horizontal and vertical as essential, left the group. It effectively ended with van Doesburg's death in 1931.

Its influence spread through teaching and exchange. Van Doesburg lectured in Weimar in the early 1920s, and De Stijl's elementary forms fed into the design school there; see [[Bauhaus]]. Ideas traveled between the Dutch group and Russian designers working with similar geometric vocabularies; see [[Constructivism]]. The later modernist practice of laying out pages on an underlying grid of rectangles owes much to this way of thinking; see [[Grid System]] and [[Swiss Style]].

Mondrian's look became popular culture. Yves Saint Laurent's 1965 cocktail dresses turned the paintings into fashion, and the grid of colored rectangles appears on everything from packaging to album covers. Interface designers notice the resemblance in layouts made of tiles, and an area chart that divides a rectangle into nested blocks is often described as looking like Mondrian; see [[Bento Grid]] and [[Treemap]]. Recreating a Mondrian painting is a common exercise for teaching two-dimensional layout on the web; see [[CSS Grid]].
