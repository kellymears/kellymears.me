**Art Deco** is a style of architecture and design that flourished between the world wars, roughly the 1920s and 1930s. It combined bold geometry, rich materials, and an enthusiasm for speed, machines, and modern life. Its name comes from the 1925 International Exhibition of Modern Decorative and Industrial Arts in Paris, though the term itself became common only in the 1960s, when the style was revived.

The vocabulary is easy to recognize. Zigzags, chevrons, sunbursts, and fan shapes; stepped profiles like ziggurats; strong vertical lines; symmetrical compositions; and stylized figures, plants, and animals. Materials were often luxurious: lacquer, chrome, inlaid wood, polished stone, and glass. Influences ran from Cubist painting to newly excavated Egyptian tombs to Aztec architecture, mixed without much concern for authenticity.

Skyscrapers gave it its most famous forms. New York's Chrysler Building, finished in 1930, wears a stainless steel crown of stacked sunbursts, and the Empire State Building followed in 1931. Cinemas, ocean liners, hotels, and railway stations adopted the style as symbols of glamour. Miami Beach preserves a district of hundreds of smaller Art Deco buildings in pastel colors.

Graphic design took it up as well. Posters for railways and ocean liners, especially those of the French artist known as Cassandre, used flat geometric shapes and dramatic perspectives to make travel look modern. Display typefaces were tall, thin, and geometric, with exaggerated contrasts between strokes; see [[Typeface]]. Logos and packaging carried the style into everyday goods; see [[Logo]].

In the 1930s, the style softened into **Streamline Moderne**. Industrial designers in the United States, such as Raymond Loewy and Norman Bel Geddes, gave trains, cars, radios, and even pencil sharpeners rounded, teardrop forms and horizontal speed lines, borrowing shapes developed to reduce air resistance; see [[Aerodynamic Drag]]. On a stationary object, the streamlining was symbolic, a promise of speed and progress during the Depression, and it helped sell products.

Art Deco ran alongside a stricter modernism. The Bauhaus and its successors rejected applied ornament as dishonest, and the architect Adolf Loos had argued that ornament was a sign of cultural backwardness; see [[Bauhaus]] and [[Ornament and Crime]]. Art Deco embraced modern materials and machine imagery while keeping decoration, which made it popular with the public and suspect to critics. After World War II, the International Style displaced it, and later the clean grids of Swiss design; see [[Swiss Style]].

The style returns regularly. It was revived in the late 1960s, again in the 1980s alongside other playful reactions to modernism, and it remains a shorthand for interwar glamour in films and games; see [[Memphis Group]] and [[Nostalgia]]. Its ideas about pattern also anticipate later decorative interfaces; see [[Skeuomorphism]].
