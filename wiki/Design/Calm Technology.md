**Calm technology** is technology designed to inform people without demanding their attention. Mark Weiser and John Seely Brown, researchers at Xerox PARC, described it in a 1995 essay, arguing that as computers spread into every object, they could not all compete for the center of attention. The answer was technology that lives mostly at the **periphery**, where people take in information without focusing on it, and moves to the center only when something needs action.

Weiser had coined **ubiquitous computing** a few years earlier, imagining a world where computers disappeared into rooms, walls, and everyday objects instead of sitting on desks as boxes demanding to be looked at; see [[Desktop Metaphor]]. Calm technology was its design principle.

Their best-known example was **Live Wire**, an artwork by Natalie Jeremijenko: a plastic string hanging from a small motor in the ceiling, which twitched every time a packet passed on the office network. A busy network made it whir and dance, a quiet one left it still. People could sense network load from across the room without reading a number or opening a tool, much as one hears traffic outside a window without listening to it.

Everyday calm technologies predate the term:

- **A kettle's whistle.** Silent until it matters, then hard to ignore.
- **Status lights.** A green charging light can be read at a glance and otherwise ignored.
- **Office windows into a corridor.** They show who is around without anyone checking.

Designer Amber Case later condensed the idea into principles: technology should require the smallest possible amount of attention, inform and create calm, use the periphery, amplify the best of technology and of people, communicate without necessarily speaking, work even when it fails, and use the minimum technology needed. Sound, light, vibration, and position can all carry information without a screen; see [[Haptic Feedback]].

The idea draws on how attention works. People filter most of what reaches their senses and notice changes against a steady background; see [[Selective Attention]] and [[Preattentive Processing]]. Ambient displays use that, encoding information in color, movement, or brightness that registers without reading. But anything at the periphery can become noise if it changes too often, and constant change pulls focus; see [[Habituation]].

Most consumer technology went the other way. Notifications, badges, and infinite feeds compete for the center of attention, because attention is what many products are paid for; see [[Doomscrolling]] and [[Dark Pattern]]. Each interruption has a cost beyond its own duration; see [[Attention Residue]]. Calm technology offers a counter-principle for designers: the right default is quiet, and the burden of proof is on interrupting. Notification design, from a quiet [[Toast Notification]] to a blocking [[Modal Dialog]], is a direct application, as is respecting [[Reduced Motion]].
