The **desktop metaphor** is the idea that a computer's screen can be treated like the top of an office desk, with documents, folders, a trash can, and a clipboard. It came together with the **WIMP** interface (windows, icons, menus, and a pointer), and for forty years it has been the standard way personal computers present themselves.

The ideas built up in stages:

- **1968.** Douglas Engelbart's research group gave a ninety-minute demonstration, later called the "Mother of All Demos," showing a mouse, linked text, windows, real-time collaborative editing, and video conferencing, decades before most became products; see [[World Wide Web]].
- **1973.** At Xerox's Palo Alto Research Center (PARC), the Alto computer had a bitmapped screen, a mouse, overlapping windows, and the first editor that showed documents as they would print. Alan Kay's group built Smalltalk on it, with icons and pop-up menus; see [[Object-Oriented Programming]].
- **1981.** The Xerox Star was the first commercial system to use the full office metaphor, with icons for documents, folders, and printers. It was expensive and sold poorly.
- **1984.** Apple, whose engineers had toured PARC, released the Macintosh, which brought the desktop to a mass market with a menu bar, drag-and-drop, and a trash can. Microsoft Windows followed, becoming dominant from version 3.0 in 1990.

The metaphor made computers approachable for people who had never used a command line; see [[Shell]]. Familiar objects gave newcomers an initial [[Mental Model]]: put a document in a folder, throw it in the trash. Every action could be found by looking through menus rather than remembered; see [[Recognition Rather Than Recall]] and [[Direct Manipulation]].

Metaphors also mislead once the real system differs from the picture. Dragging a file to another folder moves it, but dragging it to another disk copies it. Dragging a disk to the trash on early Macs ejected it, which alarmed people who thought it would erase it. Files can appear in two folders at once, which paper documents can't do; see [[Hard Link]]. Designers came to treat the metaphor as a starting point, not a rule; see [[Skeuomorphism]].

Underneath, the screen is managed by software that draws and arranges windows; see [[Display Server]] and [[Compositor]]. Alternatives to overlapping windows have persisted, notably the [[Tiling Window Manager]], which arranges windows in non-overlapping panes, and keyboard launchers that skip the desktop entirely; see [[Command Palette]].

Phones replaced the metaphor with full-screen apps and touch, and web apps moved documents into the browser and the cloud. Most people now search for files rather than browse folders. But windows, icons, menus, and pointers remain the base of desktop computing, and terms like "desktop," "folder," and "save" persist long after the office objects behind them faded; see [[Iconography]].
