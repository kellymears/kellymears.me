A **transit map** in the modern sense is a diagram, not a map. It shows which lines stop at which stations and where to change, while largely ignoring distances, curves, and geography. The form comes from Harry Beck's 1933 diagram of the London Underground, which became one of the most imitated designs of the twentieth century.

Earlier Underground maps were drawn over the street plan. The outlying stations were spread far apart and the central ones crowded together, and the result was hard to read. Beck, an engineering draftsman who had worked for the Underground, drew a new version in his spare time in 1931. His rules:

1. **Lines run only horizontally, vertically, or at 45 degrees.**
2. **Stations are spaced roughly evenly**, regardless of the real distance between them.
3. **The center is enlarged** and the suburbs compressed, so the dense area where most changes happen gets the most room.
4. **Interchanges get a distinct symbol**, and each line its own color.

The publicity department doubted it, but a trial print run in 1933 was popular with passengers, and it has been revised ever since. It is often compared to an electrical circuit diagram, which shows connections rather than layout.

Beck's insight was that a rider underground doesn't need geography, only sequence and connections, a structure mathematicians call topology; see [[Mental Model]] and [[Information Architecture]]. Removing everything else makes what remains easier to read; see [[Data-Ink Ratio]] and [[Visual Hierarchy]]. The approach relies on the eye's grouping of aligned, same-colored elements; see [[Gestalt Principles]].

The distortion has costs. Passengers use the diagram to judge distance, and a 2011 study of London riders found that route choices followed the map even when it made a trip longer. Some stations that look far apart are a short walk; some neighboring dots are miles apart. Every map chooses what to keep; see [[Map and Territory]] and [[Map Projection]].

New York tested the limits. Massimo Vignelli's 1972 subway diagram applied the same rules, with beige water, gray parks, and the clean type of the modernist style, and was admired by designers; see [[Swiss Style]]. Riders found it confusing because it bent the familiar shape of the city, and it was replaced in 1979 by a more geographic map. The lesson usually drawn is that how much abstraction readers accept depends on how well they already know the territory.

The diagram's influence reaches far beyond trains: network diagrams, flowcharts, and process maps borrow its fixed angles and color-coded lines, and its use in stations is part of a wider practice of guiding people through spaces; see [[Wayfinding]] and [[Iconography]].
