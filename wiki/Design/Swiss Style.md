**Swiss Style**, formally the **International Typographic Style**, is a school of graphic design that developed in Switzerland in the 1950s and spread worldwide over the following two decades. It aimed for clarity and objectivity: information presented as plainly and legibly as possible, with the designer's personal expression kept out of the way.

Its roots lie in the Bauhaus and in Jan Tschichold's 1928 book *Die neue Typographie*, which argued for asymmetric layouts and sans-serif type in place of centered, ornamented traditions. In Switzerland, schools in Basel and Zurich developed these ideas into a disciplined method. Key figures included Josef Müller-Brockmann, Armin Hofmann, and Emil Ruder. The journal *Neue Grafik*, published from 1958 to 1965, carried the style internationally.

Its defining traits:

- **The grid.** Layouts are built on a mathematical [[Grid System]] of columns and rows that align every element. Müller-Brockmann's 1981 book *Grid Systems in Graphic Design* remains a standard text.
- **Sans-serif type.** Clean grotesque typefaces such as Akzidenz-Grotesk, and new ones designed in Switzerland in 1957: Univers, by Adrian Frutiger, and Neue Haas Grotesk, by Max Miedinger and Eduard Hoffmann, soon renamed Helvetica.
- **Flush-left, ragged-right text.** Text aligned left with an uneven right edge, avoiding the irregular word spacing of justified type.
- **Asymmetry and space.** Layouts balance blocks of type and images against generous [[White Space]] rather than centering everything.
- **Objective imagery.** Photography rather than illustration, and type itself used as the main visual element.
- **Clear hierarchy.** Size, weight, and position establish order, the tools of [[Visual Hierarchy]] and a strict [[Typographic Scale]].

The style suited a postwar world of international trade and multilingual communication, where neutral, legible design worked across languages. It became the visual language of corporate identity programs, airports, and transit systems. Massimo Vignelli and Bob Noorda's signage for the New York City Subway, developed from the late 1960s, is a well-known example.

Critics found it cold and formulaic, and later movements reacted against it. Postmodern designers in the 1980s, including Wolfgang Weingart, who taught in Basel, broke its grids deliberately.

Its influence on screen design is direct. The flat design that replaced [[Skeuomorphism]] in the early 2010s drew openly on Swiss principles: grids, sans-serif type, flat color, and content over ornament. Responsive web layouts, column grids, and spacing scales all descend from the same habits, and a modern [[Design System]] encodes much the same discipline as reusable rules. Its emphasis on legibility, structure, and consistent alignment also supports [[Accessibility]], and its grouping by alignment and proximity reflects the [[Gestalt Principles]]. Precise spacing between letters, through careful [[Kerning]], was part of the craft, and comfortable [[Line Length]] was a given.
