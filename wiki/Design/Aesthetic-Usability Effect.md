The **aesthetic-usability effect** is the tendency for people to judge attractive designs as easier to use than plain ones, whether or not they are. The effect was first measured in 1995 by Masaaki Kurosu and Kaori Kashimura at Hitachi. They showed participants 26 layouts for an ATM interface that worked identically, and the ratings of how usable each looked correlated more strongly with how beautiful it looked than with anything about its function.

Noam Tractinsky repeated the study in Israel in 1997, expecting cultural differences to weaken the effect. It came out stronger. In a later experiment, participants actually used working ATM simulations with attractive or unattractive layouts, and the attractive ones were still rated more usable *after* use, even when their usability had been degraded.

Why it happens:

- **Halo.** A positive impression in one dimension spreads to unrelated ones, as the [[Halo Effect]] describes. A beautiful interface seems competent, trustworthy and well made.
- **Mood.** Don Norman argued in *Emotional Design* (2004) that "attractive things work better" in a real sense: positive emotion makes people more relaxed, creative and tolerant of small obstacles, so they work around problems that would frustrate them in a tense state.
- **Signal of care.** Visual polish suggests the makers paid attention to everything else.

The effect helps products. Users forgive minor flaws in attractive designs, judge them more patiently and are more likely to stay. Care in [[Visual Hierarchy]], type, spacing and motion pays off in perceived quality, and the same reasoning explains why players praise the [[Game Feel]] of a polished game over a functionally identical one. Memories of an experience lean heavily on its emotional peaks, per the [[Peak-End Rule]].

It also misleads:

- **Testing.** In [[Usability Testing]], participants rate attractive prototypes more kindly and blame themselves for problems. A polished design can hide serious usability problems, which surface only in real use. Rough prototypes draw more honest criticism of structure and flow.
- **Feedback on the wrong things.** Polished mockups draw comments on colors and fonts instead of workflow.
- **Substituting for function.** Beauty buys patience, not a pass. Eventually, a product that looks good but works badly is judged on how it works.

The effect complicates austere design doctrine. Minimalism and a high [[Data-Ink Ratio]] aim for clarity, but studies of embellished charts found they were remembered better without being understood worse. [[Skeuomorphism]], once dismissed as decoration, made early touch interfaces feel approachable for the same reason.

For designers, the practical upshot is to invest in aesthetics, test early with low-fidelity designs, and measure task success rather than asking whether people *liked* it. Like the [[Placebo Effect]], perceived quality partly creates real quality, through mood and patience, but it does not replace the real thing.
