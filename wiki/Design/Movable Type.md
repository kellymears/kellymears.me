**Movable type** is a printing system in which each letter or character is a separate piece that can be assembled into lines and pages, inked and printed, then taken apart and reused for something else. Before it, printing meant carving a whole page into a single woodblock, worthwhile only for texts in great demand.

The first known movable type was made in China around 1040 by Bi Sheng, who used characters of baked clay, according to an account written a few decades later. Wooden and metal type followed in China and Korea. The oldest surviving book printed with movable metal type is a Korean Buddhist text from 1377. With thousands of distinct characters to cast and sort, though, movable type had less of an advantage over woodblocks in East Asia than in alphabetic languages.

In Mainz, around 1450, Johannes Gutenberg combined several inventions into a working system: a hand mold for casting large numbers of identical letters quickly from a metal alloy, an oil-based ink that would stick to metal, and a press adapted from those used for wine and paper. The 42-line Bible, finished around 1455, was set in type imitating handwritten gothic script; see [[Blackletter]]. Within only fifty years, printing shops operated in hundreds of European towns and millions of books had been printed.

Printing changed what texts were. Copies became identical, so scholars in different cities could refer to the same page, compare editions and correct errors, a precondition, some historians argue, for the [[Scientific revolution]]. Spelling and grammar standardized around printers' choices. Cheap pamphlets let religious reformers reach mass audiences in the sixteenth century. Printing in vernacular languages rather than Latin helped create national reading publics; see [[Imagined Community]]. Contemporaries also worried about too many books, an early version of later complaints; see [[Information overload]] and [[Moral Panic]].

Typography's vocabulary comes from the composing room. Capital and small letters were kept in upper and lower cases. Strips of lead between lines gave **leading** its name; see [[Leading]]. Printers adjusted spaces between letters and words by hand to fit lines to a width; see [[Kerning]] and [[Text Justification]]. Italian printers introduced roman and italic type and small portable books, and typeface design became a craft in its own right; see [[Typeface]].

Hand-set type lasted until the late nineteenth century, when machines that cast whole lines of metal type sped up newspaper printing. Photographic and then digital typesetting replaced metal in the twentieth century; see [[Font Rendering]]. **Letterpress** survives as a craft, prized for the slight impression type makes in thick paper; see [[Arts and Crafts Movement]].
