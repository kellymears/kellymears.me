**Art Nouveau** was a style of decoration and design that spread across Europe and beyond around the turn of the twentieth century. Its signature is the long, sinuous line that swells and curls like a plant stem or a strand of hair, often called the **whiplash curve**, together with motifs taken from flowers, vines, dragonflies, peacocks and the female figure.

The name came from a Paris shop, the Maison de l'Art Nouveau, which opened in 1895 to sell modern decorative work. Other countries had their own names: Jugendstil in Germany, after a magazine called Youth; Secession in Austria, after a group of Viennese artists who broke from the official academy in 1897; Modernisme in Catalonia. The style grew partly out of the British reformers who wanted well-made, honestly designed objects, and partly from the Japanese woodblock prints flooding into Europe, with their flat color, bold outlines and asymmetric compositions; see [[Arts and Crafts Movement]].

Its ambition was to erase the line between fine art and the applied arts. Architects designed buildings down to the door handles, light fittings and wallpaper. Victor Horta's houses in Brussels from 1893 let iron structure curl into the interiors. Hector Guimard's cast-iron entrances to the Paris Métro, with stalks ending in lamps like seed pods, turned public infrastructure into ornament and became a recognizable sign of the system itself; see [[Wayfinding]]. Antoni Gaudí's buildings in Barcelona and Louis Comfort Tiffany's glass lamps in New York carried the style in very different directions.

Graphic design gave Art Nouveau much of its reach. Color lithography let posters be printed in large runs, and Alphonse Mucha's posters for the actor Sarah Bernhardt, with their framed figures, halos of ornament and hand-drawn lettering, set a template still imitated; see [[Iconography]] and [[Logo]]. Designers drew custom letterforms to match, and some of those shapes became typefaces; see [[Typeface]].

The style faded quickly. It was expensive, it dated fast, and by around 1910 critics attacked ornament itself as wasteful and backward. The Viennese architect Adolf Loos's essay comparing decoration to crime became a manifesto for the plain surfaces of modernism; see [[Ornament and Crime]] and [[Bauhaus]]. After the First World War, its curves gave way to the geometric glamour of the 1920s; see [[Art Deco]].

Art Nouveau returned in waves. Psychedelic rock posters of the 1960s borrowed its swirling lettering and Mucha's compositions, and its interest in organic form anticipates later design inspired by nature. Its curves also show up in digital type and illustration, where flowing outlines are built from smooth mathematical curves; see [[Bézier Curve]]. Its delight in ornament also found an unlikely echo in the 1980s, when postmodern designers again rejected modernist restraint in favor of color and pattern; see [[Memphis Group]].
