An **affinity diagram** organizes a large set of observations, ideas, or quotes into groups by similarity. Each item goes on its own note, and the notes are moved around until related ones sit together. Groups are named only after they form, which is the method's defining rule.

The method was developed by the Japanese anthropologist Jiro Kawakita in the 1960s to make sense of field notes, and is also called the KJ method after the initials of the name as written in Japanese order. It later became one of a set of management and planning tools promoted in Japanese quality circles in the 1970s, alongside tools for cause analysis and planning; see [[Root Cause Analysis]].

A typical session runs in stages:

1. **Capture.** Write one observation per note: a quote from an interview, a behavior seen in a usability session, a complaint from support tickets; see [[Usability Testing]].
2. **Group.** Move notes near others that seem related. Many facilitators ask people to do this silently, so that no single voice steers the grouping.
3. **Name.** Write a label for each cluster that says what its notes have in common, ideally as a statement, not a single word.
4. **Arrange.** Group the clusters into higher themes, and note relationships or tensions between them.

The bottom-up order matters. Starting with categories, such as navigation, pricing, and onboarding, sorts the data into what the team already expected. Letting groups emerge surfaces patterns nobody anticipated, which is the point of doing research at all; see [[Confirmation Bias]] and [[Abductive Reasoning]]. Silent sorting is a defense against social pressure in a room, where the most senior person's view tends to become the group's; see [[Groupthink]].

In design research, affinity diagrams are a standard way to move from raw interviews to findings, personas, and statements of need; see [[Persona]] and [[Jobs to Be Done]]. A close relative is card sorting, in which users group a site's content to reveal how they expect it to be organized; the affinity diagram is the team grouping its own data; see [[Card Sorting]].

The method is a manual form of clustering, with human judgment in place of a distance measure; see [[Clustering]]. That gives it flexibility, since a person can see that two differently worded complaints describe the same problem, and also variability, since different teams produce different groupings from the same notes. The value lies largely in the conversation the sorting produces.

Paper notes on a wall have given way in many teams to digital whiteboards, which make remote sessions possible and preserve the result, though they lose some of the physical ease of crowding around a wall. The underlying practice of letting structure emerge from individual notes also appears in personal note-taking systems; see [[Zettelkasten]].
