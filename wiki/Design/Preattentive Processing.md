**Preattentive processing** is the visual system's fast, parallel analysis of a scene before focused attention arrives. Certain features are detected across the whole field of view in roughly a quarter of a second, regardless of how many items there are. A single red dot among a hundred grey ones *pops out*. Finding a particular word in a paragraph requires reading, one item at a time.

Features that pop out include:

- **Color**, as hue and as lightness against the background.
- **Size** and **length**.
- **Orientation**, such as one tilted line among vertical ones.
- **Motion** and **flicker**, the strongest of all, since peripheral vision is tuned to movement.
- **Enclosure**, **added marks** and, more weakly, **shape**.

Anne Treisman's *feature integration theory* (1980) explained the limit. Individual features are registered in parallel, each in its own map, but combining them requires attention. A red circle among blue circles pops out, and so does a circle among squares. A red circle among red squares *and* blue circles does not: finding a conjunction of features means checking items one by one, and search time grows with the number of items. The fast process is in the spirit of the automatic system in [[Dual Process Theory]], and the slow one draws on [[Selective Attention]].

Design uses this deliberately:

- **Highlight with one channel.** Make the one thing that matters a different color or weight, and keep everything else muted. The [[Von Restorff Effect]] shows the distinct item is also remembered better.
- **Don't spend it everywhere.** Pop-out works only by contrast. A page where every heading is bold red has no emphasis left, so [[Visual Hierarchy]] depends on restraint. Muting non-data elements, as the [[Data-Ink Ratio]] suggests, keeps the contrast available.
- **Don't rely on hue alone.** About one in twelve men has some [[Color Vision Deficiency]], for whom a red-green distinction may vanish. Pair color with lightness, shape or position, and check [[Color Contrast]].
- **Use motion sparingly.** Motion captures attention involuntarily. Respect [[Reduced Motion]] preferences, and reserve movement for what deserves an interruption.

For charts, William Cleveland and Robert McGill (1984) ranked visual encodings by how accurately people judge quantities with them: position on a common scale first, then length, then angle and slope, then area, with color saturation near the bottom. That ranking is why bar charts usually beat pie charts, and why a [[Color Scale]] suits patterns better than precise values. [[Small Multiples]] combine accurate position encoding with comparison across panels.

Preattentive pop-out has limits: it tells you *that* something differs, not what it means. And what isn't attended can go entirely unseen, as [[Inattentional Blindness]] shows. Good design uses pop-out to direct attention and text to explain.
