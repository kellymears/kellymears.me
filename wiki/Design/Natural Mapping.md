**Natural mapping** is the design of controls whose layout mirrors the things they control, so that which control does what is obvious from position alone. Don Norman made the idea central in *The Design of Everyday Things* (1988), and his favorite example is the kitchen stove.

A typical stove has four burners in a square and four knobs in a row. Which knob runs the back left burner? Nothing in the layout says, so manufacturers add little diagrams next to each knob, and people still turn on the wrong burner. Put the knobs in a square matching the burners, or stagger them so each lines up with its burner, and the labels become unnecessary. The knowledge moves out of the user's head and into the world; see [[Recognition Rather Than Recall]].

Mapping comes in several kinds:

- **Spatial.** A light switch panel laid out like the room, or seat controls shaped like the seat, as in many cars, where pushing the seat-back icon tilts the seat back.
- **Directional.** Moving a control the way the thing should move: steering wheels turn the way the car turns, and a volume slider goes up for louder; see [[Direct Manipulation]].
- **Cultural.** Some mappings are learned rather than physical, such as clockwise for more or up for on, and they flip between places. Light switches in Britain are on when down, the opposite of the American convention, and that mismatch causes errors; see [[Mental Model]].
- **Grouping.** Controls for related things sit together and apart from unrelated ones; see [[Gestalt Principles]].

Screens raise the same questions. Scrolling direction is a famous dispute. On a scroll wheel, moving down traditionally moved the view down. Touchscreens made the content follow the finger, so moving down drags the page down and reveals what was above. When laptops adopted the touch direction, calling it "natural," many people found it backwards, because the right mapping depends on whether you imagine moving the window or the paper.

Good mapping reduces the need for other aids. A control that maps well needs no label to show what it does, though it may still need a [[Signifier]] to show that it is a control at all. Poor mapping produces errors under pressure, when nobody reads the labels: cockpit and control-room designers learned this from accidents, and arranged gauges and switches to match the systems they monitor, a discipline known as human factors.

Mapping also connects to speed. Controls placed near what they affect are easier to reach, and an arrangement that matches the task cuts the time spent searching; see [[Fitts's Law]]. Where a wrong action is dangerous, mapping alone is not enough, and the design should block the mistake outright; see [[Forcing Function]].
