**Color theory** is the body of practical guidance for mixing colors and choosing ones that work together. It is part craft tradition and part science, and the two halves don't always agree.

The **color wheel** starts with Isaac Newton, who in 1704 bent the spectrum into a circle so that violet met red. Painters later organized it around three primaries, red, yellow, and blue, mixed in pairs to make secondaries. Printers use cyan, magenta, and yellow, and screens use red, green, and blue, because pigments subtract light while pixels add it; see [[Halftone]]. The wheel is a convention, not a fact about light: hue is how the eye and brain sort wavelengths, which is why two different spectra can look identical; see [[Trichromacy]].

Harmony schemes are positions on the wheel:

- **Complementary.** Opposite hues, like blue and orange, give the strongest contrast and vibrate when placed side by side at equal strength.
- **Analogous.** Neighbors, like yellow, orange, and red, feel calm and unified.
- **Triadic.** Three evenly spaced hues give variety while staying balanced.
- **Split-complementary.** One hue plus the two neighbors of its opposite softens the clash of a straight complement.

The schemes are starting points, not guarantees. Michel Eugène Chevreul, a chemist at the Gobelins tapestry works in the 1830s, noticed that dyes looked different depending on their neighbors, a finding later dramatized in Josef Albers's *Interaction of Color* (1963), which taught color through exercises in which the same patch looks like two colors. Albers taught at the [[Bauhaus]], where Johannes Itten built a whole course around color contrasts. Their point was that context decides appearance, which the theory of the eye's red-green and blue-yellow channels later explained; see [[Opponent Process Theory]].

Interface designers inherit both halves of the tradition. The **60-30-10 rule**, from interior decorating, suggests a dominant neutral, a secondary color, and an accent used sparingly for the things that should draw the eye; see [[Visual Hierarchy]]. The traditional wheel is uneven in perceived lightness, since yellow looks far brighter than blue at full saturation, so modern palettes are built in perceptual spaces where lightness steps are even; see [[OKLCH]] and [[Color Scale]].

Three practical cautions:

- **Contrast.** A pleasing harmony can still fail legibility, since hue difference counts for little; lightness does the work; see [[Color Contrast]].
- **Color blindness.** Red-green pairs that read as opposites to most people merge for many others, so color should never carry meaning alone; see [[Color Vision Deficiency]].
- **Warmth.** Warm and cool are borrowed from light sources, where low color temperatures look amber; see [[Color Temperature]].
