**Jobs to Be Done** (JTBD) is a framework for understanding why people choose products. Its central claim is that people don't buy products; they *hire* them to make progress in a particular situation. The job, not the person's profile or the product's category, is what predicts the choice. An old marketing line by Theodore Levitt captures it: people don't want a quarter-inch drill, they want a quarter-inch hole.

The best-known illustration comes from Clayton Christensen's work on a fast-food chain trying to sell more milkshakes. Asking customers what they wanted led to recipe tweaks that changed nothing. Watching *when* milkshakes were bought revealed something stranger: many sold early in the morning to people commuting alone by car. The job was to make a long, boring drive more interesting and keep hunger away until lunch. A thick shake outlasting the drive beat a banana or a bagel; its real competition was other breakfasts, not other milkshakes.

That shift changes what a team asks:

- **Situation over persona.** "When I'm driving to work alone at 7 a.m." explains more than "men aged 25 to 40"; see [[Persona]].
- **Progress over features.** People want to get from a current state to a better one. Features matter only as means.
- **Real competition.** A note-taking app competes with paper, email to oneself, and doing nothing.

Jobs have several dimensions: **functional** (get the hole drilled), **emotional** (feel competent), and **social** (look prepared in front of others). Products that ignore the last two often lose to worse rivals.

Teams often write requirements as **job stories**: "When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome]." Compared with user stories built around a role, job stories keep the triggering context in view; see [[Acceptance Testing]].

Research studies real past decisions, not opinions. **Switch interviews** reconstruct the moment someone changed products, looking at four forces: the push of frustration with the old way, the pull of the new one, anxiety about switching, and the habit of the present. Asking "why" repeatedly about a purchase surfaces the underlying job; see [[Five Whys]].

Criticisms:

- **Vagueness.** Jobs can be framed at any level, from "drill a hole" to "make my home feel like mine," and the framework gives little guidance on which is useful.
- **Competing schools.** One tradition treats jobs as measurable outcome statements to be scored; another treats them as stories of struggle and progress. They share a name more than a method.

The lasting insight is about framing. Teams describe their work in terms of their product; JTBD forces the description into the customer's terms, guarding against building features nobody hired; see [[Feature Creep]] and [[Minimum Viable Product]]. It's closely related to designing for the user's [[Mental Model]].
