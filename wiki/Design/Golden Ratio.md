The **golden ratio**, written φ (phi), is the number about 1.618 that results from dividing a line so the whole is to the longer part as the longer part is to the shorter. It is irrational, its decimal expansion never repeats, and it has unusual properties: its square is itself plus one, and its reciprocal is itself minus one. Euclid described it around 300 BCE as dividing a line in "extreme and mean ratio." The name "golden section" dates only from the nineteenth century.

Its mathematics is genuine and turns up widely:

- **Fibonacci numbers.** In the sequence 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, where each term is the sum of the previous two, the ratio of neighbors approaches φ. This is why Fibonacci ratios such as 8:5 serve as approximations.
- **Geometry.** A regular pentagon's diagonal is φ times its side, so pentagrams and twelve-sided solids are full of it.
- **Plants.** Many plants place each new leaf or seed about 137.5 degrees round from the last, the "golden angle," which packs sunflower seeds in interlocking spirals without gaps. Counting the spirals usually gives neighboring Fibonacci numbers.
- **Approximation.** φ is the number hardest to approximate with fractions, the reason the golden angle avoids leaves stacking directly above one another.

The aesthetic claims are another matter. The ratio is often said to underlie the Parthenon, the Great Pyramid, Leonardo's paintings, and the human face, and to be the rectangle people find most pleasing. Careful measurement mostly fails to support this. Ancient sources never mention it as a design rule, and the matches depend on choosing which edges to measure, the [[Texas Sharpshooter Fallacy]] applied to buildings. Psychology experiments since Gustav Fechner's in the 1870s have found at most a weak preference for rectangles near 1.6, with wide individual variation. The famous claims mostly date from the nineteenth century, when the idea became popular; see [[Confirmation Bias]] and [[Pareidolia]].

Some designers have used it on purpose. Le Corbusier built his Modulor system of architectural proportions on φ and human height, and some typographers use it as the step of a [[Typographic Scale]] or to set the proportions of a page or a [[Grid System]]. In those cases it works as any consistent ratio does: a single proportion repeated through a design creates visual coherence; see [[Gestalt Principles]].

That is the practical lesson. Ratios like 3:2, 4:3, or the √2 of A4 paper serve as well, and a page of type is governed far more by [[Line Length]] and [[White Space]] than by any special number. Claims that a logo was "built on the golden ratio" usually describe circles overlaid after the fact.
