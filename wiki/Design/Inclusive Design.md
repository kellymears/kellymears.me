**Inclusive design** is the practice of designing with the full range of human difference in mind from the start, including disability, age, language, culture, and circumstance. It treats people at the edges of the usual design target not as special cases to handle later but as sources of insight that improve the result for everyone.

It overlaps with two related ideas:

- **[[Accessibility]]** is the outcome: whether a disabled person can use a product. It is often measured against standards such as WCAG.
- **Universal design**, a term coined by architect Ronald Mace in the 1980s, aims for environments usable by everyone without adaptation. Its seven principles include flexibility in use, simple and intuitive use, and tolerance for error.

Inclusive design is more a process than a checklist. It asks who is excluded by a design, why, and how including them would change it. Microsoft's influential inclusive design toolkit frames disability as a mismatch between a person and their environment, and describes it as a spectrum: **permanent** (one arm), **temporary** (an arm in a cast), and **situational** (holding a baby). A design that works with one hand serves all three groups, which is far more people than the permanent group alone.

That is the **curb cut effect**. Ramps cut into curbs were won by disability activists, notably in Berkeley in the 1970s, for wheelchair users. They turned out to help people with strollers, luggage, bicycles, and delivery carts. Many common features followed the same path. Captions, made for deaf viewers, are now used by many people watching video in noisy or quiet places. Autocomplete and text prediction began partly as assistive technology.

In digital products, inclusive design shows up as:

- **Perception.** Sufficient [[Color Contrast]], not relying on color alone for people with [[Color Vision Deficiency]], and supporting dark themes and zoom.
- **Motion and input.** Honoring [[Reduced Motion]], generous [[Touch Target Size]], and full [[Keyboard Navigation]].
- **Cognition.** [[Plain Language]], predictable layouts, and less reliance on memory, which helps people with differences in [[Executive Function]] and anyone tired or stressed.
- **Sensory load.** Avoiding autoplay, flashing, and noise that can cause [[Sensory Overload]], which matters for many autistic people.
- **Structure.** [[Semantic HTML]] so assistive technology can describe and navigate the page.

The movement around [[Neurodiversity]] has pushed inclusive design beyond physical disability, toward interfaces that accommodate different ways of focusing, processing, and communicating.

The common objection is cost, but inclusive decisions are cheap when made early and expensive to retrofit. A design system that bakes in focus states, contrast, and semantic structure makes every later screen inclusive by default. The opposite approach, building for an imagined average user, fails often, because almost nobody is average in every dimension at once.
