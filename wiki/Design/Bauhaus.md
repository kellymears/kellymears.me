The **Bauhaus** was a German school of art, design, and architecture that ran from 1919 to 1933, first in Weimar, then Dessau, and finally Berlin. In fourteen years it shaped how the twentieth century thought about design: that furniture, lamps, buildings, posters, and type should be made for industrial production, built from simple geometric forms, and stripped of ornament that served no purpose.

Its founder, the architect Walter Gropius, set out to unite the arts and crafts that the nineteenth century had split apart. Students began with a **preliminary course** on materials, color, and form, then trained in workshops for metal, weaving, furniture, printing, ceramics, and wall painting, each led jointly by an artist and a craftsman. The faculty included the painters Wassily Kandinsky, Paul Klee, and Josef Albers, the latter of whom went on to write *Interaction of Color*; see [[Color Contrast]].

Early work was handmade and expressive. From 1923 the motto became "art and technology, a new unity," and the workshops designed prototypes for factories. Marcel Breuer's tubular steel chairs, inspired by bicycle handlebars, and Marianne Brandt's metal lamps and teapots are the best-known products, and versions of several are still manufactured.

Characteristic ideas:

- **Function first.** Form should follow from use and from how a thing is made, a principle usually summed up as "form follows function," a phrase coined earlier by the American architect Louis Sullivan.
- **Elementary shapes.** Circle, square, and triangle, with primary colors, as a universal visual language.
- **Truth to materials.** Steel, glass, and concrete shown as they are rather than disguised as stone or wood; contrast [[Skeuomorphism]].
- **Standardization.** Designing for mass production and modular parts, so good design could be affordable.

In typography, Herbert Bayer designed a geometric alphabet with no capital letters, and László Moholy-Nagy promoted asymmetric layouts built on photographs, strong rules, and sans-serif type. Jan Tschichold, inspired by a 1923 Bauhaus exhibition, codified this as the "New Typography," which led directly to the [[Swiss Style]] and its reliance on the [[Grid System]]; see [[Typeface]].

The Nazis, who considered the school politically suspect, forced it to close in 1933. Its teachers scattered, mostly to the United States: Gropius and Breuer taught at Harvard, Mies van der Rohe led architecture in Chicago, and Moholy-Nagy founded the New Bauhaus there. Their influence shaped the International Style of glass-and-steel office towers and much of modern product design.

The legacy is mixed. Critics argue that functionalism became a style of its own, that "universal" forms ignored local culture, and that stripped-down housing blocks failed the people living in them. But its core idea, that designers should understand how things are made and used, underlies modern interface design; see [[Visual Hierarchy]], [[White Space]], and [[Flat Design]].
