The **Kano model** is a way of classifying product features by how they affect customer satisfaction. It was introduced in 1984 by Noriaki Kano, a Japanese professor of quality management, and colleagues, who argued that satisfaction isn't a single scale on which every improvement counts equally.

The model's central observation is that features behave differently. Some prevent dissatisfaction without creating satisfaction; others do the reverse. Kano proposed several categories:

**Must-be** qualities are basic expectations. A hotel room must have clean sheets; a phone must make calls. Their absence causes anger, but their presence earns no praise, because customers take them for granted and rarely mention them when asked what they want.

**One-dimensional** or performance qualities scale with satisfaction: more is better and less is worse. Battery life, speed, and price usually behave this way, and they're what customers talk about and competitors compare.

**Attractive** qualities, often called delighters, aren't expected, so their absence goes unnoticed, but their presence produces disproportionate pleasure. A free upgrade or an unexpectedly thoughtful detail falls here.

Features can also be **indifferent**, making no difference either way, or **reverse**, where some customers actively dislike them.

To classify features, Kano used paired questions: how would you feel if the product had this feature, and how would you feel if it didn't? Combining the answers places each feature in a category. Teams use the results to prioritize: secure the must-be qualities first, compete on performance, and add a few delighters to stand out. The model warns against piling on features that turn out to be indifferent; see [[Feature Creep]].

The categories aren't fixed. Delighters become performance features and then expectations as competitors copy them and customers grow used to them, much as people adapt to improvements in their circumstances; see [[Hedonic Treadmill]]. Touchscreens, camera quality, and free shipping followed that path. Categories also differ between customer groups, so results depend on whom a team asks; see [[Persona]].

The model complements other ways of understanding needs. Customers often can't articulate must-be qualities or imagine delighters, which is why research based on observing what people are trying to accomplish can uncover what surveys miss; see [[Jobs to Be Done]] and [[Usability Testing]]. Experiences also aren't judged by summing features: a single frustrating or delightful moment can dominate memory; see [[Peak-End Rule]]. And losses loom larger than equivalent gains, which may help explain why missing basics hurt more than extras please; see [[Loss Aversion]].

Critics note that the questionnaire is easy to misread and that categories assigned from surveys can be unstable. The lasting contribution is the idea itself: not all quality is linear, and the features customers ask for aren't always the ones that matter most; see [[Design Thinking]].
