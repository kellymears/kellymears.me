**Brutalist web design** is a deliberately raw style of website: default or monospace fonts, plain blocks of color, visible borders, underlined blue links, and little or no decoration. It looks unfinished on purpose, and often confrontational.

The name comes from architecture. Brutalism was a style of the 1950s to 1970s that left buildings as raw, exposed concrete, from the French béton brut. The British critic Reyner Banham popularized the term in a 1955 essay. Brutalist buildings were honest about their materials and structure, and were widely disliked for looking harsh and cold, though many now have devoted admirers and campaigns to save them from demolition. The web style borrows the idea of exposing the material, in this case HTML and the browser's defaults.

The label spread in 2014, when the designer Pascal Deville started a website collecting sites in the style under the brutalist name, and it drew wide attention in 2016. It was a reaction against the look of the web at the time:

- **Sameness.** Template themes and shared frameworks made sites look alike, with the same large hero image and three columns of features; see [[Design System]].
- **Polish.** Smooth, minimal, flat interfaces felt corporate and anonymous; see [[Flat Design]].
- **Weight.** Pages had grown heavy with scripts and images.

Brutalist sites varied widely. Some were raw in the sense of the early web, recalling hand-built personal pages before templates; see [[GeoCities]] and [[Nostalgia]]. Others were aggressively art-directed, using clashing colors, overlapping text, and layouts that broke conventions on purpose, which could make them harder to use than the polished sites they mocked.

A plainer version argued for brutalism as usability. Its proponents said a website should present its content clearly, underline links, make buttons look like buttons, let the back button work, and load quickly. That's close to designing with the browser's grain: well-structured HTML, readable defaults, and few dependencies; see [[Semantic HTML]], [[Performance Budget]], and [[Form Follows Function]].

Around 2020, **neo-brutalism** became a mainstream look: thick black outlines, hard offset shadows with no blur, flat saturated colors, and chunky type. Companies including the creator platform Gumroad adopted it for product interfaces. It keeps the visual signals of brutalism while being as carefully designed and consistent as the styles it reacted against, which shows how quickly a rebellion becomes a template.

Harsh colors and clashing text can fail people with low vision; see [[Color Contrast]] and [[Accessibility]]. The style's popularity among designers also tests the idea that people judge attractive interfaces as easier to use; see [[Aesthetic-Usability Effect]].

The impulse continues among personal websites built by hand; see [[IndieWeb]].
