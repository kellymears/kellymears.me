**White space**, also called negative space, is the empty area in a design: margins, gaps between elements, space between lines and letters. It doesn't have to be white; it is simply space with nothing in it. Beginners tend to treat it as wasted room to fill, while experienced designers treat it as one of their main tools.

White space does several jobs at once:

- **Grouping.** Things close together look related, and things far apart look separate, the law of proximity among the [[Gestalt Principles]]. Spacing alone can divide a form into sections without any lines or boxes.
- **Emphasis.** An element with generous space around it looks important, which makes space a tool of [[Visual Hierarchy]].
- **Readability.** Space between lines, paragraphs, and margins helps eyes track text. Cramped text is tiring to read, which is why [[Line Length]] and line spacing matter as much as typeface choice.
- **Calm.** A crowded layout makes everything compete. Space lowers [[Cognitive Load]] by giving the eye places to rest.

Designers often distinguish *macro* white space, the large gaps between sections and around the content, from *micro* white space, the small gaps between lines, letters, list items, and a button's label and its edge. Letter spacing adjustments such as [[Kerning]] are white space at the smallest scale.

In practice, consistency matters more than any particular amount. Systems define a *spacing scale*, a fixed set of values such as 4, 8, 12, 16, 24, 32, and 48 pixels, often built on a 4- or 8-pixel base unit, and use only those. Stored as a [[Design Token]], the scale keeps spacing consistent across a [[Design System]] and makes layouts feel ordered even when no one can say why. A common rule of thumb is that space inside a group should be smaller than space between groups, or the grouping becomes ambiguous.

White space interacts with screen size. A layout with generous margins on a wide monitor must shrink them on a phone, where space is scarce, so spacing usually changes at each [[Responsive Breakpoint]].

The modernist [[Swiss Style]] made white space central, using asymmetric layouts in which empty areas balance blocks of type and images. Its influence runs through minimalist web and app design today, and through the reaction against dense, ornamented interfaces that followed the move away from [[Skeuomorphism]]. A [[Grid System]] gives white space a structure, so the gaps line up instead of looking accidental.

The main failure is too little space, which makes a page feel cluttered and makes groups hard to see. Too much can also fail, scattering related items so far apart that they no longer look connected.
