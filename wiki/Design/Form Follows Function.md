**Form follows function** is the principle that the shape of a building or object should come from its purpose rather than from tradition or decoration. The phrase comes from the American architect Louis Sullivan, who wrote in an 1896 essay on tall office buildings that "form ever follows function," arguing that a skyscraper's design should express its stacked, repeated floors of offices.

Sullivan himself used plenty of ornament. The slogan grew stricter as others took it up. In 1908 the Viennese architect Adolf Loos wrote "Ornament and Crime," an essay arguing that decoration wasted labor and materials and belonged to the past. The Bauhaus and the International Style of the 1920s and 1930s made plain, functional form an ideal, and Ludwig Mies van der Rohe's motto "less is more" became its short form; see [[Bauhaus]] and [[Swiss Style]]. After the Second World War, glass office towers and plain concrete housing spread worldwide under its banner.

Then came the backlash. The architect Robert Venturi answered Mies in 1966 with "less is a bore," arguing that complexity, symbolism, and historical reference are part of what buildings do for people. Postmodern design in the 1970s and 1980s brought back color, pattern, and playful ornament. The critique struck at the slogan's weak point: "function" is not one thing. A building or product also has to be understood, enjoyed, trusted, and felt to belong; see [[Aesthetic-Usability Effect]].

The principle maps closely onto interface design:

- **Clear purpose.** Controls should look like what they do, so the form explains the function; see [[Affordance]] and [[Signifier]].
- **Decoration.** Ornament that carries no information gets cut, which is Edward Tufte's argument against chart decoration; see [[Data-Ink Ratio]].
- **Structure first.** Web pages describe meaning in markup and style it separately, so the same content can take different forms; see [[Semantic HTML]].

The swing between skeuomorphic and flat interfaces replayed the old argument. Early smartphone interfaces imitated leather, paper, and wood to make new functions familiar; the 2013 move to flat design stripped that away, and then partly restored shadows and depth when users couldn't tell what was tappable; see [[Skeuomorphism]] and [[Flat Design]].

The slogan also has a failure mode in imitation. Copying the look of functional design without its reasoning, such as a minimal style applied to a product that still does too much, keeps the form and loses the function; see [[Cargo Cult]].

Dieter Rams later restated the principle for industrial design as "as little design as possible," with the emphasis on honesty and usefulness rather than on bareness for its own sake; see [[Dieter Rams]]. The principle survives best in that form: a test of whether each element earns its place, not a ban on beauty.
