**Wayfinding** is the process of orienting yourself and navigating from place to place, and the design discipline that supports it. It covers airport signs, hospital corridors, subway maps, and street layouts, and its principles carry over directly to websites and software, where people face the same three questions: where am I, where can I go, and how do I get back?

The urban planner Kevin Lynch introduced the term in *The Image of the City* (1960). Interviewing residents of Boston, Jersey City, and Los Angeles, he found people navigate using a mental image built from five kinds of element:

- **Paths.** Streets, walkways, and transit lines people move along.
- **Edges.** Boundaries such as rivers, walls, and highways.
- **Districts.** Areas with a recognizable character.
- **Nodes.** Junctions and focal points where decisions happen.
- **Landmarks.** Distinctive objects visible from a distance.

A city is *legible*, in Lynch's sense, when these elements are clear enough for people to form a reliable mental map; see [[Mental Model]]. Memory works the same way: the ancient technique of placing ideas along an imagined route exploits how well people remember places; see [[Method of Loci]].

Wayfinding design draws on a few principles:

- **Information at decision points.** Signs belong where people must choose, not everywhere. Too many signs compete for attention and get ignored; see [[Banner Blindness]].
- **Progressive disclosure.** Show the next step, not the whole route; see [[Progressive Disclosure]].
- **Consistency.** The same color, typeface, and symbol for the same thing everywhere. Transit systems pair a line's color with every sign that mentions it.
- **Recognition over recall.** People should follow visible cues, not remember instructions; see [[Recognition Rather Than Recall]].
- **Oriented maps.** A you-are-here map should be rotated so that up on the map is forward for the viewer. North-up maps on walls force people to rotate the map in their heads, and many guess wrong.

Signage depends on legible type and symbols. Airport and transit signs borrow heavily from the clean sans-serif tradition of [[Swiss Style]], and in 1974 the U.S. Department of Transportation commissioned a standard set of pictograms for travelers, still widely used; see [[Iconography]]. Sufficient contrast and large text matter for readability at distance; see [[Color Contrast]].

Digital wayfinding uses the same ideas. Navigation bars are paths; sections are districts; the home page is a node; a distinctive page header is a landmark. Breadcrumbs answer "where am I," and a stable, readable [[URL]] tells people where they are and lets them return. People follow cues that promise their goal is near; see [[Information Scent]]. The overall structure that makes this possible is [[Information Architecture]]. Search and a command palette serve people who know their destination and want to skip the walk.
