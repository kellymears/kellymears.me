**Flat design** is a visual style for interfaces that uses plain shapes, solid colors, simple icons, and clean type, without the gradients, shadows, and textures that imitate physical materials. It became dominant in the early 2010s as a reaction against [[Skeuomorphism]], whose leather calendars and wooden bookshelves had come to look cluttered.

Microsoft led the change. Its Zune player and then Windows Phone 7 in 2010 used the Metro style: large type, flat colored tiles, and almost no ornament, drawing on transit signage and the [[Swiss Style]]. Apple followed with iOS 7 in 2013, replacing textures with thin type, translucent layers, and outline icons. The shift was quick partly because high-resolution screens made fine type look sharp, and flat graphics scaled cleanly across the many screen sizes of the time, from phones to large desktop monitors.

The appeal was real:

- **Clarity.** Fewer decorative effects leave content and type at the front; see [[Data-Ink Ratio]] for the same argument about charts.
- **Consistency.** Simple shapes and color are easy to systematize as tokens and components; see [[Design Token]] and [[Design System]].
- **Performance.** Vector shapes and solid fills are smaller and render faster than bitmap textures, especially on phones.

The cost was **lost signifiers**. Raised buttons and shadows told people what could be pressed. When buttons became plain colored text, people often couldn't tell a label from a link. Usability studies found people hesitated longer and missed clickable elements on very flat pages, a failure of [[Affordance]] and [[Signifier]]. Thin, light-gray type on white also failed contrast standards; see [[Color Contrast]].

The industry corrected. Google's **Material Design** (2014) kept flat color and type but brought back depth on purpose: elements sit at defined heights above the page, cast shadows that encode that height, and move in physically motivated ways; see [[Motion Design]] and [[Stacking Context]]. Many interfaces settled into a "flat 2.0" with subtle shadows and clearer buttons.

Later fashions pushed further in each direction. **Neumorphism** (around 2020) rendered controls as soft bumps extruded from the background, which looked striking but had such low contrast that controls almost disappeared, and it faded fast. **Glassmorphism** used frosted translucent panels, which looked elegant but put text over unpredictable backgrounds. Some recent systems have returned to richer textures and depth.

The underlying lesson is that style and usability are separable concerns. Any visual style can work if it keeps clear cues for interaction, consistent hierarchy, and sufficient contrast; see [[Visual Hierarchy]]. Flat design succeeded where it kept those cues and failed where it treated them as decoration; see [[Aesthetic-Usability Effect]].
