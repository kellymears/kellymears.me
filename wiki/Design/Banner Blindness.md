**Banner blindness** is the tendency of people to ignore page elements that look like advertisements, even when those elements contain exactly what they are looking for. Jan Panero Benway and David Lane named it in 1998 after a study at Rice University in which participants searching a website missed large, brightly colored banners holding the answer, while finding the same information as a plain text link. The more an element shouts, the more reliably it was skipped.

The cause is learning. Early web users quickly discovered that boxes at the top and right of the page, animated graphics, and bright rectangles were almost always ads. Ignoring them saved time, so people stopped looking, a form of [[Habituation]]. Attention is goal-driven: someone hunting for a phone number filters out whatever does not seem relevant, the mechanism behind [[Selective Attention]] and [[Inattentional Blindness]]. Nielsen Norman Group eye-tracking studies over two decades show the effect persists.

Things get ignored when they resemble ads by:

- **Location.** The top banner slot and the right-hand column.
- **Shape.** Standard ad sizes, such as wide leaderboards and tall skyscrapers.
- **Style.** Saturated colors, borders, and stock imagery that set an element apart from the surrounding content.
- **Motion.** Animation, carousels, and autoplaying video.
- **Proximity.** Sitting next to actual ads, which tars neighbors by association, a [[Gestalt Principles]] grouping working against the designer.

The broader research is about how people **scan**. Text-heavy pages are read in an *F-pattern*: a horizontal sweep across the top, a shorter sweep lower down, then a vertical scan down the left edge. Nielsen published it in 2006. Other patterns appear with different layouts, such as the *layer-cake* pattern, where people read headings and skip the text beneath, and *spotted* scanning for specific words or numbers. All share one lesson: people do not read web pages, they forage.

Design responses follow:

- **Make important content look like content.** Put it in the main column, in the same typography as the rest, not in a decorated box.
- **Front-load.** Start headings, links, and paragraphs with the words that carry meaning, since scanning catches the first few words of each line, which [[Line Length]] also affects.
- **Use [[Visual Hierarchy]] honestly.** Clear headings and structure support layer-cake scanning; decoration that mimics ads backfires.
- **Avoid crying wolf.** Promotional banners, cookie notices, and nag modals train people to dismiss anything banner-shaped, including the critical alert next week.

The effect is weaponized as well as avoided. Native advertising disguises ads as editorial content to slip past the filter, and some [[Dark Pattern]] designs exploit learned dismissal, placing a consequential choice where people habitually click through. [[Preattentive Processing]] explains what grabs the eye first; banner blindness explains what the mind has already learned to discard.
