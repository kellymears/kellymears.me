**Closed captions** are on-screen text versions of a program's speech and other important sounds, which viewers can turn on or off. "Closed" means hidden until requested; **open captions** are burned into the picture for everyone. Captions were built for people who are deaf or hard of hearing and are one of the most widely used accessibility features; see [[Accessibility]].

Captions differ from **subtitles** in intent. Subtitles usually translate dialogue for viewers who can hear but don't know the language. Captions assume the viewer can't hear the audio, so they also identify speakers and describe sounds that carry meaning, such as a door slamming offscreen, music swelling, or a phone buzzing. The writing craft they share, condensing speech to a readable pace and breaking lines at natural phrases, is its own discipline; see [[Subtitling]] and [[Internationalization]].

In the US, PBS broadcast open-captioned episodes of *The French Chef* in 1972, and closed captioning began in 1980. The caption data rode on line 21 of the analog TV picture, in the part of the signal the screen never showed, and required a separate decoder box; see [[Vertical blanking interval]]. A 1990 law required decoder circuits in televisions with screens of 13 inches or more from 1993, which put captions into nearly every home. Later laws extended requirements to most broadcast programming and to programs reposted online.

Producing captions takes several approaches:

1. **Offline captions** for recorded programs, written and timed in advance, can be accurate and well placed.
2. **Live captions** for news and sports were traditionally typed by stenographers on chorded shorthand keyboards at speaking speed, with a few seconds of delay.
3. **Respeaking** has a trained speaker repeat the dialogue clearly into speech recognition software.
4. **Automatic captions** generated by speech recognition are now common on video platforms; quality has improved sharply but still stumbles on names, accents, and overlapping speech; see [[Automatic Speech Recognition]].

Good captions follow design rules for readability: limited characters per line and lines per screen, enough time on screen to read them, placement that doesn't cover faces or on-screen text, and enough contrast against changing video; see [[Line Length]] and [[Color Contrast]]. Viewers should be able to adjust size, font, and background, since no single style suits everyone.

Captions show the curb cut effect clearly. Many hearing viewers use them in noisy places, in quiet homes, with unfamiliar accents, or with dialogue mixed low under music, and social video is often watched muted with open captions; see [[Curb cut effect]].

For still images, the parallel is alt text; for people who are blind, the parallel to captions is audio description, a narrated track describing on-screen action; see [[Alt Text]] and [[Screen Reader]].
