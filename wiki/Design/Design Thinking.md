**Design thinking** is a problem-solving process modeled on how designers work: start from the people affected, understand their problem before solving it, generate many ideas, and test rough versions quickly. It spread from design schools into business and education in the 2000s, popularized by the firm IDEO and Stanford's d.school.

The common versions describe the same rhythm. The British Design Council's **Double Diamond** (2005) draws it as two diamonds, each widening and then narrowing:

1. **Discover.** Go wide on the problem: interviews, observation, and research into how people actually behave; see [[Usability Testing]].
2. **Define.** Narrow to a clear statement of the problem worth solving.
3. **Develop.** Go wide again on possible solutions, generating many before judging any.
4. **Deliver.** Narrow to one, build it, test it, and refine.

Stanford's five steps, empathize, define, ideate, prototype, and test, cover the same ground. Both versions insist the process loops: testing reveals that the problem was misunderstood, and the team goes back.

Its most valuable habit is refusing to accept the first framing of a problem. A request for a feature is often a guess at a solution to some other need, and the research phase exists to find that need; see [[XY Problem]] and [[Jobs to Be Done]]. A second habit is making ideas cheap to test. Sketches, paper prototypes, and clickable mockups let a team learn from users before building anything expensive; see [[Wireframe]].

Common tools:

- **Personas** and **journey maps** to keep specific people and their experience in view; see [[Persona]].
- **Card sorting** to learn how people group content; see [[Card Sorting]].
- **"How might we" questions** to turn problems into prompts for ideas.
- **Design sprints**, a five-day format published by Google Ventures in 2016, which compresses the cycle into a week ending with tests on five users.

The criticisms are real. Critics, including some designers, argue that the method is often sold as a workshop recipe of sticky notes and exercises, producing the look of innovation without the research or the follow-through, which has been called "innovation theater"; see [[Cargo Cult]]. A few days of empathy exercises do not replace deep knowledge of a domain, and the method says little about what happens after the prototype, when the hard engineering, policy, and business constraints arrive. Others note that the process is not new: scientific method and engineering practice already alternate between exploring and testing.

Used well, it is less a procedure than a set of habits: talk to the people affected, separate the problem from the proposed solution, and test cheaply before committing. Expert reviews can supplement user testing but not replace it; see [[Heuristic Evaluation]].
