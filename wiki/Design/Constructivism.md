**Constructivism** was an art and design movement that emerged in Russia around the time of the 1917 revolution. Its members argued that the artist should become an engineer, constructing useful things for a new society rather than painting pictures for collectors. Posters, books, textiles, furniture, theater sets and buildings were to be designed with the same rigor as machines.

Vladimir Tatlin's design for a Monument to the Third International, made as a model in 1919 and 1920, became its emblem: a leaning spiral of steel, taller than the Eiffel Tower, enclosing rotating glass buildings. It was never built. Alexander Rodchenko, Varvara Stepanova, Lyubov Popova and others turned from abstract painting to applied work, and the term "constructivism" was adopted by a group of them in 1921.

Graphic design was where it had the widest reach. In a largely illiterate country recovering from civil war, posters, leaflets and decorated trains carried political messages, a practice known as **agitprop**, short for agitation and propaganda. El Lissitzky's 1919 poster "Beat the Whites with the Red Wedge" told a story of the civil war with nothing but a red triangle piercing a white circle. Designers favored strong diagonals, flat red and black, heavy [[Sans-serif]] lettering set at angles, and type used as an image in its own right; see [[Visual Hierarchy]] and [[Call to Action]].

**Photomontage**, cutting and combining photographs into new compositions, became a signature technique. Rodchenko's 1924 advertising poster for a state publishing house, with a woman calling out through cupped hands and the slogan bursting outward in a megaphone-shaped triangle, is one of the most imitated images in graphic design. Lissitzky's 1923 book of Mayakovsky's poems, designed with type set entirely from the printer's stock of letters and rules, showed that the page itself could be constructed; see [[Grid System]].

Lissitzky traveled through Germany and the Netherlands in the early 1920s, and the movement's ideas mixed with those of Dutch and German modernists; see [[De Stijl]] and [[Bauhaus]]. The rational, asymmetric layouts that resulted fed directly into later European typography; see [[Swiss Style]].

At home, Constructivism lost official favor by the early 1930s, when the Soviet state imposed socialist realism, a style of idealized figurative art, and avant-garde experiment became politically dangerous. Some of its practitioners were sidelined; others adapted.

The look keeps returning. Album covers, film titles and advertising borrow its angled type, red-and-black palette and photomontage, often with none of its politics. Its premise that form should follow purpose, with no decoration for its own sake, is shared with other modernist movements; see [[Form Follows Function]]. Its approach to communicating complex ideas through simple, bold graphics also prefigures modern information graphics; see [[Infographic]].
