A **logo** is a graphic mark that identifies a company, organization, or product. It may be a word set in a particular way, a symbol, or both. Its job is recognition: to be seen once and remembered, and to be known again at a glance.

Marks of origin are ancient. Potters, stonemasons, and printers marked their work; families and cities used heraldic devices. Modern trademark law gave such marks legal force in the nineteenth century, and the red triangle of a British brewery became the first registered under a new UK trademark law, in 1876.

Logos are usually classified by what they contain:

- **Wordmarks** are a name in a distinctive typeface or lettering; see [[Typeface]] and [[Kerning]].
- **Lettermarks** reduce a long name to initials.
- **Symbols** are pictures or abstract shapes with no text.
- **Combination marks** pair a symbol with a name, often designed to separate as the space requires.
- **Emblems** enclose the name inside a badge or seal.

The modernist approach to logo design took hold in the 1950s and 1960s. Designers influenced by the Bauhaus and Swiss typography favored simple geometric marks that would reproduce cleanly in any medium; see [[Bauhaus]] and [[Swiss Style]]. Paul Rand's mark for IBM, with letters cut by horizontal stripes, is a canonical example. Some of the most valuable marks cost almost nothing: the Nike swoosh was designed in 1971 by a student for a small fee.

Practical constraints shape good logos:

- **Scale.** A logo must survive at the size of a browser tab icon and on the side of a building. Detailed marks often ship in simplified versions for small sizes; see [[Favicon]].
- **Color.** It should still work in one color, in reverse on dark backgrounds, and when printed cheaply; see [[Color Theory]].
- **Format.** Logos are drawn as vector shapes so they scale without loss; see [[Vector Graphics]].
- **Flexibility.** Responsive logo systems define variants for different widths, following the same logic as responsive layouts; see [[Responsive Breakpoint]].

Familiarity counts for much of how logos are judged. People tend to prefer what they've seen often, so an established logo feels right partly because it's established, and redesigns routinely meet backlash that fades with exposure. A clothing retailer's 2010 redesign was withdrawn within a week after public complaints; see [[Mere-Exposure Effect]] and [[Processing Fluency]].

Claims that famous logos were built on the golden ratio are mostly drawn on afterward, with circles chosen to fit; see [[Golden Ratio]]. In the 2010s many brands flattened their marks, stripping gradients and shadows to suit small screens, and some later restored them; see [[Flat Design]].

A logo is the smallest unit of a larger visual identity, which also specifies typefaces, colors, and usage rules; see [[Design System]].
