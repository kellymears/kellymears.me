**Dieter Rams** is a German industrial designer, born in 1932, best known for the products he designed and directed at the electronics company Braun, where he was head of design from 1961 to 1995. His work, and the ten principles he wrote to explain it, became a reference point for product and interface designers, summarized in his motto "less but better."

Rams trained as an architect and joined Braun in 1955. Working with designers and teachers from the Ulm School of Design, a successor in spirit to the Bauhaus, Braun produced radios, record players, shavers, and kitchen appliances with plain forms, restrained color, and controls laid out in clear grids; see [[Bauhaus]] and [[Grid System]]. The 1956 radio-phonograph SK 4, with Hans Gugelot, put a clear plastic lid over a white metal body and was nicknamed "Snow White's coffin." For the furniture maker Vitsœ he designed a 1960 shelving system still in production.

His **ten principles** say good design:

1. Is innovative.
2. Makes a product useful.
3. Is aesthetic; see [[Aesthetic-Usability Effect]].
4. Makes a product understandable, so that it explains itself; see [[Affordance]] and [[Signifier]].
5. Is unobtrusive, a tool rather than a work of art; see [[Calm Technology]].
6. Is honest, not making a product appear more powerful or valuable than it is.
7. Is long-lasting, avoiding fashion so it doesn't look dated.
8. Is thorough down to the last detail.
9. Is environmentally friendly.
10. Is as little design as possible.

The principles are less a method than a set of values, and they reject much of the industry Rams worked in. He has said he regrets contributing to a culture of overconsumption and has argued for fewer, better, longer-lasting things; see [[Planned Obsolescence]] and [[Right to Repair]].

His influence on consumer technology is direct and acknowledged. Apple's design team under Jony Ive admired his work, and resemblances are easy to spot: the 1958 Braun T3 pocket radio and the first iPod, and the 1987 ET66 calculator, by Dietrich Lubs under Rams, and the original iPhone calculator app. Rams himself called Apple one of the few companies designing in his spirit.

The "as little design as possible" principle maps onto software, where it argues against features, ornament, and settings that serve the maker more than the user; see [[Feature Creep]] and [[White Space]]. It also has a common misreading: minimal appearance is not the same as minimal design. A blank interface that hides what it can do is not simple to use, only simple to look at; see [[Flat Design]].

His principles are often paired with the older slogan they extend; see [[Form Follows Function]].
