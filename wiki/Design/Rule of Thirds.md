The **rule of thirds** is a guideline for arranging an image. Imagine the frame divided by two horizontal and two vertical lines into nine equal parts. Placing the main subject along one of those lines, or at one of the four points where they cross, and putting a horizon on the upper or lower line rather than across the middle, tends to produce a more balanced and dynamic picture than centering everything.

The term goes back to 1797, when the English engraver John Thomas Smith described it in a book about painting rural landscapes, discussing how to divide a scene between light and dark, land and sky. Painters had used similar proportions long before. The idea passed into photography manuals and then into cinema and design teaching, and today most camera apps can overlay the grid on the viewfinder.

Its appeal is partly about avoiding defaults. A beginner instinctively centers the subject and splits the frame with the horizon, producing static, symmetrical pictures. Moving the subject off center leaves space in the frame for it to look or move into, and creates tension between the subject and the empty area; see [[White Space]] and [[Visual Hierarchy]]. The grid is also an easy-to-remember approximation of compositions based on the [[Golden Ratio]], whose so-called phi grid places its lines slightly closer to the center.

Its scientific support is modest. Eye-tracking studies show viewers fixate first on faces, text and areas of high contrast, wherever they are, and evidence that thirds placement makes pictures more pleasing is mixed; see [[Eye Tracking]] and [[Preattentive Processing]]. Many experienced photographers treat it as a training aid to be outgrown. Symmetrical, centered compositions are effective when the subject itself is symmetrical, or when the goal is stillness or confrontation, and some film directors build entire visual styles around them.

The same thinking applies to page and screen layout. Dividing a page into columns and rows gives designers anchor points for headlines and images, and asymmetric layouts based on a grid are a hallmark of modernist graphic design; see [[Grid System]] and [[Swiss Style]]. Thumbnails and cropped previews need to keep the important part visible across many shapes; see [[Aspect Ratio]].

Automatic cropping makes the question practical. Software that crops images for previews usually predicts where people will look and keeps that region, rather than applying the rule of thirds. One social network's automated cropping tool was retired in 2021 after users and researchers found it favored some faces over others; see [[Saliency map]] and [[Algorithmic Bias]]. Resizing to fit a frame introduces its own trade-offs; see [[Image Resampling]].
