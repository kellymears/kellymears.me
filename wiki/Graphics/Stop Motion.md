**Stop motion** animates physical objects by photographing them one frame at a time, moving them slightly between exposures. Played back at speed, the still images merge into movement; see [[Apparent Motion]]. Puppets, clay figures, cut paper, toys, and even people can be animated this way, and the result keeps something no other method has: real materials, real light, and the fingerprints of the people who moved them.

The technique is almost as old as film. Early trick films around 1900 brought toys and objects to life, and Willis O'Brien's work on *The Lost World* (1925) and *King Kong* (1933) used jointed metal skeletons covered in rubber and fur. His student Ray Harryhausen spent decades on fantasy films, culminating in the sword fight against seven skeletons in *Jason and the Argonauts* (1963), a few seconds of which took months.

Common forms:

- **Puppet animation** uses jointed internal armatures that hold any pose, sometimes with replaceable faces.
- **Clay animation** sculpts figures in plasticine that can be reshaped between frames. Will Vinton trademarked "Claymation" in the 1970s, and Aardman's *Wallace & Gromit* made it a British institution.
- **Cutout** and **pixilation** animate flat paper shapes and live actors, respectively.

Animators work in strict sequence. Each pose must be decided before the next frame, since there is no going back without reshooting the whole shot, and timing is planned in charts beforehand; see [[Keyframe Animation]] and [[Twelve Basic Principles of Animation]]. Productions usually shoot on "twos," one pose held for two frames, giving 12 poses a second, and a skilled animator might complete a few seconds of footage in a day; see [[Frame Rate]].

Each frame is a perfectly sharp still, so fast movement lacks the motion blur a camera gives real moving objects, producing the medium's characteristic staccato flicker; see [[Motion Blur]]. For *Dragonslayer* (1981), Phil Tippett's team developed **go motion**, moving the puppet with computer-controlled rods during each exposure so frames blurred naturally.

Computers transformed the craft and nearly ended it. Tippett's studio was preparing go-motion dinosaurs for *Jurassic Park* when computer-animated tests proved more convincing; his line "I think I'm extinct" was adapted into the film's dialogue, and his team moved on to directing the digital animation, using a jointed input device shaped like a dinosaur armature; see [[Motion Capture]] and [[Skeletal Animation]].

Stop motion survived by changing. Laika's *Coraline* (2009) used thousands of 3D-printed replacement faces, swapped frame by frame, and later films printed hundreds of thousands. Digital cameras show the previous frame overlaid as the animator works, and software removes rigs and supports afterward; see [[Rotoscoping]] and [[Chroma Key]]. Some studios now imitate the medium's look in computer animation, and audiences have learned to read the jitter as warmth rather than error; see [[Uncanny Valley]].
