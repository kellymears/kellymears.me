**Apparent motion** is the perception of continuous movement from a series of still images shown in quick succession. Film, animation, television, and every game run on it: nothing on a screen actually moves, yet the visual system fills in the motion between frames. The effect has several overlapping names. **Beta movement** is the smooth movement seen when a light flashes in one place and then another. The **phi phenomenon**, described by Max Wertheimer in 1912, is a stranger variant in which motion is sensed without any object appearing to travel, an observation that helped found Gestalt psychology; see [[Gestalt Principles]].

The popular explanation, **persistence of vision**, says each image lingers on the retina until the next arrives. It is wrong, or at least incomplete. A lingering afterimage would blur frames together rather than produce motion. Modern accounts locate the effect in the brain's motion-detecting circuits, which respond to a shift in position over time whether the shift is continuous or not. The visual system infers the path between samples much as an animator's inbetweener would; see [[Keyframe Animation]].

Optical toys exploited it long before film. The phenakistiscope (1833) and zoetrope (1834) spun a disc or drum of drawings viewed through slits, which acted as a shutter between frames. Eadweard Muybridge's photographs of a galloping horse in 1878 broke motion into sequences, and projected film followed in the 1890s.

Two different thresholds matter, and they are often confused:

- **Motion.** Somewhere around 10 to 12 images per second, a sequence starts to read as movement rather than a slideshow. Hand-drawn animation often runs at 12 drawings per second, each held for two film frames; see [[Twelve Basic Principles of Animation]].
- **Flicker.** The light between frames must alternate fast enough not to flicker. That threshold is higher, about 50 to 90 flashes per second depending on brightness and where in the visual field it falls. Film projectors flash each of 24 frames two or three times for this reason; see [[LED Flicker]].

Sampling motion also produces illusions. A spinning wheel filmed at a fixed rate can appear to stand still or turn backward when its spokes advance almost exactly one gap per frame, the wagon-wheel effect, a form of temporal [[Aliasing]]. Fast-moving objects on a screen that holds each frame steady are smeared by the eye as it tracks them; see [[Motion Blur]].

Higher [[Frame Rate]]s narrow the gap between samples, so motion looks smoother and objects stay sharper while the eye follows them. [[Virtual Reality]] headsets go further, flashing each frame briefly and keeping the screen dark between, because a held image smears with every turn of the head. The eye's own jumps also shape what is seen; see [[Eye Tracking]].
