**Gacha monetization** sells randomized draws from a pool of rewards rather than selling the rewards directly. The name comes from Japanese capsule-toy machines; the model reached scale in mobile games around 2010 and spread outward as the Western "loot box".

Four structures do the work. **Published pull rates** state the probability of each rarity tier, typically near one percent at the top — a range where a fraction of a [[Percentage Point]] compounds into a large difference in expected spend. **Pity and spark counters** guarantee a top-tier result after a fixed number of failures, converting an unbounded loss into a bounded one; a player who cannot price a probability can price a ceiling, and the ceiling becomes the target. **Duplicate-to-power ladders** keep an item valuable after it is obtained, so the pool never saturates. **Multi-currency economies** put two or three conversions between money and a draw, in bundles that never divide evenly into a draw's cost, so pricing an outcome takes deliberate [[Fermi Estimation]] rather than reading a label.

The draw itself is ordinary [[Seeded Randomness]] resolved on a server; the effort goes into presentation. A long reveal, a rarity flash and a near-miss framing are tuned with the craft of [[Game Feel]]. Limited-time banners supply an [[Anchoring Effect]] against which smaller purchases read as restraint, and the escalating relief of a guaranteed pull follows [[Opponent Process Theory]]. Where revenue per player becomes the design metric, [[Goodhart's Law]] applies with force.

Disclosure rules followed. Japan banned "complete gacha" in 2012 under prize-labelling law; China has required published probabilities since 2017; Belgium's regulator treated paid loot boxes as gambling in 2018; South Korea mandated rate disclosure in 2024. Enforcement varies, and disclosure in [[Plain Language]] remains rarer than disclosure as such.
