**Holography** records and reconstructs light so that a flat medium shows a fully three-dimensional image. Move your head and you see around the objects, as if looking through a window. Unlike a photograph, which records only how bright light is at each point, a hologram also records the phase of the light waves, and with it the direction they came from.

Dennis Gabor invented holography in 1947 while trying to improve electron microscopes, and received the 1971 Nobel Prize in Physics for it. Practical holograms had to wait for a light source with a steady, uniform wavelength. The laser, demonstrated in 1960, provided one; see [[Laser]]. In 1962 Emmett Leith and Juris Upatnieks in the United States, and Yuri Denisyuk in the Soviet Union, made the first convincing 3D holograms.

Making a hologram uses interference:

1. A laser beam is split in two.
2. One beam, the object beam, lights the scene, and light scattered from the objects reaches a photographic plate.
3. The other, the reference beam, goes straight to the plate.
4. Where the two beams meet, they reinforce or cancel, producing a microscopic pattern of fringes; see [[Wave Interference]].

The plate records only that fringe pattern, which looks like meaningless swirls. Lit again by a beam like the reference, the pattern bends light so that it recreates the original light waves from the scene; see [[Diffraction]]. The eye can't distinguish them from light from real objects, so the image shows real depth and parallax, unlike stereoscopic displays, which give each eye a separate flat picture; see [[Stereoscopy]].

A curious property: every part of the plate receives light from the whole scene. Cut a hologram in half, and each half still shows the entire scene, from a narrower range of viewpoints and with less detail.

Most holograms people encounter are embossed **rainbow holograms**, mass-produced by stamping fringe patterns into metallized plastic. They appear on credit cards, banknotes, and product packaging as anti-counterfeiting features, since they're hard to copy with ordinary printing. Their shifting colors come from diffraction, the same way the surface of a disc or a butterfly's wing produces color; see [[Structural Color]].

The word is often borrowed for things that aren't holograms. Concert performances by deceased musicians projected onstage typically use Pepper's ghost, a 19th-century stage illusion that reflects an image off angled glass. Mixed reality headsets that call their images holograms generally use stereoscopic displays; see [[Virtual Reality]].

Researchers continue to work on holographic displays computed in real time, which require enormous pixel densities and computation, partly using Fourier methods; see [[Fourier Transform]]. Related approaches capture light fields, recording light by direction as well as position; see [[Gaussian Splatting]]. Holographic interferometry also measures tiny deformations and vibrations in engineering.
