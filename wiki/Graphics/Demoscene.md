The **demoscene** is a computer art subculture that makes **demos**: real-time audiovisual pieces, generated by code as they play, that show off programming, art, and music together. It grew out of the 1980s home computer scene in Europe, especially on the Commodore 64 and Amiga, and is still active, with competitions held at events called demoparties.

Its roots are in software piracy. Groups that cracked copy protection on games added short intros, "cracktros," with scrolling text, logos, and music to sign their work. The intros grew more elaborate until making them became the point, and the scene split from piracy into its own culture of friendly rivalry between groups with names like Future Crew and Farbrausch.

What separates a demo from a video is that everything is computed live, and much of the prestige comes from doing the impossible on fixed hardware. Coders pushed old machines past their documented limits, timing code to the display beam to show more colors and sprites than the hardware supposedly allowed; see [[Racing the Beam]]. Many demosceners went on to work in the games industry.

Competitions are split by platform and by size:

- **Size-limited intros.** A 64-kilobyte intro fits minutes of 3D graphics and a full soundtrack into a file smaller than many web page images. A 4-kilobyte intro does it in less space than this note's text. Nothing can be stored, so textures, meshes, and music are generated from formulas at startup, and executables are squeezed with custom compressors; see [[Procedural Generation]] and [[Data Compression]].
- **Sizecoding.** Categories go down to 256 bytes and below, where one clever formula has to produce the whole effect.
- **Oldschool.** Demos for the C64, Amiga, and other old machines still compete, exploiting hardware studied for forty years.

Tiny intros are a practical demonstration of [[Kolmogorov Complexity]]: a few kilobytes of code describe something that would take gigabytes to store as video. A common modern approach draws the whole scene in one [[Shader]] that steps through shapes defined by distance functions; see [[Ray Marching]] and [[Signed Distance Field]]. The website Shadertoy grew out of this style. Textures come from noise functions; see [[Perlin Noise]].

The scene has its own aesthetics: [[Pixel Art]] drawn by hand on old machines, tracker music composed in scrolling grids of notes, and effects with names like plasma, rotozoomer, and tunnel. It values craft visible mainly to people who know how hard it is, much like [[Speedrun]] communities.

Several European countries, starting with Finland in 2020, have recognized the demoscene as intangible cultural heritage. Preserving it raises the usual problems of keeping old software runnable; see [[Emulation]] and [[Digital Preservation]].
