A **smear frame** is a drawing in an animated sequence that distorts a fast-moving figure to show the motion between two poses. A character whipping around to look behind them might, for one frame, become a long streak of color from the old position to the new one. Seen at speed, the smear is barely registered as an image; it reads as speed. Paused, it looks bizarre, which is why paused smears circulate as internet curiosities.

The technique solves a sampling problem. Film shows 24 frames a second, and hand-drawn animation often holds each drawing for two frames, so a motion lasting a tenth of a second may get only one or two drawings. If a limb is drawn sharp in each, the eye sees it jump instead of move, a stuttering called strobing; see [[Apparent Motion]] and [[Frame Rate]]. A live-action camera avoids this automatically, since its shutter stays open long enough to blur anything moving fast; see [[Motion Blur]]. Animators have to draw the blur themselves.

Animators developed several kinds:

- **Stretched smears.** The figure is elongated along its path, like taffy, often thinning in the middle. It's an extreme form of squash and stretch.
- **Multiples.** Several copies of a limb or a whole figure are drawn at once along the arc, like a stroboscopic photograph. Chuck Jones's 1942 Warner Bros. short The Dover Boys is famous for both kinds.
- **Speed lines and dry-brush streaks.** Lines or broken strokes trail the moving shape, a convention shared with comics.
- **Abstract shapes.** At the extreme, the figure becomes a blob or a swoosh of color for a frame before resolving.

Smears let animators keep a sequence crisp and readable while still conveying force. Combined with strong poses on either side and timing that holds poses and snaps between them, they give classic cartoons their punch; see [[Twelve Basic Principles of Animation]] and [[Keyframe Animation]].

Computer animation inherited the problem in reverse. Rendered frames are perfect instants, so CG adds motion blur to look filmed. Stylized productions sometimes choose drawn-looking smears instead. Sony's Spider-Man: Into the Spider-Verse in 2018 mixed animation on twos, holding poses for two frames, with smear frames and multiples built from extra geometry, to make its CG look hand drawn; see [[Cel Shading]] and [[Stop Motion]]. Games use similar tricks, stretching meshes or adding trails along a swing to make attacks feel faster; see [[Game Feel]] and [[Particle System]].

Interpolating frames by computer has trouble with smears. Software that generates in-between frames assumes objects keep their shape, so a deliberate distortion confuses it, and fans have long complained that motion smoothing ruins hand-drawn timing; see [[Frame Generation]].

Smears show that the eye perceives motion from cues rather than accurate snapshots; see [[Gestalt Principles]].
