**Ludonarrative dissonance** is the conflict between what a game's systems reward and what its narrative says it values. Clint Hocking coined the term in a 2007 essay about *BioShock*, whose story rebukes the objectivist philosophy of its setting while its systems pay out for rational self-interest — most visibly in the choice to harvest or spare the Little Sisters. His formulation was narrow: the work offers two contracts, ludic and narrative, and honouring one breaks the other.

The counter-position is that the tension is often deliberate. *Spec Ops: The Line* (2012) makes the player's continued compliance its subject; a game about the futility of an atrocity that felt uncomplicated to play would fail at its own argument. A stricter objection is that most conventions are dissonant if read literally — health pickups, respawns, an inventory carried in nothing — and audiences treat them as notation rather than claims about the world. The charge is worth making only when a work asserts the thing its systems contradict.

The term escaped that sense quickly and now covers any mismatch a player notices, usually that an affable action hero kills hundreds of people between cutscenes. Cases accumulated by resemblance to a central example, much as [[Prototype Theory]] describes category membership. It is a standing caution about [[Naming]]: a memorable coinage travels faster than its definition.

What survives is a design check. A reward schedule teaches behaviour regardless of the script wrapped around it, which is [[Goodhart's Law]] operating inside a fiction, and the sharpest cases are economic — a story about sacrifice sitting on top of [[Gacha Monetization]]. Works built on [[Environmental Storytelling]] largely sidestep the problem by asserting less.
