**Garbage in, garbage out** is the principle that the output of a computation can be no better than its input. Flawed data, processed flawlessly, gives flawed results. The phrase appears in print by 1957, but the idea is older. In 1864 Charles Babbage recalled being asked twice, by members of Parliament, whether his machine would give the right answers if fed the wrong figures, and wrote that he could not comprehend the confusion of ideas behind the question; see [[Analytical Engine]].

The principle sounds obvious, so its force is in where it gets forgotten. Computers lend their output an authority the input never had. A clean chart, a precise decimal, or a fluent paragraph looks trustworthy whatever went into it, which is why the joke variant "garbage in, gospel out" exists.

Common sources of garbage:

- **Entry and conversion errors.** Spreadsheets silently turn some gene names into dates, such as SEPT1 into September 1, and the problem was widespread enough in published genetics papers that in 2020 the gene-naming committee renamed the affected genes; see [[Spreadsheet]].
- **Biased samples.** Data that over-represents some group carries that skew into every conclusion drawn from it; see [[Algorithmic Bias]].
- **Mislabeled examples.** Machine learning models learn whatever their labels say. A 2021 study estimated that several percent of the labels in widely used benchmark test sets were wrong, enough to change which model ranks best; see [[Training data]].
- **Recycled output.** Models trained on text generated by earlier models can lose the rare parts of the original distribution, a degradation called model collapse; see [[Model collapse]].
- **Drift.** A source changes its format or meaning without notice, and downstream jobs keep running on values that no longer mean what they did; see [[Schema Drift]].

The defenses are mostly about catching garbage at the door. Validating data where it enters a system, against an explicit schema or agreement with its producer, stops bad records before they spread; see [[Schema Validation]] and [[Data Contract]]. Rejecting malformed input loudly is usually better than guessing what was meant; see [[Fail Fast]]. That cuts against the old advice to be liberal in what you accept, which lets garbage in quietly; see [[Postel's Law]]. Tracking where each value came from makes it possible to find and purge the bad batch later; see [[Data Lineage]] and [[Data Cleaning]].

The principle has limits. When the kind of error is known, it can be filtered: error-correcting codes recover messages from noisy channels, and robust statistics tolerate outliers; see [[Error-Correcting Code]]. What no method can recover is information that was never collected or a bias nobody measured.
