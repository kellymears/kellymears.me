**ISO 8601** is the international standard for writing dates and times as text, first published in 1988. Its central rule is to put the largest unit first: `2026-10-18` for a date, `2026-10-18T14:30:00Z` for a moment in time.

That ordering solves two problems. It removes ambiguity: `03/04/2026` means March 4 in the United States and 3 April in most of the world, while `2026-03-04` means only one thing. And with fixed-width fields, dates sorted as plain text come out in chronological order, so file names, log lines, and keys that start with a date stay in order without any parsing; see [[Collation]].

The standard covers much more than calendar dates:

- **Times and offsets.** A `T` separates date from time. `Z` marks UTC, and an offset such as `+02:00` gives local time relative to it.
- **Durations.** `P3DT4H` is three days and four hours.
- **Intervals.** Two times joined by a slash.
- **Week dates.** `2026-W42-7` is the seventh day of week 42. ISO week 1 is the week containing the year's first Thursday.
- **Ordinal dates.** `2026-291` is the 291st day of the year.

An offset is not a time zone. `+02:00` records the difference from UTC at that moment, but not which region's rules apply, so it can't say what the offset will be after a daylight saving change. Scheduling future events in local time needs the zone name as well; see [[Time Zone]].

Week dates cause a classic bug. The ISO week-numbering year differs from the calendar year for a few days around New Year, so December 29 can fall in week 1 of the following year. Many date-formatting libraries use `YYYY` for the week-based year and `yyyy` for the calendar year, and code that used the wrong one has displayed the wrong year every late December, sometimes breaking logins and expiry checks.

**RFC 3339**, published in 2002, is a stricter profile of ISO 8601 for internet protocols. It requires a full date, time, and offset, and drops the rarer forms. Because [[JSON]] has no date type, RFC 3339 strings are the usual convention for sending dates in APIs; see [[Serialization]]. The main alternative is a count of seconds since 1970, which is compact but unreadable; see [[Unix Time]]. ISO 8601 even allows a 60th second in a minute, for leap seconds; see [[Leap second]].

Four-digit years were part of the point. The Year 2000 problem came from storing years as two digits to save space, and a similar overflow in timestamp counters is due in 2038; see [[Year 2038 problem]].

The format also guards against unwanted conversion. Spreadsheets turn anything that looks like a date into one. Gene names such as SEPT1 and MARCH1 were mangled so often that in 2020 the genes were renamed; see [[Spreadsheet]].
