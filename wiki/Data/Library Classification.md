**Library classification** assigns each book a code that fixes its place on the shelf, so that books on related subjects stand next to each other. It solves two problems at once: finding a known book, and browsing a subject by walking along the shelf.

The best known scheme is the **Dewey Decimal Classification**, published by Melvil Dewey in 1876 while he was a student assistant at Amherst College. It divides all knowledge into ten main classes numbered 000 to 900, such as 500 for natural sciences and 800 for literature, each divided into ten divisions and ten sections, with decimals subdividing as finely as needed. Its numbers can grow without limit, so new subjects fit between old ones without renumbering. Public and school libraries in over a hundred countries use it; see [[Hierarchical Data]].

Large research libraries mostly use the **Library of Congress Classification**, developed from 1897 to organize that library's own collection. It uses letters for broad classes, such as Q for science and P for language and literature, followed by numbers, and leaves more room for the fine distinctions specialist collections need.

A **call number** combines the class with a code for the author and often the year, giving every copy a unique, sortable address.

Every classification embeds a worldview, and its early choices stick; see [[Taxonomy]]. Dewey gave Christianity most of the 200s, religion, and squeezed all other religions into the 290s. Topics such as homosexuality were long placed under mental disorders or social problems. Changing a class means relabeling and moving thousands of books, so such fixes come slowly. Subject headings share the tension: a campaign begun in 2014 to change the Library of Congress heading "Illegal aliens" became a congressional dispute.

A physical book can stand in only one place, but most books are about several things. A history of medicine in wartime belongs with medicine, war, and history. Shelf order picks one; the catalog records the rest. The **card catalog** held a card for each book under its author, its title, and each of its subjects, all pointing to the one shelf location. In database terms, shelf order is the clustered index and the catalog is a set of secondary indexes; see [[Database Index]]. French revolutionaries inventorying seized church libraries wrote entries on the backs of playing cards.

The Indian librarian S. R. Ranganathan proposed a different approach in 1933, building a book's code from independent facets, such as subject, place, and time, an idea that now drives filtering on shopping sites; see [[Faceted Search]].

Digital catalogs, search, and tags freed collections from a single order; see [[Metadata]] and [[Inverted Index]]. Shelf browsing still offers what search does not: books you didn't know to look for, sitting beside the one you came for; see [[Information Architecture]].
