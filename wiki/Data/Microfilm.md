**Microfilm** stores documents as tiny photographs on film, typically shrunk to between a fifteenth and a fiftieth of their original size. Reading it takes only magnification and light. Rolls of film are microfilm; flat sheets holding a grid of page images are **microfiche**; the family together is **microform**.

Photographic reduction was demonstrated within a few years of photography itself, but its first serious use was military. During the siege of Paris in 1870 and 1871, the photographer René Dagron shrank pages of messages onto thin film, carrier pigeons flew them into the city, and they were projected onto a wall and copied out by hand.

Commercial use began with banks. In the 1920s a New York banker built a machine to photograph checks as they were processed, and Kodak sold it as Recordak, giving banks a compact record of every transaction; see [[Audit Log]]. Libraries followed in the 1930s, filming newspapers, which were printed on acidic paper that crumbled, and doctoral dissertations, which could then be copied on demand. During the Second World War the US military's V-mail service photographed soldiers' letters, flew the film overseas, and printed them at the other end, saving tons of cargo space; see [[Data Compression]].

Vannevar Bush's 1945 vision of a desk holding a person's whole library, with linked trails through it, assumed microfilm as its storage; see [[Memex]].

For decades microfiche held library catalogs, parts lists, and government records, and a generation of researchers knew the whir of the reader at the back of the library; see [[Library Classification]]. Digitizing those reels later fed newspaper archives online, with text recognition turning page images into searchable text, often badly for faded type; see [[Optical Character Recognition]].

Microfilm's lasting appeal is preservation. Silver-image film on a polyester base, kept cool and dry, is expected to last for centuries, and it will never need a file format, a program, or a working computer to read; see [[Digital Preservation]]. Digital files, in contrast, must be copied forward and migrated or they become unreadable. Some archives therefore still print digital records onto film as a last-resort copy; see [[Backup and Restore]]. Older film on an acetate base is a counterexample, and many collections from the mid-twentieth century are on it: it decays, giving off a vinegar smell as it shrinks and warps.

Filming also had a cost. Many libraries discarded original newspapers after microfilming them, losing color, photographs, and pages the filming missed, which the novelist Nicholson Baker criticized in his 2001 book *Double Fold*. A copy is a format choice, and the choice shapes what later readers can see; see [[Data Retention]] and [[Link Rot]].
