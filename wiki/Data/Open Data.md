**Open data** is data that anyone is free to use, reuse, and redistribute, for any purpose, subject at most to crediting the source and sharing alike. The definition, set out by the Open Knowledge Foundation in 2005, has two halves: the **legal** half, an open license that grants these rights, and the **technical** half, publication in complete, machine-readable form at no more than the cost of reproduction.

The movement drew on open source software and on older traditions of public records and shared science. In 2009 the United States launched data.gov and the United Kingdom followed with data.gov.uk, and governments around the world set up **data portals** cataloging their published data sets. Science moved in the same direction: funders increasingly require that the data behind published papers be shared, partly to let others check results; see [[Replication Crisis]].

Some of the best-known successes:

- **Transit.** When transit agencies published timetables in a common format, every map and trip-planning app could suddenly route buses and trains, rather than only the agency's own site.
- **Weather.** Freely published government forecasts and observations feed nearly every weather app and much commercial forecasting.
- **Maps.** OpenStreetMap, built by volunteers since 2004, is an open map of the world used by companies and humanitarian groups alike; see [[Commons-Based Peer Production]] and [[Map Projection]].
- **Satellite imagery.** When the US made Landsat archives free in 2008, downloads rose from thousands of images a year to millions.

Format matters as much as permission. Tim Berners-Lee's five-star scheme ranks open data from a scanned PDF (one star) through a spreadsheet, then non-proprietary formats like [[CSV]] or [[JSON]], up to data that uses web identifiers and links to other data sets (five stars); see [[Ontology]]. A table published as an image is technically open and practically useless. Well-described data also needs [[Metadata]]: what each column means, when it was collected, and how.

Open data has persistent problems:

- **Privacy.** Records stripped of names can often be re-identified by combining them with other public data; see [[Differential Privacy]] and [[Personal Data]].
- **Maintenance.** Portals fill with data sets published once and never updated, and links to them break; see [[Link Rot]].
- **Quality.** Raw government data is full of inconsistent codes and missing values, and someone has to clean it; see [[Data Cleaning]].
- **Funding.** Publishing well costs money, and the benefits go to users rather than the publisher, a classic public-goods problem; see [[Tragedy of the Commons]].

Licenses differ in what they ask. Some only require credit, while share-alike licenses require that derived data stay open, a dispute OpenStreetMap went through when it relicensed in 2012.
