A **gazetteer** is a dictionary of places. Each entry gives a name, the kind of place it is, such as a city, river or mountain, where it lies, and often other facts like population or the larger regions it belongs to. Gazetteers are the index behind maps: they connect a name a person types or reads to a point or area on the earth; see [[Geospatial Data]].

The genre is ancient. Greek and Roman geographers compiled lists of places with their coordinates or descriptions, and a sixth-century Byzantine scholar, Stephanus of Byzantium, produced a large alphabetical dictionary of place names. Printed gazetteers became standard references from the eighteenth century, often appearing as companions to atlases; see [[Library Classification]].

Modern gazetteers are databases. National mapping agencies maintain official ones, and in the United States a federal board decides which names official maps use. GeoNames, an openly licensed global gazetteer, holds millions of entries, and [[Wikidata]] links places to identifiers in many such sources; see [[Persistent Identifier]] and [[Linked Data]]. Volunteer projects such as [[OpenStreetMap]] attach names to features directly.

Place names are messier than they seem. The same name often refers to many places: dozens of US towns are called Springfield, and Paris is also a town in Texas. Telling them apart needs context, such as the surrounding region or what else is mentioned nearby; see [[Entity Resolution]]. Names also change. Bombay became Mumbai in 1995, Ceylon became Sri Lanka in 1972, and many places have carried several names over the centuries, so a historical document needs a gazetteer that knows which name applied when. Projects like Pleiades catalog the places of the ancient world for exactly that reason, recording each name with the period in which it was used.

Languages add another layer. A place's local name is its endonym; the name outsiders use is an **exonym**. Germany is Deutschland to its residents and Allemagne in French. Names written in other scripts need consistent romanization, and rival systems give different spellings; see [[Transliteration]]. Names can be political, too: what a place is called on a map may imply which country it belongs to, and some disputed territories have different official names in neighboring states.

Gazetteers quietly power much everyday technology. Turning an address or a place name into coordinates is geocoding, and it depends on a gazetteer's coverage and quality; see [[Geocoding]] and [[Postal Address]]. Search engines recognize place names in queries, news archives tag articles by location, and researchers link historical records to maps. Standard codes for countries and regions are a minimal gazetteer of their own; see [[Country Code]].
