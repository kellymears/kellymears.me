**Archival science** is the study and practice of keeping records for the long term: deciding which to keep, arranging and describing them, preserving them, and making them available. An archive differs from a library. A library collects published works, mostly copies that exist elsewhere, and describes each one individually; see [[Library Classification]]. An archive holds unique, unpublished records, such as letters, minutes, ledgers, and files, created by people and organizations in the course of their work, and describes them in groups.

Two principles define the field:

- **Respect des fonds.** Records created by one person or organization, a body called a fonds, are kept together and never mixed with records from other sources. A French circular of 1841 is usually cited as the origin. The context of creation is itself evidence; see [[Provenance]].
- **Original order.** Within a fonds, records stay in the arrangement their creator used. A Dutch manual of 1898 made this a rule. The order shows how the records were used, and rearranging them by subject destroys that information.

Both principles reject the instinct to reorganize by topic. A historian studying a ministry wants to see what passed through each office together, not every document on railways gathered in one box.

Archives describe collections with a **finding aid**, a document that moves from the whole down through series, files, and sometimes items, giving each level's dates, extent, creator, and context; see [[Hierarchical Data]] and [[Metadata]]. Describing items one by one is impossible at archival scale, so description is pitched at the level where it's affordable and useful.

**Appraisal**, deciding what to keep, is the field's hardest judgment. Most records ever created are eventually destroyed, and archivists keep only a small fraction. What's chosen shapes what later generations can know, and gaps in the record are easy to mistake for gaps in history; see [[Survivorship Bias]]. Organizations formalize these decisions in schedules that say how long each kind of record is kept; see [[Records management]].

Physical preservation means controlling temperature, humidity, light, and handling, and making copies so originals can rest; see [[Microfilm]]. Born-digital records add new problems: file formats that become unreadable, media that decay, and the need to prove a file hasn't changed; see [[Digital Preservation]] and [[Checksum]]. Websites are archived by capturing snapshots, since the live versions change or vanish; see [[Link Rot]].

Archival thinking has influenced data work. Tracking where a dataset came from and what transformed it is provenance under another name; see [[Data Lineage]]. Records that must never be altered, only added to, follow archival ideas of authenticity; see [[Audit Log]]. And stable identifiers that keep resolving long after a system changes are an archivist's priority; see [[Persistent Identifier]].
