A **citation index** lists, for each publication, the later works that cite it. An ordinary bibliography points backward, to sources a paper relied on; a citation index lets a reader go forward, from an old paper to everyone who has built on it, challenged it or corrected it since.

The idea first appeared in law. From 1873, Shepard's Citations let American lawyers check whether a court decision had been cited, followed or overturned by later rulings, so that they didn't rely on dead precedent. Eugene Garfield proposed applying the same idea to science in 1955, arguing that following citations could find related work that subject classifications missed; see [[Library Classification]]. Garfield's company launched the Science Citation Index in 1964, compiled by hand and published in thick printed volumes.

Garfield's index gave rise to **bibliometrics**, the quantitative study of publications. Counting citations made it possible to measure which papers and journals were most used. The **impact factor**, roughly the average number of recent citations to a journal's articles, was meant to help libraries choose subscriptions. Physicist Jorge Hirsch proposed the **h-index** in 2005: a researcher has index h if h of their papers have each been cited at least h times. Citations turned out to follow a heavily skewed distribution, where a few papers collect most citations and well-cited work attracts more; see [[Power law]] and [[Matthew Effect]].

The same structure shaped the web. Treating hyperlinks like citations, and weighting a link from a much-cited page more heavily, is the core idea of the algorithm that founded Google; see [[PageRank]] and [[Backlink]]. Citations form a directed graph that can be analyzed to map fields and spot emerging topics; see [[Graph theory]].

Using citations to evaluate people has caused well-known distortions. Once hiring, funding and promotion depend on counts, the counts get gamed: journals have pressured authors to cite the journal itself, groups of researchers have cited each other in coordinated rings, and some publishers have been delisted for manipulation; see [[Goodhart's Law]] and [[Metric fixation]]. Citation counts measure attention, not correctness, and a paper may be heavily cited because it's being refuted; see [[Replication Crisis]]. A 2012 declaration signed by many scientific organizations urged that journal metrics not be used to judge individual scientists.

The field is now dominated by commercial databases and Google Scholar, which since 2004 has built citation links automatically from text across the web, more inclusive but noisier. Open alternatives have grown, built on persistent identifiers that make linking reliable; see [[Persistent Identifier]]. Broken links between papers and the sources they cite are a continuing problem; see [[Link Rot]].
