**Freedom of information** laws give anyone the right to ask a public body for its records and, unless an exemption applies, to receive them. They reverse the default: instead of officials deciding what to publish, the public decides what to ask for, and secrecy has to be justified.

Sweden passed the first such law in 1766, as part of a press freedom act that opened official documents to public inspection. The idea spread slowly. The United States enacted its Freedom of Information Act in 1966, and Congress strengthened it in 1974, overriding a presidential veto, amid the distrust that followed Watergate. A wave of laws followed the end of the Cold War, and more than a hundred countries now have one. The UK's act came into force in 2005; the prime minister who introduced it later wrote in a memoir that it was among the biggest mistakes of that government, a sign of how much it constrained officials.

Requests reach almost anything a government records: emails, contracts, inspection reports, spending data, meeting minutes. Journalists use them to investigate, and many major stories began as a request for a spreadsheet; see [[Data Journalism]]. Researchers, companies and lawyers use them too, and individuals can usually ask for records about themselves; see [[Personal Data]].

Every law carries exemptions, typically for national security, law enforcement, commercial secrets, personal privacy and internal deliberations. Released documents often arrive with large blacked-out passages. Agencies can sometimes refuse to confirm whether records even exist, the **Glomar response**, named after a 1975 case about a CIA ship built to recover a sunken Soviet submarine. Critics say exemptions are stretched, and that delays, fees and narrow searches defeat the law in practice. Some officials have moved discussions to personal email or messaging apps that disappear, to avoid creating records at all; see [[Data Retention]].

The laws depend on good record keeping. A right of access is worth little if records aren't created, organized or kept, which ties freedom of information to archives and records management; see [[Archival Science]]. Data that an agency can't find or extract from old systems is effectively unavailable; see [[Dark Data]].

Many governments have moved from answering requests to publishing proactively, posting datasets in machine-readable form so they don't have to be asked; see [[Open Data]] and [[CSV]]. Published logs of requests and responses let others see what's already been released, and a disclosure to one requester is generally a disclosure to everyone.

The principle extends beyond government in some places, covering publicly funded bodies such as universities and public companies. Released records sometimes become a scandal of their own, as when the public learns how much a public body spends on its officials; see [[Audit Log]].
