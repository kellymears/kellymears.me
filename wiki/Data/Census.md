A **census** is an attempt to count every person in a country at a single moment, usually with a few facts about each: age, sex, household, and often occupation, language, or ethnicity. Most countries hold one every ten years. It is the oldest large-scale data collection still running, and many other statistics, from unemployment rates to survey weights, are calibrated against it.

Counting people has always been about power. Ancient states counted subjects to tax them and raise armies, and the Domesday Book of 1086 surveyed England's land and livestock for the same reason. The US Constitution made the census political in a new way: since 1790 the count has decided how many seats each state gets in the House of Representatives, and today it also steers large sums of federal funding.

The scale has pushed data technology forward. The 1890 US census was the first tabulated by Herman Hollerith's punched-card machines, and the Census Bureau bought one of the first commercial computers, a UNIVAC, in 1951; see [[Punched Card]].

No census reaches everyone, and the misses are not random. Young children, renters, rural residents, and people without stable housing are harder to find, so the count is lower for some groups than others, a **differential undercount**; see [[Selection Bias]]. A follow-up survey estimated that the 2020 US census undercounted Black, Hispanic, and Native American residents on reservations while overcounting some other groups. Statistical adjustment could correct for this, but in 1999 the US Supreme Court ruled that sampling cannot replace the head count for apportioning seats.

Several countries have stopped knocking on doors. Denmark ran the first census built entirely from administrative registers in 1981, and other Nordic countries followed, linking population, housing, and tax records instead; see [[Entity Resolution]]. That requires a national identity number and a public willing to have records joined.

Census data is published as tables for small areas, which feed maps, planning, and research; see [[Choropleth Map]] and [[Open Data]]. Reading individual behavior off area totals is a classic mistake; see [[Ecological Fallacy]].

Privacy is the central tension. Individual US census records are sealed for 72 years, so the 1950 forms were released only in 2022; see [[Data Retention]]. Confidentiality has failed before: during the Second World War the Census Bureau supplied information used to locate Japanese Americans for internment. For 2020, after showing that published tables could be used to reconstruct individual records, the Bureau added calibrated noise to its releases, the largest use of differential privacy to date, and drew objections from researchers who rely on small-area accuracy; see [[Differential Privacy]] and [[Personal Data]].

Benedict Anderson argued that the census, along with the map and the museum, helped colonial states invent the categories people later identified with; see [[Imagined Community]].
