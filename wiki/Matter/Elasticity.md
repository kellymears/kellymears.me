**Elasticity** is the tendency of a solid to return to its original shape after a force deforms it. Pull a steel wire, bend a ruler, or compress a rubber ball, and as long as the force stays modest, the material springs back fully when released. The energy you put in is stored and returned, a case of [[Conservation of Energy]].

Robert Hooke described the basic rule in 1678, after first publishing it as an anagram to claim priority: *ut tensio, sic vis*, "as the extension, so the force." Within its elastic range, a material deforms in proportion to the load. Double the weight on a spring, double the stretch. That proportionality makes springs and elastic structures oscillate with a steady rhythm, the [[Simple Harmonic Motion]] behind clocks and suspension systems.

Engineers describe materials with two normalized quantities. **Stress** is force divided by the cross-sectional area carrying it. **Strain** is the fractional change in length. Their ratio, **Young's modulus**, measures stiffness independent of an object's size. Steel's modulus is about 200 gigapascals, aluminum's about 70, bone's around 20, and rubber's tens of thousands of times smaller. An aluminum bicycle tube must be wider or thicker than a steel one to be equally stiff, which is why aluminum frames have fat tubes.

Three properties are easy to confuse:

- **Stiffness** is resistance to deforming: how much it bends under a load.
- **Strength** is the stress at which it stops recovering (*yield strength*) or breaks (*ultimate strength*).
- **Toughness** is how much energy it absorbs before breaking. Glass is stiff and strong but not tough, so it shatters. Rubber is tough but not stiff.

Past the *elastic limit*, a material deforms permanently. Metals yield and flow, called **plastic deformation**: bend a paperclip far enough and it stays bent. Metalworking depends on this, and so does crash safety, since car bodies are designed to crumple, absorbing energy as they deform. Repeated loading below the elastic limit has its own failure mode, [[Material Fatigue]].

Real materials are rarely perfectly elastic. Some energy is lost as heat in each cycle, a property called *hysteresis*. In tires, that loss is most of [[Rolling Resistance]]. In a bouncing ball it is why each bounce is lower than the last, and in structures it provides useful damping against [[Resonance]].

The body exploits elasticity constantly. The Achilles tendon stretches on landing and recoils on push-off, returning much of the energy in each running stride, as described in [[Tendon and Ligament]]. Bone adjusts its stiffness to the loads it carries, a process covered in [[Bone Remodeling]]. Temperature also matters: most materials soften as they warm, and [[Thermal Expansion]] creates stress when a structure is not free to grow.
