**Caramelization** is what happens when sugar alone is heated until it breaks down. Sucrose (table sugar) begins to caramelize at around 160–180 °C. The molecules split, dehydrate, and recombine into hundreds of compounds: brown polymers for color, plus volatiles such as diacetyl (buttery), furans (nutty), and maltol (toasty). Taken further, the result becomes bitter and finally carbon. The darker the caramel, the less sweet and more bitter it becomes, since sugar is being destroyed.

Different sugars caramelize at different temperatures. Fructose begins well below sucrose, which is one reason honey and fruit brown so quickly. Caramelization is often confused with the [[Maillard Reaction]], which involves amino acids as well as sugars and proceeds at lower temperatures. Most savory "caramelized" foods, including onions, roasted vegetables, and seared meat, owe more to Maillard. Pure caramelization belongs mainly to the sugar work of desserts, brittles, and the top of crème brûlée.

Making caramel on purpose comes in two methods:

- **Dry caramel**: sugar heated alone in a pan until it melts and browns. It is fast but uneven, and burns easily at the edges.
- **Wet caramel**: sugar dissolved in water first. The water must boil off before the temperature can rise past 100 °C, which makes heating more even and gives more control. This is the same lid on temperature that water puts on browning.

Wet caramel has a characteristic failure: **crystallization**. A supersaturated sugar solution can suddenly seize into a grainy mass when a single crystal forms, from an undissolved grain on the pan wall, a stirring spoon, or a speck of dust, and triggers a chain reaction. Cooks guard against it with *interfering agents*: a little acid or corn syrup converts some sucrose into glucose and fructose (*invert sugar*), whose differently shaped molecules disrupt the crystal lattice. Brushing the pan sides with water and not stirring help too. The same seeding principle is why rock candy grows on a string and why fudge is beaten at a particular temperature to make many tiny crystals rather than a few large, gritty ones.

Once cooled, caramel's texture depends on its final temperature, from soft sauce to hard glass. Sugar thermometers mark these as named stages, from soft ball to hard crack, which correspond to sugar concentration as water leaves. The [[Viscosity]] of the finished syrup rises steeply as it cools.
