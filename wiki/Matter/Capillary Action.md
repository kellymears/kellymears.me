**Capillary action** is the movement of a liquid into a narrow space, such as a thin tube, the gaps between fibers, or the pores in soil, often against gravity. Dip the corner of a paper towel in water and watch the water climb. The cause is a contest between two attractions: *cohesion*, liquid molecules pulling on each other, and *adhesion*, liquid molecules pulling on the solid around them.

When adhesion wins, as with water in clean glass, the liquid spreads along the walls and its surface curves up at the edges into a concave *meniscus*. [[Surface Tension]] then acts like a stretched skin, pulling the rest of the column up behind the edges. The liquid rises until the weight of the lifted column balances that pull. When cohesion wins, as with mercury in glass, the meniscus curves down and the liquid is pushed below the surrounding level instead.

The height reached follows a simple rule, known as Jurin's law: it is inversely proportional to the width of the gap. Halve the tube's diameter and the water climbs twice as high. In a tube one millimeter across, water rises a few centimeters; in a tenth of a millimeter, tens of centimeters. That is why capillary effects matter in fine materials and vanish in wide containers.

Capillary action is everywhere once you look:

- **Wicks.** A candle wick draws melted wax up to the flame, where it vaporizes and burns, the fuel supply for [[Combustion]]. Oil lamps and fountain pens work the same way.
- **Fabric.** Moisture-wicking sportswear uses fine synthetic fibers that pull sweat away from the skin and spread it over a large outer surface, where it evaporates faster. The cooling comes from [[Latent Heat]], and helps [[Thermoregulation]] during hard efforts. Cotton absorbs water but holds it, which is why it stays heavy and cold when wet.
- **Soil and plants.** Water spreads through soil pores by capillarity. In trees, capillarity alone can lift water only a short way; most of the lift comes from evaporation at the leaves pulling a continuous column of water upward, a process that relies on water's strong cohesion.
- **Buildings.** *Rising damp* is groundwater wicking up through porous brick and mortar, which is why walls get damp-proof barriers near the ground.

A related effect explains coffee rings. As a spilled drop dries, its edge stays pinned, and evaporation is fastest there, so liquid flows outward from the center to replace it, carrying dissolved particles to the rim. The *coffee ring effect* matters in inkjet printing, where it spoils even coats.

Capillary action relates to [[Osmosis]], which also moves water through fine structures, but by concentration differences rather than surface forces. A liquid's [[Viscosity]] sets how quickly it wicks, not how far.
