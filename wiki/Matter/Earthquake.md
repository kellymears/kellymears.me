**An earthquake** is a sudden shaking of the ground caused by rock slipping along a fault. Most occur where tectonic plates meet; see [[Plate Tectonics]]. The plates move steadily, but friction locks the rock along faults. Strain builds for years or centuries until the rock gives way and springs back, releasing stored energy in seconds; see [[Friction]] and [[Elasticity]]. This picture, called elastic rebound, was developed by Harry Fielding Reid after the 1906 San Francisco earthquake.

The released energy travels as **seismic waves**:

- **P waves**, or primary waves, compress and stretch rock along their direction of travel, like sound. They're fastest and arrive first; see [[Sound]].
- **S waves**, or secondary waves, shake rock side to side. They're slower and can't pass through liquid. Their absence on the far side of Earth revealed that the outer core is molten.
- **Surface waves** travel along the ground and usually cause the most damage.

Size is measured by magnitude. Charles Richter introduced a scale in 1935 for California earthquakes. Seismologists now mostly use the moment magnitude scale, which better captures large events. Both are logarithmic: each step of one magnitude means about 32 times more energy released; see [[Logarithmic Scale]]. Small earthquakes vastly outnumber large ones, following a power law; see [[Power law]]. The largest ever recorded, in Chile in 1960, had a magnitude of about 9.5.

Undersea earthquakes can lift or drop the seafloor and displace the water above it, generating a **tsunami**, a series of waves that cross oceans at airliner speeds and grow tall in shallow water. The 2011 Tōhoku earthquake in Japan, around magnitude 9, produced a tsunami that killed thousands and caused the Fukushima nuclear disaster.

Damage depends on more than magnitude. Loose, waterlogged soil can behave like a liquid during shaking, so buildings sink or topple. Buildings sway, and if their natural frequency matches the shaking they amplify it; see [[Resonance]]. Engineering responses include flexible steel frames, base isolators that decouple a building from the ground, and tuned mass dampers; see [[Steel]] and [[Margin of Safety]].

Earthquakes can't be predicted in time and place, but their probabilities can be estimated, and aftershocks follow statistical patterns. **Early warning** systems exploit the gap between fast P waves and destructive S waves. Sensors near the epicenter detect the first waves and send alerts that outrun the shaking, giving seconds to tens of seconds to stop trains and take cover. Some phone systems use their built-in accelerometers as a crowd-sourced sensor network; see [[Inertial Measurement Unit]].

Earthquakes also affect infrastructure far away. A 2006 earthquake off Taiwan triggered undersea landslides that cut several submarine cables, disrupting internet traffic across East Asia; see [[Submarine Cable]] and [[Cascading Failure]].
