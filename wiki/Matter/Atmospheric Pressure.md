**Atmospheric pressure** is the force exerted by the weight of air above a surface. At sea level, the column of air over every square centimeter weighs about one kilogram, a pressure of roughly 101 kilopascals, or 14.7 pounds per square inch. We don't feel it because it presses equally from all directions, and the fluids in our bodies push back equally; see [[Pascal's Principle]].

Evangelista Torricelli showed in 1643 that the atmosphere has weight. He filled a glass tube with mercury and upended it in a dish; the mercury fell to about 76 centimeters and stopped, held up by air pushing on the dish. That was the first **barometer**, and its height rose and fell with the weather. Blaise Pascal had one carried up a mountain and found the column shorter at the top, proving the pressure came from the air overhead.

Pressure falls with **altitude**, roughly halving every 5.5 kilometers, because there's less air above. The air also becomes less dense, since gas density depends on pressure; see [[Ideal Gas Law]]. The consequences:

- **Breathing.** At altitude, each breath contains fewer oxygen molecules, though the proportion is unchanged at 21 percent. Less pressure pushes less oxygen into the blood, so endurance drops and people acclimatize by producing more red blood cells; see [[Altitude Training]] and [[Diffusion]].
- **Cooking.** Water boils at a lower temperature when pressure is lower, about 90 °C at 3,000 meters, so pasta and beans take longer and baking recipes need adjustment. Pressure cookers raise pressure to boil water at about 120 °C, cooking far faster; see [[Boiling Point]].
- **Speed.** Thinner air means less [[Aerodynamic Drag]], which is why cycling hour records and sprint times are often set at altitude, though the lack of oxygen limits endurance.
- **Sealed things.** Bags of chips puff up on mountains and planes, and ears pop as pressure changes. Airliners pressurize their cabins to the equivalent of about 2,000 meters.

Pressure also drives weather. Air flows from high to low pressure, making wind. High-pressure systems bring sinking air and clear skies; low-pressure systems bring rising air, which cools until its moisture condenses into clouds and rain; see [[Dew Point]]. A falling barometer signals an approaching storm, and some people feel pressure changes as headaches or aching joints.

Pressure differences do a lot of work. Drinking through a straw lowers pressure in your mouth so atmospheric pressure pushes liquid up. Suction cups, vacuum cleaners, and siphons work the same way. Air pressure differences over a wing create lift; see [[Bernoulli's Principle]].

Gauges usually read pressure relative to the atmosphere, so a bicycle tire at 80 psi holds about 95 psi absolute; tire pressure trades comfort against [[Rolling Resistance]]. Divers and engineers must track which reading they mean.
