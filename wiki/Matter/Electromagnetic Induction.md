**Electromagnetic induction** is the production of a voltage in a conductor by a changing magnetic field. Move a magnet near a coil of wire, or change the current in a neighboring coil, and a voltage appears in the coil; if the circuit is closed, current flows. Michael Faraday discovered it in 1831, and James Clerk Maxwell later folded it into his equations of electromagnetism, which also showed that light is an electromagnetic wave, as in [[Polarized Light]].

*Faraday's law* says the induced voltage depends on how fast the magnetic field through the loop changes. A steady field does nothing; only change counts. *Lenz's law* adds the direction: the induced current flows so as to oppose the change that caused it. Drop a magnet through a copper pipe and it falls slowly, braked by the currents it induces, because the energy has to come from somewhere, as required by [[Conservation of Energy]].

Induction runs much of modern life:

- **Generators.** Nearly all grid electricity comes from turbines spinning magnets past coils, whether driven by steam, water, or wind.
- **Transformers.** Alternating current in one coil creates a changing field that induces voltage in a second coil, stepping voltage up or down according to the ratio of turns. Transformers work only with changing current, a major reason grids settled on alternating current in the 1880s and 1890s. High transmission voltage cuts losses, as explained under [[Ohm's Law]].
- **Motors.** Motors run the same physics backward. A spinning motor also acts as a generator, producing a *back voltage* that limits its speed, and electric vehicles use this for regenerative braking.
- **Induction cooktops.** A coil under the glass carries rapidly alternating current, and the changing field induces swirling *eddy currents* in the pan's base, heating the pan directly. Response is instant and most of the energy reaches the food rather than the room. Pans must be ferromagnetic, which is why a magnet test tells you whether cookware will work. Searing heat from induction drives the [[Maillard Reaction]] like gas does.
- **Wireless charging.** Phone chargers use paired coils, often tuned to the same frequency, a use of [[Resonance]], to transfer power across a small gap to a [[Lithium-Ion Battery]].

Eddy currents also brake without contact. Roller coasters, some trains, and many indoor bike trainers use magnets moving past metal to create resistance that rises with speed. In transformers and motors, unwanted eddy currents waste energy as heat, so their cores are built from thin insulated sheets that break up the current loops.

Guitar pickups, metal detectors, and contactless cards rely on induction too. The heat these currents produce is ordinary resistive heating, and getting rid of it is a [[Heat Transfer]] problem.
