A **hydrogen bond** is an attraction between a hydrogen atom attached to a strongly electron-hungry atom, usually oxygen or nitrogen, and a lone pair of electrons on another such atom nearby. The oxygen or nitrogen pulls the shared electrons toward itself, leaving the hydrogen with a slight positive charge, which is drawn to the slight negative charge on a neighbor. A hydrogen bond is much weaker than the covalent bonds that hold a molecule together, typically a few percent of their strength, but there are so many of them that they shape much of chemistry and biology.

Water is the main example. Each molecule can form up to four hydrogen bonds with its neighbors, and the network explains several of water's oddities:

- **High boiling point.** Hydrogen sulfide, a heavier molecule of similar shape, boils at about −60 °C. Water boils at 100 °C because molecules must break free of their neighbors to escape; see [[Boiling Point]].
- **Floating ice.** In ice the bonds lock molecules into an open hexagonal lattice that takes more room than the liquid. Liquid water is densest at about 4 °C, so lakes freeze from the top down; see [[Phase Transition]] and [[Thermal Expansion]].
- **Heat storage.** Warming water or evaporating it means breaking bonds, so water absorbs a lot of heat for each degree and even more to turn to vapor. Coastal climates stay mild and sweat cools skin for this reason; see [[Specific Heat Capacity]] and [[Latent Heat]].
- **Surface tension.** Molecules at the surface are pulled inward by bonds they can't form outward, making a skin strong enough to hold up a water strider; see [[Surface Tension]] and [[Capillary Action]].

In living things, hydrogen bonds hold structures that must be stable but not permanent. The two strands of DNA are joined by hydrogen bonds between paired bases, two for each A–T pair and three for each G–C pair. That is strong enough to keep the molecule intact but weak enough for enzymes to unzip it for copying. Proteins fold into helices and sheets held by hydrogen bonds, and cooking breaks those bonds as an egg white turns opaque and firm; see [[Protein Denaturation]].

The kitchen is full of them. Starch granules swell when hot water breaks the bonds between starch chains, which is how a sauce thickens; see [[Starch Gelatinization]]. Dough becomes elastic partly through hydrogen bonds in its gluten network; see [[Gluten]].

Nylon and Kevlar owe much of their strength to hydrogen bonds between neighboring chains; see [[Polymerization]]. Dissolving is often a matter of hydrogen bonding as well: sugar and alcohol mix into water because they can join its network, while oils can't, which is why they separate; see [[Emulsion]].
