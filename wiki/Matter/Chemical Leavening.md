**Chemical leavening** makes baked goods rise by producing carbon dioxide gas inside the batter or dough, without the hours yeast needs. Its two workhorses are baking soda and baking powder, and they are not interchangeable.

**Baking soda** is sodium bicarbonate, a base. On its own, heated, it releases only part of its gas and leaves behind sodium carbonate, which tastes soapy and metallic. Paired with an acid, it reacts completely, releasing carbon dioxide and leaving a neutral salt. So recipes that use baking soda also contain an acidic ingredient: buttermilk, yogurt, lemon juice, brown sugar, honey, or natural cocoa. The reaction starts the moment the two meet in water, so batter made with soda alone should be baked promptly before the bubbles escape.

**Baking powder** packages soda with its own dry acid, plus starch to absorb moisture and keep the two apart in the tin. Most is *double-acting*: one acid reacts as soon as the batter is mixed, and a second, slower acid reacts only when heated in the oven. That second burst lifts the batter just as it begins to set.

Chemical leaveners don't create the bubbles in a cake. Gas from the reaction diffuses into air pockets that already exist, beaten in when butter and sugar were creamed together or eggs whipped, and inflates them. Without those seed pockets, gas has nowhere to collect, the same need for a starting site that governs [[Nucleation]]. Heat then expands the gas, as the [[Ideal Gas Law]] predicts, and turns water into steam, itself a powerful leavener; popovers and puff pastry rise on steam alone.

The structure has to hold the gas and then set around it. In cakes and muffins, egg proteins undergo [[Protein Denaturation]] and starch swells through [[Starch Gelatinization]], fixing the foam into a crumb. In biscuits and scones, a little [[Gluten]] adds strength, though overmixing builds too much and makes them tough.

The balance of soda and acid also shifts [[pH]], which changes more than rise:

- **Color.** Alkaline batter browns faster and darker, because the [[Maillard Reaction]] speeds up as pH rises. A pinch of extra soda deepens cookie color; pretzels get their mahogany crust from a dip in a strongly alkaline bath.
- **Flavor.** Excess soda tastes bitter and soapy. Too much acid tastes sour.
- **Texture.** Alkaline dough spreads more, so soda-heavy cookies come out thinner and crisper.

Too much leavening is a classic failure. The bubbles grow too large, merge, and rise to the top, so the cake domes, then collapses as it cools, leaving a coarse, sunken crumb. At high altitude, lower air pressure makes bubbles expand more and water boil at a lower [[Boiling Point]], so recipes reduce leavening and add structure.

Yeast leavening is the slower, biological alternative, covered under [[Fermentation]].
