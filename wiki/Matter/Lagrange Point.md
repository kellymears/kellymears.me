**Lagrange points** are five positions near two large orbiting bodies, such as the Sun and Earth, where a small object can move in step with them. At these points the gravity of the two bodies, combined with the motion of the rotating system, balances so that the small object stays in the same position relative to both; see [[Gravity]] and [[Centripetal Force]].

They come from the **three-body problem**, the challenge of predicting how three objects move under their mutual gravity. Unlike the two-body case, it has no general closed-form solution, and its motions can be chaotic, sensitive to tiny differences in starting conditions; see [[Determinism]]. Leonhard Euler found three special solutions in 1767, and Joseph-Louis Lagrange found two more in 1772, for the case where the third object is too small to matter.

For the Sun and Earth:

- **L1** lies about 1.5 million kilometers sunward of Earth. A spacecraft there has an uninterrupted view of the Sun. The SOHO solar observatory has worked there since 1996, and space weather monitors watch the solar wind from there; see [[Aurora]].
- **L2** lies the same distance on the far side of Earth. Earth, Moon, and Sun all sit in roughly the same direction, so a single shield can block their heat and light. The James Webb Space Telescope arrived there in early 2022 to observe in infrared, which requires extreme cold; see [[Black-Body Radiation]].
- **L3** lies on the far side of the Sun, permanently hidden from Earth.
- **L4 and L5** lead and trail Earth by 60 degrees along its orbit, each forming an equilateral triangle with the two bodies.

L1, L2, and L3 are unstable equilibria, like a ball balanced on a hilltop. A spacecraft that drifts away keeps drifting, so missions there fly large looping paths called halo orbits around the point and fire thrusters periodically. L2's distance from Earth also means repair missions are impractical. The Hubble Space Telescope, in low Earth orbit, could be serviced by astronauts; Webb could not, which made testing on the ground critical; see [[Hubble Mirror Flaw]].

L4 and L5 are stable, provided one body is much more massive than the other. An object nudged away tends to circle the point rather than leave, thanks partly to the deflection that motion in a rotating frame produces; see [[Coriolis Effect]]. Material collects there. Jupiter shares its orbit with thousands of Trojan asteroids clustered at its L4 and L5 points.

Lagrange points also make fuel-efficient routes. Low-energy pathways connecting them, sometimes called the interplanetary transport network, let spacecraft drift between regions with little propulsion, at the cost of long travel times; see [[Escape Velocity]] and [[Orbit]].

From L1, the Moon sometimes crosses Earth's face, revealing its far side; see [[Eclipse]].
