The **uncertainty principle** is a basic limit in quantum mechanics: certain pairs of properties of a particle, most famously its position and its momentum, can't both have precise values at the same time. The more sharply one is defined, the more spread out the other must be. Werner Heisenberg stated the idea in 1927, and it's often called the **Heisenberg uncertainty principle**.

The limit has a precise form. The spread in position multiplied by the spread in momentum can't be smaller than a fixed amount set by Planck's constant, a very small number. For everyday objects the limit is far below anything measurable, which is why a thrown ball seems to have an exact place and speed; see [[Newton's Laws of Motion]]. For an electron in an atom, it's decisive.

The principle is often explained as a measurement disturbance: to see an electron, you must hit it with light, and the light knocks it off course; see [[Photon]]. Heisenberg's own early explanation took this form, but it's misleading. The limit isn't about clumsy instruments. In quantum mechanics a particle is described by a wave, and a wave can't be both sharply localized and have a single definite wavelength, which corresponds to momentum. A short pulse is made of many frequencies, and a pure tone must go on for a long time. The same trade-off holds for any wave, including sound and radio signals; see [[Fourier Transform]] and [[Wave Interference]].

The trade-off has real consequences. It explains why electrons don't spiral into the nucleus: confining one too tightly would give it so much momentum that its energy rises, so atoms settle at a balance and have a definite size. It's why matter at absolute zero still has some residual motion; see [[Absolute Zero]]. Particles squeezed into a tiny region push back, which helps hold up collapsed stars; see [[Neutron Star]]. And the spread of a particle's wave lets it sometimes appear on the far side of a barrier it shouldn't be able to cross; see [[Quantum Tunneling]].

A related relation links energy and time. Very short-lived states have poorly defined energies, which broadens the lines in the light atoms emit; see [[Spectroscopy]]. Lasers face the same tension, since an extremely short pulse necessarily contains a broad range of colors; see [[Laser]].

The phrase has drifted far from physics. It's often used loosely to mean that observing anything changes it. That idea has its own names elsewhere, from people changing their behavior when they know they're being studied to software bugs that vanish when examined; see [[Hawthorne Effect]] and [[Heisenbug]]. Those are disturbance effects. The uncertainty principle would hold even with perfect instruments.
