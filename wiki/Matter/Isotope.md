**Isotopes** are versions of the same chemical element that have different numbers of neutrons in their nuclei. An element is defined by its number of protons: every carbon atom has six. But a carbon atom may carry six, seven or eight neutrons, giving carbon-12, carbon-13 and carbon-14. The name, coined by the chemist Frederick Soddy in 1913, means "same place," since all of them occupy the same spot on the periodic table.

Because chemistry is mostly about electrons, and the number of electrons follows the number of protons, isotopes of an element behave almost identically in reactions. They differ in two ways. They have different masses, which matters for any process sensitive to weight, and they have different nuclear stability. Most elements have one or more **stable isotopes**; others are radioactive, and many elements have both; see [[Radioactive Decay]].

Hydrogen shows the mass effect most strongly, because adding a neutron to a single proton doubles the weight. **Deuterium**, hydrogen with one neutron, makes up about one in every 6,400 hydrogen atoms. Water made with it, **heavy water**, is about 11 percent denser than ordinary water; an ice cube of it sinks. Heavy water slows neutrons without absorbing many of them, which made it valuable to early nuclear reactor programs, and Allied sabotage of a Norwegian heavy water plant during the Second World War targeted that supply; see [[Nuclear Fission]]. Deuterium and the radioactive tritium are also the fuel of most fusion designs; see [[Nuclear Fusion]].

Separating isotopes is hard precisely because they're chemically the same. Natural uranium is only about 0.7 percent uranium-235, the isotope that sustains a chain reaction. Enriching it relies on the tiny mass difference, usually by spinning uranium gas in long cascades of fast centrifuges; see [[Centripetal Force]].

Mass differences also leave a record in nature. Water molecules containing the heavier oxygen-18 evaporate slightly less readily and condense slightly more readily than ordinary water, so the ratio of oxygen isotopes in polar ice and ocean sediments tracks past temperatures. Measuring such ratios requires separating ions by mass; see [[Spectroscopy]]. Carbon-14, produced continuously in the atmosphere by cosmic rays, dates once-living material; see [[Cosmic Ray]].

Radioactive isotopes are tools in medicine and industry. Technetium-99m is the most widely used tracer in medical imaging, and positron emitters such as fluorine-18 make PET scans possible; see [[Antimatter]] and [[Ionizing Radiation]]. The international definition of the second rests on a transition in the stable isotope cesium-133; see [[Atomic Clock]].

Most isotopes on Earth were made in stars and stellar explosions, and the lightest, hydrogen, deuterium and helium, in the first minutes after the Big Bang; see [[Supernova]] and [[Big Bang]].
