A **siphon** is a tube that carries liquid from a container, up over its rim or some other obstacle, and down to an outlet lower than the liquid's surface, with no pump. Once the tube is full, flow continues on its own until the source empties to the level of the tube's end, or the outlet's level is reached. It is how fish tanks are drained.

The driving force is gravity acting on the height difference. The liquid in the longer, outgoing leg falls further than the liquid in the shorter, rising leg, and the falling column draws the rest along behind it. Ignoring friction, the outflow speed is the speed of an object dropped from the source's surface to the outlet, as energy conservation predicts; see [[Gravity]], [[Bernoulli's Principle]], and [[Conservation of Energy]].

What holds the liquid together on the way up is the harder question. The usual textbook answer is that air pressure on the source's surface pushes liquid up the rising leg, the same pressure that holds up a column in a barometer. That explains the siphon's limit: at sea level, air pressure can support about 10 meters of water. Above that height, the pressure at the top of the tube falls to the point where water boils at room temperature, bubbles form, and the column breaks; see [[Atmospheric Pressure]], [[Boiling Point]], and [[Cavitation]].

But air pressure isn't the whole story. Water molecules cling to each other, and a narrow tube of gas-free water can hold together under tension. Siphons have been run in low-pressure chambers, where air pressure alone couldn't explain them. In 2010 the physicist Stephen Hughes pointed out that the Oxford English Dictionary's definition credited air pressure alone. Both explanations describe real effects, and which dominates depends on the conditions; see [[Hydrogen Bond]] and [[Surface Tension]].

Siphons appear in more places than fish tanks:

- **Toilets.** Many toilet bowls flush by siphon: the rush of water fills the curved drain passage, which then pulls the bowl's contents out until air breaks the flow with a gurgle.
- **Pythagorean cup.** An ancient novelty cup with a hidden siphon in its center column. Filled moderately, it holds its drink; filled past a line, it drains completely into the drinker's lap.

An **inverted siphon** is a misnomer. Roman aqueducts and modern sewers carry water across valleys in sealed U-shaped pipes that dip down and rise again. They work by water pressure, not by the siphon's pull, so they have no height limit; see [[Pascal's Principle]]. The vacuum coffee maker, often called a siphon brewer, isn't one either: vapor pressure pushes the water up.
