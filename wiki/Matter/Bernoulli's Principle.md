**Bernoulli's principle** says that where a fluid flows faster, its pressure is lower. Daniel Bernoulli published it in 1738 in *Hydrodynamica*. It is really [[Conservation of Energy]] applied to a moving fluid: along a streamline, the energy carried by pressure, by motion, and by height adds up to a constant, so if the fluid speeds up, its pressure must drop to compensate.

The **Venturi effect** is the clearest demonstration. When fluid flows through a constriction, it must speed up to get the same volume through a narrower space, and the pressure in the narrow section falls. This low pressure can draw in a second fluid:

- **Carburetors** used it to pull fuel into the air flowing into an engine.
- **Perfume atomizers and paint sprayers** draw liquid up a tube into a fast stream of air.
- **Lab aspirators** create suction from running tap water.

A **Pitot tube** uses the principle in reverse to measure speed. It compares the pressure of air rammed into a forward-facing opening with the static pressure of the surrounding air; the difference gives airspeed. Every aircraft carries them, and blocked Pitot tubes have caused crashes when pilots received false speed readings.

The principle is often used to explain how wings produce **lift**, usually with a story that is wrong. The *equal transit time* explanation says air going over the curved top must travel farther and so must go faster to rejoin the air beneath at the trailing edge. But nothing requires the two streams to meet, and in fact air over the top arrives well before the air beneath. Flat and symmetric wings, and planes flying upside down, all produce lift too.

The fuller account is that a wing, by its shape and angle, turns a large mass of air downward. By [[Newton's Laws of Motion]], pushing air down pushes the wing up. The pressure difference, lower above and higher below, is real and related by Bernoulli's equation to the different flow speeds, so the principle is part of the picture. What's wrong is the invented reason for the speed difference.

Bernoulli's equation in its simple form assumes a fluid that is steady, incompressible, and free of [[Viscosity]]. Real flows break these assumptions. Viscosity causes energy losses in pipes and creates the boundary layers where flow separates from a surface, which produces the wake behind a cyclist and much of [[Aerodynamic Drag]]. The low-pressure pocket in that wake is part of what makes [[Drafting]] work. Fast-moving air is also less able to hold up a surface than intuition suggests, which is why a gust can lift a roof.

Static fluids follow simpler rules: pressure spreads evenly through an enclosed fluid, as in [[Pascal's Principle]], and pushes up on submerged objects, as described under [[Buoyancy]].
