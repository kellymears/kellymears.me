**Quantum entanglement** is a link between particles in which their properties can't be described independently, however far apart they are. Measure one member of an entangled pair and the result is random, but it will be correlated with a measurement on the other in a way no ordinary shared information can explain. Albert Einstein, uneasy with the idea, called it "spooky action at a distance."

Entanglement arises when particles interact or are created together. Two photons produced by the same process can share a polarization, so that when both are measured along the same direction they always agree, though each result on its own is a coin toss; see [[Photon]] and [[Polarized Light]]. Until measured, neither photon has a definite polarization of its own. Only the pair's relationship is fixed.

In 1935, Einstein, Boris Podolsky, and Nathan Rosen argued that this showed quantum mechanics was incomplete. The correlations, they suggested, must come from hidden properties set when the particles parted, like a pair of gloves mailed in separate boxes: opening one box and finding a left glove tells you the other is a right, with nothing spooky involved. This became known as the **EPR paradox**.

In 1964, John Bell showed the question could be tested. Any theory in which particles carry predetermined answers, and no influence travels faster than light, must keep certain statistical correlations below a limit. Quantum mechanics predicts that entangled particles exceed it. Experiments from the 1970s onward, culminating in 2015 tests that closed the main loopholes, found the limit broken just as quantum theory predicts, work recognized by the 2022 Nobel Prize in physics. **Bell's theorem** is often called one of the most profound results in science, since it rules out a whole class of explanations by experiment rather than argument; see [[Falsifiability]].

Entanglement doesn't allow faster-than-light messages. Each side sees only random results, and the correlation appears only when the two records are compared, which requires an ordinary signal. The limits set by relativity stay intact; see [[Time Dilation]].

It's also fragile. Any interaction with the surroundings spreads the correlation into countless particles and effectively destroys it, which is why everyday objects don't show quantum behavior and why quantum hardware is kept extremely cold and isolated; see [[Absolute Zero]].

Entanglement is now a resource for technology. Quantum computers rely on entangling many qubits; see [[Quantum Computing]]. Quantum key distribution uses entangled or carefully prepared photons to share secret keys, since eavesdropping disturbs the correlations and can be detected; see [[Public-Key Cryptography]] and [[One-Time Pad]]. Entangled photons have been shared between a satellite and ground stations more than 1,000 kilometers apart. Quantum measurements also serve as a source of truly random numbers; see [[Pseudorandom Number Generator]].
