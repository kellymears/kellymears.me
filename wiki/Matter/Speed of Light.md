Light in a vacuum travels at exactly 299,792,458 meters per second, about 300,000 kilometers per second. The number is exact because since 1983 the meter has been defined from it: a meter is the distance light travels in 1/299,792,458 of a second, with the second itself set by an atomic clock; see [[Atomic Clock]]. Physicists write the speed as **c**.

For most of history, light seemed to arrive instantly. In 1676 Ole Rømer noticed that eclipses of Jupiter's moon Io came early when Earth was near Jupiter and late when it was far away, and concluded that light takes minutes to cross the gap; see [[Eclipse]]. Later ground measurements timed a beam over a few kilometers with spinning toothed wheels or rotating mirrors.

Physicists then assumed light was a wave in an invisible medium, the ether, so its measured speed should change with Earth's motion through it. In 1887 Albert Michelson and Edward Morley split a beam, sent the halves at right angles, and recombined them to look for that shift in the interference pattern; see [[Wave Interference]]. They found none. In 1905 Einstein took the result as a principle: the speed of light is the same for every observer, however they move. Everything strange about special relativity follows, including slowed clocks, shortened lengths and the rule that nothing with mass can reach c; see [[Time Dilation]] and [[Inertial Frame of Reference]].

The same speed applies to every kind of electromagnetic wave, from radio to gamma rays, and to gravitational waves; see [[Electromagnetic Spectrum]] and [[Photon]].

In a material, light travels more slowly. The ratio is a substance's refractive index, about 1.33 for water and 1.5 for glass, and it's why lenses bend light; see [[Refraction]]. A charged particle can move through water faster than light does in water, though never faster than c. It then emits a cone of light, much as a jet makes a sonic boom. That's **Cherenkov radiation**, the blue glow in the pools around nuclear reactors; see [[Shock Wave]] and [[Nuclear Fission]].

At human scales c is enormous, but distances in space are bigger. Moonlight is about 1.3 seconds old, sunlight about eight minutes, and a radio command to Mars takes between three and twenty-two minutes. A **light-year**, the distance light covers in a year, is about 9.5 trillion kilometers. Looking far away therefore means looking back in time; see [[Big Bang]].

The limit matters on Earth too. Light in glass fiber covers roughly 200 kilometers per millisecond, so a transatlantic round trip can never take less than several tens of milliseconds, and satellite positioning works only because receivers correct for signal travel time to within nanoseconds; see [[Optical Fiber]] and [[Global Positioning System]].
