**Microwave heating** cooks food by making its molecules rotate. A microwave oven fills its metal box with electromagnetic waves at about 2.45 gigahertz. Water molecules are *polar*, slightly positive at one end and negative at the other, so the oscillating electric field twists them back and forth billions of times per second. Jostling against their neighbors, they turn that motion into heat. The general process is called **dielectric heating**, and it works on any material with polar molecules; fats and sugars heat too, though less strongly than water.

The oven was an accident. In 1945, Percy Spencer, an engineer working on radar equipment, noticed that a candy bar in his pocket had melted near a running magnetron, the tube that generates the microwaves. Early commercial ovens stood nearly six feet tall.

Several common beliefs about microwaves are wrong:

- **They don't cook from the inside out.** Microwaves penetrate one to a few centimeters, so the outer layer heats directly and the center heats by ordinary [[Heat Transfer|conduction]], which is why thick food needs standing time, a form of [[Carryover Cooking]].
- **The frequency isn't tuned to water.** 2.45 GHz was chosen because it is an unlicensed frequency band and penetrates food well, not because it matches a [[Resonance]] of water. Strong absorption would be worse, since the energy would all be absorbed at the surface.
- **They aren't nuclear.** Microwave radiation is non-ionizing, with too little energy per photon to break chemical bonds. Its only effect on tissue is heating, measured as the [[Specific Absorption Rate]].

The quirks of microwave cooking follow from the physics. Waves reflecting off the metal walls form a standing pattern of hot and cold spots, through the same [[Wave Interference]] that makes standing waves on a string, which is why ovens have turntables. Melt a plate of chocolate with the turntable removed and the gaps between melted spots reveal the wavelength, about 12 centimeters. Ice absorbs microwaves poorly, because its molecules can't rotate freely, so defrosting is uneven: a thawed patch absorbs much more and starts cooking while the rest stays frozen.

Surfaces don't brown, because evaporating water holds them near the [[Boiling Point]], far below the temperatures needed for the [[Maillard Reaction]]. Bread turns rubbery and then hard as its starch loses moisture fast. Water heated in a smooth cup can also become *superheated*, above its boiling point without boiling, because there are no rough spots for bubbles to form, the problem of [[Nucleation]]. A spoon or tea bag can trigger violent boiling.

The 2.4 GHz band is shared with [[Wi-Fi]] and Bluetooth, so a leaky microwave oven can disrupt nearby wireless connections while it runs. Induction cooktops heat differently, through currents set up in the pan by [[Electromagnetic Induction]].
