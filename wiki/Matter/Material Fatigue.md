**Material fatigue** is the weakening of a material under repeated loading, until it breaks at a stress far below what it could survive once. Bend a paperclip back and forth and it snaps within a few dozen cycles, though a single bend never breaks it. Every component that vibrates, rotates, or flexes, such as axles, wings, bicycle frames, bridges, and springs, is at risk, and fatigue causes most mechanical failures of structures in service.

It proceeds in three stages:

- **Initiation.** A microscopic crack starts at a weak point: a scratch, a sharp corner, a weld flaw, a bolt hole, or a corrosion pit. These *stress concentrations* multiply the local stress several times over what the rest of the part sees.
- **Growth.** Each load cycle opens the crack slightly and extends it a tiny amount. The fracture surface records the cycles as curved *beach marks*.
- **Final fracture.** When the remaining material can no longer carry the load, the part breaks suddenly, often with no visible deformation beforehand.

That last stage is what makes fatigue dangerous. A part can look perfect until it fails.

The study of fatigue began with railways. After an axle broke and derailed a train near Versailles in 1842, killing dozens, engineers investigated why axles that had passed every static test were cracking. August Wöhler, testing axles through millions of rotations in the 1850s and 1860s, produced the first **S-N curves**, plotting stress against the number of cycles to failure. He found that steel has an *endurance limit*: below a certain stress, it survives indefinitely. Aluminum has no such limit, so an aluminum part will eventually fail at any repeated stress, a fact that sets the design life of aircraft and lightweight bicycle frames.

The de Havilland Comet, the first jet airliner, made the lesson famous. Two Comets broke up in flight in 1954. Investigators pressurized a fuselage repeatedly in a water tank until it failed, and found cracks growing from the corner of a window opening in the roof, where stress concentrated. Aircraft windows have had rounded corners ever since, and pressurization cycles became a counted, inspected quantity.

Defenses follow from the mechanism. Designers avoid sharp corners, polish surfaces, and compress the surface layer by shot peening so cracks struggle to open. Operators inspect for cracks on a schedule and retire parts at a set number of cycles. [[Oxidation]] makes things worse, since corrosion pits start cracks, and [[Resonance]] can multiply cycle counts and amplitudes dramatically.

Fatigue has close relatives elsewhere. Chips wear out from [[Electromigration]], a slow accumulation of damage from current. Bone develops stress fractures from repeated impact faster than [[Bone Remodeling]] repairs it, and [[Repetitive Strain Injury]] follows a similar pattern of accumulated load in tendons.
