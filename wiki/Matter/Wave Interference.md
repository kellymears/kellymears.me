**Wave interference** is what happens when two or more waves overlap. At each point, their displacements add, a rule called *superposition*. Where crests align with crests, the waves reinforce each other, *constructive* interference. Where crests meet troughs, they cancel, *destructive* interference. Sound, light, water ripples, and radio all interfere, and the patterns that result are some of physics' most useful tools.

Thomas Young's double-slit experiment around 1801 used interference to show that light is a wave. Light passing through two narrow slits produced alternating bright and dark bands on a screen, impossible for particles but exactly what overlapping waves do. Interference is closely related to [[Diffraction]], the spreading of waves around edges and through openings.

Interference in everyday life:

- **Noise-cancelling headphones.** Microphones sample incoming sound and the headphones play an inverted copy, crest for trough, so the two cancel. It works best on steady low frequencies such as engine drone, where the waves are long and predictable, and poorly on sudden or high sounds.
- **Soap bubbles and oil slicks.** Light reflects from both the top and bottom surfaces of a thin film. Depending on the film's thickness, certain colors interfere constructively and others destructively, producing swirling rainbows. Anti-reflective coatings on lenses and glasses use films a quarter-wavelength thick so reflections cancel.
- **Wi-Fi dead spots.** Radio signals reflect off walls and furniture, and reflected copies interfere with the direct signal. Moving a laptop a few centimeters can change reception noticeably.
- **Room acoustics.** Sound reflecting between parallel walls forms *standing waves*, patterns that stay fixed in place, causing booming bass in some spots and thin bass in others. These room modes are a form of [[Resonance]].
- **Beats.** Two notes at slightly different frequencies interfere to produce a pulsing loudness at the difference between them. Musicians tune by listening for beats to slow and vanish.

Precision measurement exploits interference. An interferometer splits a light beam, sends the halves along two paths, and recombines them; a change in one path's length as small as a fraction of a wavelength shifts the pattern. In 2015, LIGO's interferometers, with arms 4 kilometers long, detected gravitational waves by measuring changes in length about a thousandth the width of a proton.

Interference also relates to [[Polarized Light]], since waves polarized at right angles do not interfere, and to the [[Doppler Effect]], which shifts the frequencies being combined. In digital imaging, fine patterns sampled on a grid produce moiré, a visual cousin of interference covered under [[Aliasing]].

Colors produced by interference rather than pigment are called structural colors: the shimmer of peacock feathers, butterfly wings, and beetle shells comes from microscopic layers, and they do not fade the way dyes do.
