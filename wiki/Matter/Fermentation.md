**Fermentation**, in the strict biochemical sense, is a way of extracting energy from sugar without oxygen. Cells break glucose down partway and dump the remainder as a by-product, such as lactic acid or ethanol and carbon dioxide. In food, the word covers any transformation driven by microbes, which have been put to work for at least nine thousand years to make bread, beer, wine, cheese, yogurt, pickles, soy sauce, and much more. Louis Pasteur showed in the 1850s and 1860s that living microbes were responsible, ending the belief that fermentation was a purely chemical decay.

The main kinds:

- **Alcoholic fermentation** by yeasts turns sugar into ethanol and carbon dioxide. The gas raises bread, and the alcohol makes beer and wine.
- **Lactic acid fermentation** by lactic acid bacteria turns sugar into lactic acid, souring yogurt, sauerkraut, kimchi, sourdough, and salami. Human muscles do the same thing when demand outruns oxygen, which is the chemistry around the [[Lactate Threshold]].
- **Acetic fermentation** turns alcohol into vinegar. It actually uses oxygen, so it is fermentation only in the culinary sense.
- **Mold and koji fermentation** uses fungal enzymes to break proteins and starches down, producing the glutamates behind miso, soy sauce, and much of [[Umami]].

Fermentation preserves food by making it hostile to spoilage organisms. Acid lowers the pH below what most pathogens tolerate, alcohol is toxic to many microbes, and an established culture simply outcompetes newcomers for food. Salt steers the outcome. Brined vegetables favor salt-tolerant lactic acid bacteria while holding back spoilage organisms, and salt draws water out of the vegetables by [[Osmosis]] to form the brine in the first place. This is selection by environment: the cook sets conditions and lets the right microbes win.

Fermentation also transforms flavor. Proteins become savory amino acids, sugars become sour and complex, and new aromatic compounds appear. A sourdough starter is a stable community of yeasts and bacteria maintained by repeated feeding, often for decades, and shared by giving away a portion. Each starter is a fork of another, adapting to its new kitchen. Kombucha and kefir grains spread the same way.
