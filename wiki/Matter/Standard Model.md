The **Standard Model** of particle physics is the theory of what matter is made of at the smallest known scale and how those pieces interact. Assembled during the 1960s and 1970s, it has passed essentially every experimental test since, though physicists know it is incomplete.

It describes two families of matter particles:

- **Quarks**, which come in six types. Protons and neutrons are each made of three: a proton is two up quarks and a down quark, a neutron two downs and an up. Quarks are never found alone. The theory of quarks was proposed in 1964 by Murray Gell-Mann and independently by George Zweig; the heaviest, the top quark, was found only in 1995.
- **Leptons**, which include the electron, its heavier relatives the muon and tau, and three kinds of neutrino; see [[Neutrino]] and [[Cosmic Ray]].

Every matter particle has an antimatter partner; see [[Antimatter]].

Forces come from exchanging other particles. The **electromagnetic force**, responsible for light, chemistry and magnetism, is carried by the photon; see [[Photon]] and [[Magnetism]]. The **strong force**, carried by gluons, binds quarks into protons and neutrons and holds nuclei together. The **weak force**, carried by the W and Z bosons, changes one kind of quark into another; it drives some forms of radioactive decay and the first step of fusion in the Sun; see [[Radioactive Decay]] and [[Nuclear Fusion]].

The last piece was the **Higgs boson**. In 1964 Peter Higgs and several other physicists proposed a field filling all of space; particles that interact with it acquire mass. The field's particle was detected at the Large Hadron Collider in 2012; see [[Particle Accelerator]]. The Higgs explains the mass of fundamental particles, but most of the mass of a proton, and so of everyday matter, comes instead from the energy binding quarks together.

The model's precision is remarkable. Its prediction of the electron's magnetic strength agrees with measurement to better than one part in a billion, among the most accurate predictions in science.

Its gaps are just as notable:

- **Gravity** is missing. General relativity describes it well, but the two theories have never been combined; see [[Gravity]].
- **Dark matter** isn't any particle in the model; see [[Dark Matter]].
- **Neutrino masses** were originally set to zero, but experiments show they are tiny yet nonzero.
- It can't explain why the universe contains far more matter than antimatter; see [[Big Bang]].
- It contains about nineteen numbers, such as particle masses, that must be measured rather than derived, and offers no reason why matter comes in three near-copies, or generations.

Physicists search for cracks with ever more precise measurements and higher-energy collisions; see [[Uncertainty Principle]].
