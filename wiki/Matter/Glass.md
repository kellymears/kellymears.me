**Glass** is a solid whose atoms are arranged like a liquid's, randomly rather than in the orderly repeating pattern of a crystal. It's made by melting a material and cooling it fast enough that its atoms can't organize; see [[Nucleation]]. Window and bottle glass is mostly silica, the main ingredient of sand, with soda added to lower the melting point and lime to keep it from dissolving in water.

The trick is [[Viscosity]]. As molten silica cools, it thickens so steeply that its atoms lose the ability to move into a crystal arrangement. The temperature where it stops behaving as a very thick liquid and becomes rigid is the **glass transition**. It isn't a sharp melting point; glass softens gradually, which is what lets glassblowers shape it; see [[Phase Transition]].

A popular myth says old cathedral windows are thicker at the bottom because glass slowly flows. At room temperature it would take far longer than the age of the universe to sag visibly. The real reason is that old panes were spun into uneven discs, and glaziers usually set the thick edge down.

Glass is transparent for two reasons. It has no grain boundaries, the internal edges between crystals that scatter light and make most solids opaque. And its electrons can't absorb the energy of visible light, though they do absorb ultraviolet, which is why it's hard to tan through a window. Light passing through it slows and bends; see [[Refraction]]. Different colors bend by different amounts, the effect that lets a prism split white light; see [[Dispersion]]. The purest silica glass carries light for kilometers; see [[Optical Fiber]].

Glass is strong but brittle. Without crystals that can slip, nothing stops a crack once it starts, so a tiny surface scratch can break a pane. Manufacturers fight this by putting the surface under compression, which squeezes cracks shut:

- **Tempered glass** is heated, then its surfaces cooled quickly with air jets. It's several times stronger and shatters into small, blunt cubes; see [[Thermal Expansion]].
- **Chemically strengthened glass**, used on phone screens, swaps small sodium atoms at the surface for larger potassium ones, crowding the surface.
- **Laminated glass** sandwiches a plastic layer, so a broken windshield holds together.

Borosilicate glass, sold as Pyrex, expands so little when heated that it survives going from oven to counter. Stress frozen inside glass becomes visible as colored bands between two polarizing filters; see [[Polarized Light]].

Glass forms naturally too: volcanic obsidian, which made the sharpest stone tools, and fulgurites, fused when [[Lightning]] strikes sand. Some butterfly scales are colored by glassy nanostructures; see [[Structural Color]].
