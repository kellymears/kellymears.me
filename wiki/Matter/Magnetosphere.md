The **magnetosphere** is the region of space around Earth dominated by the planet's magnetic field. It acts as a shield. The Sun constantly blows out a stream of charged particles, the solar wind, at hundreds of kilometers per second, and most of it is deflected around the planet instead of striking the atmosphere; see [[Aurora]] and [[Plasma]].

The field originates in Earth's outer core, a layer of molten iron about 2,900 kilometers down. Heat escaping from the inner core keeps the liquid metal churning, and the planet's rotation organizes the flow into columns. Moving conductive fluid in a magnetic field generates electric currents, and those currents generate the field, a self-sustaining loop called the **geodynamo**; see [[Electromagnetic Induction]] and [[Magnetism]]. At the surface the field is weak, about a hundredth the strength of a fridge magnet, but it extends far into space.

The solar wind shapes it. On the day side, the field is compressed to about ten Earth radii, and a standing shock wave forms ahead of it where the wind abruptly slows; see [[Shock Wave]]. On the night side it stretches into a long magnetotail. Energy stored in the tail is released during substorms, sending particles down along field lines near the poles, where they light up the aurora.

Inside lie the **Van Allen belts**, two doughnut-shaped zones of trapped high-energy particles discovered in 1958 by the first American satellite, Explorer 1. Spacecraft passing through them need shielding, and satellites crossing a weak spot over the South Atlantic suffer more radiation-induced glitches than elsewhere; see [[Single-Event Upset]], [[Ionizing Radiation]] and [[Cosmic Ray]].

The field isn't fixed. Its magnetic poles wander, and the north magnetic pole has moved hundreds of kilometers in recent decades, which is why navigation systems update their field models every few years; see [[Inertial Measurement Unit]]. Over longer spans the whole field **reverses**, north and south swapping, at irregular intervals averaging a few hundred thousand years; the last was about 780,000 years ago. Lava records the field direction when it cools, and symmetric stripes of reversed magnetism on either side of mid-ocean ridges were key evidence for seafloor spreading; see [[Plate Tectonics]].

Strong solar storms compress and shake the magnetosphere, inducing currents in long conductors on the ground. In 1989 a geomagnetic storm collapsed the power grid in Quebec for about nine hours; see [[Alternating Current]].

Many animals sense the field. Migrating birds, sea turtles and salmon appear to use it for navigation, through mechanisms still debated. Mars lost most of its global field billions of years ago, and the solar wind is thought to have stripped away much of its atmosphere as a result; see [[Atmospheric Pressure]] and [[Hall Effect]].
