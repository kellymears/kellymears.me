A **neutrino** is an elementary particle with no electric charge and a mass so small it has never been measured directly. It feels only the weak force and gravity, so it almost never interacts with matter. Tens of billions of neutrinos from the Sun pass through each square centimeter of your body every second, day and night, since the Earth is no obstacle; see [[Standard Model]].

The particle began as a guess. In beta decay, a nucleus emits an electron, but the electrons came out with a spread of energies rather than one fixed value, as if energy were going missing; see [[Radioactive Decay]]. In 1930 Wolfgang Pauli proposed that an undetected neutral particle carried off the difference, rescuing the conservation of energy; see [[Conservation of Energy]]. Pauli worried it could never be found. In 1956 Clyde Cowan and Frederick Reines detected neutrinos streaming from a nuclear reactor, which produces them in vast numbers; see [[Nuclear Fission]].

Catching them requires enormous detectors, deep underground to block other particles. Super-Kamiokande in Japan is a tank of 50,000 tonnes of ultrapure water lined with light sensors; IceCube uses a cubic kilometer of Antarctic ice. When a neutrino does strike, it can knock out a fast charged particle whose faint Cherenkov glow the sensors catch; see [[Speed of Light]].

The Sun's fusion should produce a predictable flood of neutrinos; see [[Nuclear Fusion]]. Starting in the late 1960s, Ray Davis counted them with a tank of cleaning fluid in a South Dakota gold mine and found only about a third of the expected number. This **solar neutrino problem** lasted three decades. The answer was that neutrinos come in three types, or flavors, and **oscillate** between them as they travel, so many solar neutrinos arrive as types the early detectors couldn't see. Super-Kamiokande found oscillation in 1998 and the Sudbury Neutrino Observatory confirmed the solar case in 2001. Oscillation is possible only if neutrinos have mass, the first clear crack in the Standard Model, and the discovery won the 2015 Nobel Prize.

Neutrinos also carry news from violent events. When a star in a neighboring galaxy exploded in 1987, detectors on Earth caught about two dozen neutrinos a few hours before the light arrived, since neutrinos escape the collapsing core at once while light is trapped until the shock reaches the surface; see [[Supernova]] and [[Neutron Star]]. IceCube has traced high-energy neutrinos to distant galaxies, linking them to the origin of cosmic rays; see [[Cosmic Ray]].

Neutrinos were once proposed as the dark matter, but they are too light and fast to clump into galaxies; see [[Dark Matter]]. Physicists still don't know their masses or whether a neutrino is its own antiparticle; see [[Antimatter]].
