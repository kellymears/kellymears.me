An **emulsion** is a dispersion of droplets of one liquid inside another liquid it doesn't mix with, most often oil and water. Mayonnaise, milk, hollandaise, vinaigrette, and cream are oil droplets in water (*oil-in-water*). Butter is the reverse, water droplets in fat (*water-in-oil*). An emulsion looks and behaves like a single uniform substance, but on a microscopic scale it is two phases, and left alone they will eventually separate. Emulsions are not thermodynamically stable. They are slowed down.

Oil and water separate because their molecules attract their own kind more strongly than each other, so the boundary between them costs energy. That cost is the interfacial tension, the same force studied in [[Surface Tension]]. Droplets merge to reduce total boundary area, and the lighter oil rises, through [[Buoyancy]]. Three things hold an emulsion together:

- **Emulsifiers.** Molecules with one water-loving end and one oil-loving end sit at the droplet surfaces, lowering interfacial tension and keeping droplets from fusing. Egg yolk is rich in lecithin and proteins that do this, which is why it anchors mayonnaise and hollandaise. Mustard and honey help vinaigrettes.
- **Small droplets.** Vigorous whisking or blending breaks oil into fine droplets that rise more slowly and pack into a thick, creamy texture. The more oil is packed into small droplets, the stiffer the emulsion, which is why mayonnaise can stand up on a spoon.
- **A thick continuous phase.** High [[Viscosity]] slows droplet movement. Starches and gums are added to sauces for this reason.

Emulsions **break** in predictable ways: oil added faster than it can be broken into droplets, a temperature too cold for the fat or too hot for the emulsifier, or too little water for the amount of oil. Hollandaise is the textbook case. Overheat it and the egg proteins undergo [[Protein Denaturation]] and clump, and the butter separates out. A broken emulsion can usually be rescued by starting with a fresh emulsifier, such as a new yolk or a spoonful of water, and whisking the broken sauce back in slowly.

Beyond the kitchen, emulsions are everywhere: paint, cosmetics, salad dressing, crude oil spills, and the fat transport in blood, where lipoproteins play the emulsifier's role.
