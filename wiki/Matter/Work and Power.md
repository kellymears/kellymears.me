In physics, **work** is energy transferred when a force moves something: work equals force times the distance moved in the direction of the force. Lift a 1-kilogram book one meter and you do about 10 joules of work against gravity. Pushing against a wall that doesn't move does no work, however tiring.

**Power** is the rate of doing work, measured in watts, where one watt is one joule per second. The same climb up a flight of stairs takes the same work whether you walk or run, but running takes more power because it does the work faster. The unit honors James Watt, the 18th-century steam engine pioneer who defined *horsepower*, about 746 watts, to sell engines to mine owners.

The difference between energy and power causes constant confusion. A kilowatt is a rate; a kilowatt-hour is an amount of energy, one kilowatt sustained for an hour. Electricity bills charge for kilowatt-hours. Electrical power is voltage times current, as described under [[Ohm's Law]].

Cycling made power a household number among athletes. A **power meter** measures the [[Torque]] applied at the cranks or hub and multiplies it by how fast they turn. Because power is what the rider actually produces, it's a more direct measure of effort than speed, which depends on wind and slope, or heart rate, which lags and drifts. Riders train against their [[Functional Threshold Power]] and compare climbing ability in watts per kilogram, since lifting body weight uphill is work against gravity.

Where that power goes depends on speed:

- **On the flat**, most of it overcomes [[Aerodynamic Drag]]. Drag force rises with the square of speed, and power is force times speed, so the power needed rises with the *cube* of speed. Going 10 percent faster takes about a third more power, which is why [[Drafting]] saves so much.
- **Uphill**, most goes into lifting the rider and bike, so weight dominates.
- **At low speed**, [[Rolling Resistance]] and drivetrain losses make up a larger share.

A pleasing coincidence links a ride's energy to food. The human body converts food energy to mechanical work at about 20 to 25 percent efficiency, and one food calorie is 4.184 kilojoules, so the kilojoules a power meter reports roughly equal the calories burned. The rest becomes heat, which is why hard efforts overwhelm [[Thermoregulation]]. The fuel comes through the body's [[Energy Systems]].

Engines and motors are rated the same way: power is torque times rotational speed, which is why a [[Gear Ratio]] can trade one for the other without changing the power delivered, apart from friction losses. Work and power are bookkeeping for energy, which, by [[Conservation of Energy]], is never created or destroyed, only moved and converted.
