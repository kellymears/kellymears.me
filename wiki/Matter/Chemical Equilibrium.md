Many chemical reactions can run in both directions. Products can react to re-form the starting materials while the starting materials are still forming products. Left alone in a closed container, such a **reversible reaction** settles into **chemical equilibrium**, a state where the forward and backward reactions run at the same rate. The amounts of each substance then hold steady.

Equilibrium is **dynamic**, not static. Molecules keep reacting in both directions; the totals stop changing only because the two flows balance, like a crowded escalator going up and down at once. Where the balance lies depends on the reaction and the conditions. Some equilibria sit almost entirely on the product side, others barely move from the reactants. Thermodynamics sets the position, balancing the energy released by forming bonds against the entropy gained by spreading out; see [[Entropy]] and [[Conservation of Energy]].

A **catalyst** doesn't move the equilibrium. It speeds both directions equally, so equilibrium arrives sooner but at the same place; see [[Catalysis]].

In 1884 Henri Le Chatelier stated a rule of thumb for predicting shifts. **Le Chatelier's principle** says that if a system at equilibrium is disturbed, it shifts in the direction that partly counteracts the disturbance. Add more of a reactant and more product forms to use it up. Increase the pressure on a reaction between gases and it favors whichever side has fewer gas molecules; see [[Ideal Gas Law]]. Heat a reaction that gives off heat and it shifts backward, absorbing some.

The **Haber process** for making ammonia from nitrogen and hydrogen shows the trade-offs at industrial scale. Four gas molecules become two, so high pressure, around 200 atmospheres, pushes the equilibrium toward ammonia. The reaction releases heat, so lower temperatures favor ammonia too, but at low temperatures it's impossibly slow. Plants compromise at around 400 to 450 degrees Celsius with an iron catalyst and recycle unreacted gas. The fertilizer this enables is commonly estimated to feed around half of the world's population.

Equilibria are everywhere in daily life. A sealed bottle of soda holds carbon dioxide in balance between the gas above the liquid and the dissolved gas and carbonic acid within it; opening the bottle lowers the pressure and the balance shifts toward gas, so it fizzes; see [[Henry's Law]] and [[Solubility]]. Blood keeps its acidity within a narrow range through a bicarbonate buffer that shifts to absorb added acid or base; see [[pH]]. Hemoglobin loads oxygen in the lungs, where oxygen is plentiful, and releases it in working muscle, where it's scarce; see [[Altitude Sickness]].

Physical changes follow the same logic. A liquid in a sealed jar evaporates until vapor condenses back as fast as it leaves; see [[Boiling Point]] and [[Phase Transition]].
