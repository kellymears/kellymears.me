**Angular momentum** is the rotational counterpart of ordinary momentum: a measure of how much spinning motion an object carries. It depends on how fast the object rotates and on its *moment of inertia*, a measure of how its mass is distributed around the axis of rotation. Mass far from the axis counts for much more than mass near it. A hoop resists changes in spin far more than a solid disc of the same mass and size.

The key law is **conservation**: angular momentum stays constant unless a [[Torque]] acts from outside. Because it is the product of moment of inertia and rotation rate, changing one changes the other:

- **Figure skaters** start a spin with arms out, then pull them in. Their moment of inertia drops and their spin rate rises sharply, with no push from anything.
- **Divers and gymnasts** tuck to rotate faster and open out to slow down before landing.

A falling cat seems to break the rule, turning over in midair without anything to push against. It doesn't: by twisting its front and back halves separately, extending one pair of legs while tucking the other, it changes its orientation while its total angular momentum stays zero.

A spinning object also resists changes to the *direction* of its axis. That is the **gyroscopic effect**, and it is why a spinning top stays upright, why a thrown football is stable in a spiral, and why gyroscopes are used for navigation. Push sideways on a spinning gyroscope and it doesn't tip. It swings at right angles to the push, a motion called *precession*.

Bicycles are often explained by the gyroscopic effect of their wheels, but that turns out to be a minor factor. In 2011 a team led by Jodi Kooijman and Arend Schwab built a bicycle with counter-rotating wheels that cancelled the gyroscopic effect, and a front wheel arrangement with no trail, and it still balanced itself when pushed. Self-stability comes mainly from steering geometry and where the mass sits, including the [[Center of Mass]] of the front assembly. Riders balance by steering under their own mass, sensing tilt through the [[Vestibular System]] and [[Proprioception]]. The vestibular system's semicircular canals are themselves sensors of rotational acceleration.

Wheels carry rotational inertia as well as ordinary inertia, so lighter rims do take slightly less energy to speed up, though the effect is small next to [[Aerodynamic Drag]] and [[Rolling Resistance]] at steady speed. Physics simulations in games and the [[Inertial Frame of Reference]] used to describe them both have to account for rotation, and errors in angular momentum are a classic source of objects spinning wildly in a game engine.
