**Gravity** is the attraction between everything that has mass. It holds people to the ground, keeps the Moon circling Earth, raises the tides, and gathers gas into stars. It's by far the weakest of the four fundamental forces: a small magnet lifts a paperclip against the pull of the entire planet. It dominates at large scales because mass only adds up, while electric charges mostly cancel.

Isaac Newton described it in 1687 as a force between any two masses, proportional to each and falling off with the square of the distance between them; see [[Inverse-Square Law]]. The same rule explained a falling apple and the Moon's path, joining earthly and celestial physics for the first time; see [[Orbit]]. Henry Cavendish measured the strength of the force in 1798 with a torsion balance, which let scientists calculate Earth's mass.

Near Earth's surface, gravity accelerates everything at about 9.8 meters per second squared, regardless of mass. Galileo argued this, and on Apollo 15 in 1971 an astronaut dropped a hammer and a feather on the airless Moon, and they landed together. On Earth, air resistance makes the difference; see [[Terminal Velocity]]. **Weight** is the force gravity exerts on a mass, so a person's mass is the same on the Moon but their weight is a sixth. An object behaves as if gravity acts at a single point; see [[Center of Mass]].

Gravity also produces pressure. The weight of the air above creates [[Atmospheric Pressure]], and the weight of displaced water creates [[Buoyancy]]. Its uneven pull across a body stretches it, raising the ocean into bulges; see [[Tide]].

Albert Einstein's general relativity, in 1915, reframed gravity as the curvature of spacetime by mass and energy. Objects in free fall follow the straightest possible paths through curved spacetime, which is why falling feels like floating: astronauts on the International Space Station experience about 90% of surface gravity, but they and the station fall together; see [[Inertial Frame of Reference]]. The weightlessness confuses the inner ear and causes space sickness; see [[Vestibular System]]. Relativity also predicts that clocks run faster farther from a mass, an effect GPS must correct for, or positions would drift kilometers a day; see [[Global Positioning System]].

Games tune gravity for feel rather than accuracy. Platformers often use gravity several times stronger than real, with extra pull on the way down, so jumps feel snappy instead of floaty, and add grace periods to forgive late jumps; see [[Coyote Time]]. Physics engines apply it as a constant acceleration every step; see [[Rigid Body Dynamics]].
