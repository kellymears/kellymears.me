A **chemical element** is a substance made of atoms that all have the same number of protons in the nucleus, the atomic number. Hydrogen has one, carbon six, gold 79. Atoms of an element can carry different numbers of neutrons, but those variants, isotopes, behave almost the same chemically; see [[Isotope]]. There are 118 known elements, and the **periodic table** arranges them so their relationships show at a glance.

By the mid-nineteenth century chemists had noticed that properties recurred when elements were listed by atomic weight. In 1869 Dmitri Mendeleev published a table that took the pattern seriously. Where no known element fit, Mendeleev left a gap and predicted the missing element's weight, density and behavior. Gallium was found in 1875, scandium in 1879 and germanium in 1886, each closely matching the predictions, which convinced chemists the table reflected something real.

What that was became clear only with atomic physics. In 1913 Henry Moseley showed with X-ray measurements that the true ordering principle was atomic number, not weight, which fixed a few pairs that had seemed out of order. Quantum mechanics then explained the periodicity: electrons occupy shells at increasing energies, and an element's chemistry depends mostly on the outermost electrons; see [[Uncertainty Principle]] and [[Chemical Bond]].

The layout follows from that:

- **Rows**, called periods, correspond to filling a new shell.
- **Columns**, called groups, hold elements with the same arrangement of outer electrons. The alkali metals in the first column each have one outer electron to give away and react violently with water. The halogens next to the last column each need one more electron and are fiercely reactive too. The noble gases in the last column have complete shells and barely react at all; see [[Oxidation]].
- The diagonal boundary between metals and nonmetals runs past silicon and germanium, the elements used in chips; see [[Semiconductor]].

The table also records where elements come from. Hydrogen and helium were made in the first minutes after the [[Big Bang]]. Stars fuse them into heavier elements up to iron, and most heavier ones are forged in supernovae and in collisions of neutron stars; see [[Nuclear Fusion]], [[Supernova]] and [[Neutron Star]]. Helium was identified in the Sun's spectrum in 1868 before anyone found it on Earth; see [[Spectroscopy]].

Elements beyond uranium are almost all made artificially and are radioactive, many existing only for fractions of a second; see [[Radioactive Decay]]. The seventh row was completed when the last four elements were named in 2016. Some elements also take more than one solid form, such as carbon as diamond or graphite; see [[Allotrope]].
