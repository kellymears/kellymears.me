**Timbre** (pronounced TAM-ber) is the quality that makes two sounds of the same pitch and loudness sound different: a violin and a flute playing the same note, or two people saying the same vowel. Much of it comes from **overtones**, the higher frequencies that sound along with a note's main pitch.

A plucked string doesn't vibrate in only one way. It vibrates as a whole, which sets the pitch, called the **fundamental**, and at the same time in halves, thirds, quarters, and so on, each a [[Standing wave]] at two, three, or four times the fundamental's frequency. This ladder of whole-number multiples is the **harmonic series**. Every pitched instrument produces some mix of harmonics, and the mix is a large part of its sound; see [[Resonance]].

- A **clarinet** in its low register produces mostly odd harmonics, because its cylindrical tube acts as if closed at one end, giving a hollow, woody sound.
- A **flute** has few strong overtones and sounds close to a pure tone; see [[Simple Harmonic Motion]].
- A **violin** has a bright spectrum rich in high harmonics.
- **Bells and drums** produce overtones that aren't whole-number multiples, which is why many don't sound a clear pitch.

Piano strings are stiff, so their overtones run slightly sharp of the ideal ratios; see [[Elasticity]]. Tuners compensate by "stretching" octaves, setting high notes a little sharp so they agree with the overtones of the low ones.

How a sound starts matters as much as its spectrum. Cut off the first fraction of a second of a piano note and it becomes hard to recognize; the attack, with its brief burst of noise, carries much of an instrument's identity. Vowels are timbre too: the mouth and throat boost certain bands, called formants, whatever the voice's pitch.

Joseph Fourier's insight that any repeating wave can be built from sine waves makes timbre measurable. A spectrum analyzer splits a sound into its component frequencies, usually with the [[Fast Fourier transform]]; see [[Sound]]. Synthesizers work the other way, adding sine waves together or filtering a bright waveform. Digital ones must keep their harmonics below half the sample rate, or the excess folds back as harsh, unrelated tones; see [[Aliasing]].

The ear does a similar analysis mechanically, with each region of the cochlea tuned to a different frequency. Damage to the high-frequency regions dulls timbre first, which is why people with [[Hearing Loss]] often find speech loud enough but unclear.

Hearing differs from sight here. The eye reduces a whole spectrum of light to three signals, so very different spectra can look identical; see [[Metamerism]]. The ear keeps far more spectral detail, so two sounds with different overtones rarely sound the same. Lossy audio formats drop quiet overtones hidden behind louder ones; see [[Data Compression]].
