**Cosmic rays** are charged particles arriving from space at very high energies. Despite the name, they aren't rays of light. About 90 percent are protons, most of the rest are helium nuclei, and a small fraction are heavier nuclei and electrons. Their energies range from modest to far beyond anything human accelerators can reach.

Their discovery came from a puzzle. Early twentieth-century physicists found that electroscopes slowly lost their charge even when shielded, implying some radiation was ionizing the air. Most assumed it came from radioactive rocks. In 1912 Victor Hess carried instruments up in a balloon to over 5,000 meters and found the ionization increased with altitude, so the source had to be above; see [[Ionizing Radiation]]. Hess shared the 1936 Nobel Prize for it.

A primary cosmic ray rarely reaches the ground. It hits a nucleus in the upper atmosphere and shatters it, producing a cascade of secondary particles that spread into an **air shower**, sometimes covering many square kilometers. Many of the particles reaching sea level are **muons**, heavier cousins of the electron. Muons decay in about two microseconds, which should give them time to travel only a few hundred meters, yet they're created around 15 kilometers up and arrive in large numbers. Their clocks run slow because they move close to the speed of light, one of the clearest everyday confirmations of relativity; see [[Time Dilation]].

Cosmic rays have shaped physics. The positron, the first known antimatter, was found in cosmic ray tracks in 1932, followed by the muon and other new particles; see [[Antimatter]]. They also continuously produce carbon-14 in the atmosphere, which is what makes radiocarbon dating possible; see [[Isotope]] and [[Radioactive Decay]].

Where they come from remains partly open. Lower-energy cosmic rays come mainly from the Sun. Most of the rest are thought to be accelerated by the shock waves of supernova remnants within our galaxy; see [[Supernova]] and [[Shock Wave]]. The most energetic, such as a 1991 particle nicknamed the "Oh-My-God particle," carried as much energy as a well-thrown baseball, and probably came from outside the galaxy, possibly from regions around massive black holes; see [[Black Hole]].

Earth's magnetic field and atmosphere absorb most of the flux; see [[Magnetosphere]]. Airline crews receive a measurably higher radiation dose than people on the ground, and astronauts report seeing flashes of light when particles pass through their eyes.

Cosmic rays also reach computers. A secondary neutron striking a memory chip can flip a stored bit, an effect called a soft error. It's rare per chip but common across large data centers, which is why servers use error-correcting memory and spacecraft use radiation-hardened parts; see [[Single-Event Upset]] and [[Error-Correcting Code]]. An unexplained extra 4,096 votes in a 2003 Belgian election has often been attributed to one.
