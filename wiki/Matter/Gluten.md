**Gluten** is the stretchy protein network that forms when wheat flour is mixed with water. Flour contains two groups of proteins, glutenins and gliadins, that do little while dry. Hydrated and worked, they bond into long, tangled chains: glutenins provide elasticity, the spring-back, and gliadins provide extensibility, the ability to stretch without tearing. Together they make dough that traps gas and holds its shape, which is why wheat dominates bread.

Gluten development is the process of building and aligning that network. Kneading stretches and folds the proteins, organizing them into sheets. Bakers check progress with the **windowpane test**: a well-developed dough can be stretched thin enough to see light through without tearing.

Time can replace much of the work. Proteins bond on their own once hydrated, so a dough left to rest develops structure without being touched. The French baker Raymond Calvel promoted the *autolyse* in the 1970s: mixing flour and water and resting them before adding salt and yeast, which shortens kneading. Jim Lahey's no-knead bread, popularized in 2006, relies almost entirely on a long, wet, slow rise.

Bread rises because gluten traps gas. Yeast [[Fermentation]] produces carbon dioxide that inflates countless small pockets in the network. In the oven, the gas expands, the proteins set through [[Protein Denaturation]], and starch sets through [[Starch Gelatinization]], fixing the airy structure as the crumb. The browned crust comes from the [[Maillard Reaction]].

Bakers adjust gluten in many ways:

- **Flour choice.** Bread flour has about 12 to 14 percent protein for a strong network; cake flour has 7 to 9 percent for tenderness.
- **Salt.** Tightens the network and makes dough stronger and less sticky.
- **Fat.** Coats proteins and keeps them from linking, which is why it is called *shortening*: it shortens the gluten strands. Flaky pastry and tender cookies depend on it.
- **Water.** Wetter doughs develop more easily and give open, irregular crumb.
- **Mixing.** Muffin batter is stirred only until combined, because overmixing builds gluten and makes muffins tough and tunneled.

Rested dough also relaxes. Freshly worked gluten is tight and springs back, so pizza dough that resists stretching usually just needs a few minutes' rest, not more force.

Other grains behave differently. Rye has proteins that form only weak networks and relies on starches and gums, which is why rye breads are dense. Gluten-free baking substitutes binders such as xanthan gum and psyllium to imitate the structure.

For about 1 percent of people, gluten triggers celiac disease, an autoimmune reaction that damages the small intestine and causes [[Inflammation]]. Others report sensitivity without celiac disease, a condition still debated.

Gluten is a good example of [[Viscosity]] and elasticity combined, a viscoelastic material that flows slowly yet springs back.
