A **tropical cyclone** is a large, rotating storm that forms over warm ocean water, with a low-pressure center, a spiral of thunderstorms, and winds that can exceed 250 kilometers per hour. In the Atlantic and northeast Pacific, strong ones are called **hurricanes**; in the northwest Pacific, **typhoons**; and in the Indian Ocean and South Pacific, simply cyclones. They're the same phenomenon under different regional names.

A tropical cyclone is in effect a heat engine fueled by the ocean; see [[Heat Engine]]. Warm surface water, generally at least about 26 degrees Celsius, evaporates into the air. As that moist air rises and cools, the vapor condenses into cloud and rain, releasing the heat it absorbed when it evaporated; see [[Latent Heat]] and [[Dew Point]]. That heat warms the rising air so it rises faster, which lowers the pressure at the surface and draws in more moist air. The cycle can feed itself, a positive feedback that lets a loose cluster of storms grow into an organized system; see [[Feedback Loop]].

The rotation comes from Earth's spin. Air drawn toward the low-pressure center is deflected, so it spirals inward instead of flowing straight in, counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and clockwise in the Southern; see [[Coriolis Effect]]. The effect vanishes at the equator, which is why tropical cyclones almost never form within about five degrees of it. As air spirals inward it speeds up, the way water circles faster as it nears a drain; see [[Angular Momentum]].

At the center of a mature storm is the **eye**, a calm, often clear region tens of kilometers across, where air sinks. Around it, the eyewall holds the strongest winds and heaviest rain. Pressure in the eye can fall far below normal; see [[Atmospheric Pressure]].

The Atlantic's **Saffir-Simpson scale** rates hurricanes from 1 to 5 by sustained wind speed. Wind is only part of the danger. Storm surge, seawater pushed ashore by the wind and raised by the low pressure, has historically caused much of the death toll, and heavy rain can flood areas far inland; see [[Tide]]. Hurricane Katrina in 2005 did much of its damage through surge and failed levees in New Orleans.

Cyclones weaken over land or cold water, which cut off their supply of warm moisture. Forecasters now predict their tracks days ahead with steadily improving accuracy, running many simulations with slightly varied starting conditions to show the range of possible paths; see [[Superforecasting]]. Predicting sudden strengthening remains harder.

Warmer oceans give storms more energy to draw on, and research suggests a warming climate is making the strongest storms more common and wetter, though the total number may not rise; see [[Greenhouse Effect]]. Cyclones also move heat, carrying it from the tropics toward the poles.
