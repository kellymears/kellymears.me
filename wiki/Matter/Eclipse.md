**An eclipse** happens when one celestial body moves into the shadow of another. For people on Earth, the two that matter involve the Sun, Earth, and Moon lining up.

- **A solar eclipse** happens when the Moon passes between the Sun and Earth, casting its shadow on Earth's surface.
- **A lunar eclipse** happens when Earth passes between the Sun and the Moon, and the Moon moves into Earth's shadow.

Total solar eclipses depend on a coincidence. The Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon and about 400 times farther away, so the two appear nearly the same size in the sky. When the Moon covers the Sun exactly, the sky darkens to twilight in minutes, temperatures drop, and the Sun's faint outer atmosphere, the corona, becomes visible. Totality is visible only along a narrow path, often under 200 kilometers wide, and lasts at most about seven minutes. The Moon's orbit is elliptical, so when it's farther away it can't fully cover the Sun, leaving a bright ring in an annular eclipse; see [[Orbit]].

Eclipses don't happen every month because the Moon's orbit is tilted about five degrees relative to Earth's. The three bodies line up only when a new or full moon falls near one of the two points where the orbits cross. Eclipses recur in patterns. The **saros cycle**, about 18 years and 11 days, returns the Sun, Earth, and Moon to nearly the same geometry, and was known to Babylonian astronomers. Eclipses can be predicted centuries ahead.

During a total lunar eclipse the Moon doesn't vanish. It glows coppery red, lit by sunlight bent through Earth's atmosphere, which scatters away the blue light along the way; see [[Rayleigh Scattering]] and [[Refraction]]. In effect, the Moon is lit by every sunrise and sunset on Earth at once.

Eclipses have shaped science. In 1919 expeditions led by Arthur Eddington photographed stars near the eclipsed Sun and found their positions shifted by about the amount general relativity predicted for light bending around the Sun's mass, a result that made Einstein famous; see [[Gravity]]. Helium was first identified in 1868 from an unexplained line in the spectrum of the Sun's edge during an eclipse; see [[Spectroscopy]]. Records of ancient eclipses help measure how Earth's rotation has slowed over millennia, an effect of the tides; see [[Tide]].

Looking at the partially eclipsed Sun can burn the retina without pain, so viewers need certified filters or indirect methods such as pinhole projection; see [[Lens]]. Animals often behave as if night has fallen.

Eclipses have long carried meaning beyond astronomy, read as omens and woven into myth across cultures. Many people describe totality as one of the most moving experiences of their lives; see [[Awe]].
