A **heat engine** turns heat into work by letting it flow from something hot to something cold and capturing part of that flow as motion. Steam engines, car engines, jet engines, and the turbines in most power plants are all heat engines. They power most of the modern world, and every one of them wastes a large share of its fuel as heat by necessity rather than by bad design.

The pattern is the same in each. A working fluid, such as steam or a mix of air and burned fuel, is heated so it expands and pushes a piston or spins a turbine; see [[Ideal Gas Law]] and [[Combustion]]. Then it gives up its leftover heat to a colder place, the condenser, radiator, or exhaust, and the cycle repeats.

In 1824 the French engineer Sadi Carnot showed that no engine can turn all its heat into work. The best possible efficiency depends only on the two temperatures: one minus the cold temperature divided by the hot, both measured from absolute zero. An engine running between 600 kelvins and 300 kelvins can never exceed 50%. The rest must flow into the cold side. This limit follows from the second law of thermodynamics, since turning heat entirely into work would decrease [[Entropy]].

Real engines fall short of Carnot's limit because of friction, heat leaks, and practical cycles:

- **Car engines** turn about 25 to 30% of their fuel into motion in typical driving; the rest leaves through the radiator and exhaust.
- **Coal plants** reach around 35 to 40%; see [[Work and Power]].
- **Combined-cycle gas plants** reach about 60% by running a gas turbine and then using its hot exhaust to raise steam for a second turbine.

The history runs from Thomas Newcomen's pumping engine of 1712, through James Watt's separate condenser in 1769, which greatly raised efficiency, to Nikolaus Otto's four-stroke engine of 1876 and Rudolf Diesel's compression-ignition engine of the 1890s. Steam engines use boiling and condensing water, which carry heat through [[Latent Heat]].

Run backward, the same cycle moves heat from cold to hot using work. That's a refrigerator or heat pump, which can move three or four times more heat than the electricity it uses; see [[Vapor-Compression Refrigeration]].

Not every engine is a heat engine. An electric motor converts electrical energy to motion directly, without a hot and cold side, so it avoids Carnot's limit and exceeds 90% efficiency; see [[Electric Motor]]. Muscles also work chemically rather than thermally, at roughly 20 to 25% efficiency, which is why exercise makes a body hot; see [[Energy Systems]].
