**Concrete** is a mixture of cement, water and aggregate, meaning sand and gravel or crushed stone. Poured as a thick slurry, it fills any mold and sets into artificial rock. By volume it's the most used manufactured material in the world, and after water perhaps the most consumed substance of any kind.

Concrete doesn't harden by drying out. The cement reacts chemically with water in a process called **hydration**, growing interlocking crystals that bind the aggregate together. That's why concrete can set underwater, and why keeping fresh concrete moist makes it stronger: water that evaporates too soon can't take part. The reaction continues for months or years, slowly gaining strength. It also gives off heat. The Hoover Dam was built in blocks threaded with pipes carrying cold water; engineers estimated that without them the mass would have taken over a century to cool, cracking as it did; see [[Heat Transfer]] and [[Specific Heat Capacity]].

Concrete is very strong when squeezed but weak when pulled or bent; under tension it cracks at about a tenth of the load it can take in compression; see [[Elasticity]]. **Reinforced concrete** solves this by embedding steel bars, rebar, where tension will occur. The pairing works partly by luck: steel and concrete expand with heat at almost the same rate, so they don't tear apart as temperatures change; see [[Steel]] and [[Thermal Expansion]]. Prestressed concrete goes further, stretching steel tendons before or after casting so the concrete starts out compressed.

The main enemy is water reaching the steel. Concrete is highly alkaline, which protects rebar, but over decades carbon dioxide and road salt penetrate the surface and corrosion begins. Rust takes up more volume than the iron it came from, so it bursts the surrounding concrete apart; see [[Oxidation]] and [[Material Fatigue]].

The Romans built with a concrete of lime and volcanic ash that has lasted two thousand years, including the unreinforced dome of the Pantheon, still the largest of its kind; see [[Plate Tectonics]]. Studies suggest it partly heals itself: lumps of lime react with water seeping into cracks and fill them with new mineral. Modern Portland cement, patented in 1824, is stronger and faster to set, but reinforced structures often last decades rather than centuries because of rebar corrosion.

Cement has a large climate cost. Making it means heating limestone in kilns to about 1,450 degrees Celsius, burning fuel and releasing carbon dioxide from the limestone itself. Cement production is commonly estimated at around 8 percent of global carbon dioxide emissions; see [[Greenhouse Effect]] and [[Combustion]]. Researchers are pursuing lower-carbon cements and methods that store carbon dioxide in the concrete as it cures.
