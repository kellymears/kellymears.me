**Magnetic resonance imaging** produces detailed pictures of the inside of the body, especially soft tissues such as the brain, spinal cord, muscles, ligaments and organs. Unlike X-rays and CT scans, it uses no ionizing radiation; see [[Ionizing Radiation]]. It relies on the fact that the body is mostly water and fat, both rich in hydrogen.

The nucleus of a hydrogen atom is a single proton, and it behaves like a tiny spinning magnet. Normally these point every which way. Inside an MRI scanner's magnet, typically 1.5 or 3 tesla, tens of thousands of times stronger than Earth's field, a slight majority line up with the field and wobble around it at a precise rate proportional to the field's strength; see [[Magnetism]].

A pulse of radio waves at exactly that rate tips them over, a case of resonance: energy delivered at a system's natural frequency is absorbed efficiently; see [[Resonance]] and [[Radio Spectrum]]. When the pulse stops, the nuclei relax back into line and emit a faint radio signal, which coils pick up much as a generator's coil picks up a moving magnet; see [[Electromagnetic Induction]]. The key is that relaxation takes different amounts of time in different tissues. Fat, fluid, gray matter and tumors relax at different rates, so timing the pulses and measurements differently produces images that emphasize different tissues.

To tell where a signal came from, additional gradient coils make the field vary slightly across the body, so nuclei in different places resonate at slightly different frequencies. A computer separates the frequencies to build the image; see [[Fourier Transform]]. The gradient coils switching on and off rapidly in the strong field cause the loud banging and buzzing, loud enough that patients wear hearing protection; see [[Hearing Loss]].

The main magnet is usually a coil of superconducting wire chilled by liquid helium to about 4 degrees above absolute zero, so the current flows without resistance and the field stays on permanently, even when nobody is being scanned; see [[Superconductivity]] and [[Absolute Zero]]. That creates the chief hazard: loose iron objects such as oxygen cylinders can be pulled in at high speed. Radio pulses also warm tissue slightly, and scanners limit the dose; see [[Specific Absorption Rate]]. Scanner rooms are shielded to keep outside radio noise out; see [[Faraday Cage]].

The underlying effect, **nuclear magnetic resonance**, was observed in 1946 and became a standard chemistry tool for working out molecular structures; see [[Spectroscopy]]. Paul Lauterbur and Peter Mansfield showed in the 1970s how to make images with it, sharing the 2003 Nobel Prize. The word "nuclear" was dropped from the medical name partly because it alarmed patients. **Functional MRI** tracks changes in blood oxygen to show which brain regions become more active during a task.
