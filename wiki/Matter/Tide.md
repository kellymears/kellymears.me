**Tides** are the regular rise and fall of sea level, usually twice a day. They come from the way gravity weakens with distance: the Moon pulls the near side of Earth slightly harder than the center, and the center slightly harder than the far side; see [[Gravity]]. The difference, called the **tidal force**, stretches Earth along the line to the Moon, raising two bulges of water, one facing the Moon and one opposite it.

Earth rotates under these bulges, so most coasts see two high tides and two low tides. Because the Moon moves along its [[Orbit]] in the same direction Earth spins, each cycle takes about 12 hours and 25 minutes, and high tide arrives roughly 50 minutes later each day.

The Sun raises tides too, a bit under half as strong as the Moon's. When Sun, Moon, and Earth line up, at new and full moon, the effects add into large **spring tides**. At the quarter moons they partly cancel, giving smaller **neap tides**.

Real tides look little like the simple two-bulge picture. Continents block the bulges, so water sloshes around ocean basins instead, steered by Earth's rotation into rotating systems; see [[Coriolis Effect]]. At the centers of these systems, called amphidromic points, the tide barely rises at all. The shape of a bay matters most. Water in a basin has a natural sloshing period, and when it's close to the tidal period the effect builds up, the way pushes time a swing; see [[Resonance]]. Canada's Bay of Fundy is tuned this way and sees ranges of 16 meters.

Tides change the Earth and Moon over long timescales. Friction between the moving water and the seabed slows Earth's rotation, lengthening the day by about two milliseconds per century. The rotation Earth loses goes to the Moon, which drifts about 3.8 centimeters farther away each year, measured by bouncing lasers off reflectors left by Apollo astronauts; see [[Angular Momentum]]. Earth's rotation is irregular enough that clocks have needed leap seconds to stay aligned with it, a practice due to end by 2035; see [[Unix Time]].

The same friction long ago slowed the Moon's spin until it matched its orbit. This **tidal locking** is why the Moon always shows Earth the same face. Tidal forces can also heat: Jupiter's moon Io is squeezed so hard by Jupiter that it's the most volcanic body in the solar system.

Tides can be harnessed. The La Rance plant in France has generated electricity from tidal flow since 1966, using turbines in a dam across an estuary; see [[Electromagnetic Induction]]. Tide tables are among the oldest successful predictions in science, computed by summing many periodic components; see [[Simple Harmonic Motion]].
