The **water cycle** is the movement of Earth's water among its reservoirs: the oceans, the atmosphere, ice sheets and glaciers, lakes and rivers, soil, underground aquifers and living things. Sunlight lifts water into the air by evaporation, and gravity brings it back down as rain and snow and carries it downhill to the sea.

The reservoirs are lopsided. The oceans hold about 97 percent of all water. Most fresh water is locked in ice or underground, and the atmosphere holds only around a thousandth of a percent at any moment. Water moves through the air quickly, staying aloft for about nine days on average, while deep groundwater and ice-sheet water can sit for thousands of years.

**Evaporation** happens wherever liquid water meets unsaturated air, mostly over the oceans. Turning liquid into vapor absorbs a large amount of energy, which the vapor carries with it and releases when it condenses back into droplets; see [[Latent Heat]] and [[Phase Transition]]. This makes the water cycle one of the planet's main heat-transport systems. Tropical storms run on it, drawing energy from warm ocean water and releasing it as rising air condenses; see [[Tropical Cyclone]]. Water's high capacity for heat also lets oceans moderate coastal climates; see [[Specific Heat Capacity]].

Plants contribute too, drawing water up from roots and releasing it from their leaves. Over land, a large share of the water returning to the air comes through plants rather than straight evaporation; see [[Capillary Action]] and [[Osmosis]].

Clouds form when air rises and cools until it reaches its dew point; see [[Dew Point]] and [[Atmospheric Pressure]]. Even then, droplets need something to form on, such as dust, salt or smoke particles, which is why clean air can stay supersaturated; see [[Nucleation]]. Cloud droplets are tiny and must merge or grow on ice crystals before they're heavy enough to fall as **precipitation**. Rising and falling ice in storm clouds also separates electric charge; see [[Lightning]].

Once on land, water soaks into soil, recharges aquifers, runs off into streams, or freezes into snowpack that feeds rivers through the dry months. Evaporation leaves salts behind, so rain is fresh even though most of it came from the sea. Over geological time, rivers carry dissolved minerals the other way, which is part of why the sea is salty.

Warming changes the cycle. Warmer air can hold about 7 percent more water vapor for each degree Celsius, so heavy downpours become heavier, while higher temperatures also dry soils faster between them. Water vapor is itself the most abundant greenhouse gas, but its amount follows temperature rather than setting it, so it amplifies warming caused by other gases; see [[Greenhouse Effect]].
