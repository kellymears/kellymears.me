The **seasons** come from the tilt of Earth's axis, about 23.4° from perpendicular to the plane of its orbit. Through the year the axis keeps pointing in the same direction in space, so for half the orbit the Northern Hemisphere leans toward the Sun and for the other half it leans away. Seasons in the two hemispheres are opposite; see [[Orbit]] and [[Angular Momentum]].

A common belief is that summer happens when Earth is nearer the Sun. It isn't so. Earth's orbit is close to a circle, and the planet is actually closest in early January, during northern winter. The distance varies by about 3 percent, changing sunlight's intensity by about 7 percent, far less than the tilt does; see [[Inverse-Square Law]].

The tilt acts in two ways:

- **Sun angle.** When the Sun is high, a beam of sunlight falls on a small patch of ground. When it's low, the same beam spreads over a larger area, so each square meter receives less energy.
- **Day length.** In summer the Sun is above the horizon longer, so the ground gains heat for more hours and loses it for fewer.

The turning points have names. The **solstices**, around 21 June and 21 December, mark the longest and shortest days. The **equinoxes**, around 20 March and 22 September, are when day and night are roughly equal everywhere. Inside the Arctic and Antarctic circles, the extremes become a midnight sun in summer and a polar night in winter. Near the equator, day length barely changes and seasons are defined more by rain than by temperature.

The hottest weeks come well after the longest day. Land and especially oceans absorb heat through early summer and release it slowly, so temperatures keep rising for weeks after the solstice. Coastal places have milder, more delayed seasons than inland ones because water stores more heat; see [[Specific Heat Capacity]] and [[Heat Transfer]].

Living things track the seasons. Short winter days shift sleep and mood, and at high latitudes winter sunlight is too weak for the skin to make vitamin D; see [[Circadian Rhythm]] and [[Vitamin D]].

The tilt itself changes over long periods. It varies between about 22.1° and 24.5° over a cycle of roughly 41,000 years, and the axis slowly wobbles like a spinning top, tracing a circle every 26,000 years or so. Along with slow changes in the orbit's shape, these cycles alter how sunlight is distributed across the seasons and are linked to the timing of ice ages; see [[Greenhouse Effect]].

Other planets have their own seasons. Mars, tilted about 25°, has seasons like Earth's but twice as long. Uranus is tipped on its side, so each pole gets decades of continuous daylight and then decades of darkness.
