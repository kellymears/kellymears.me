A liquid boils when its *vapor pressure*, the push of molecules escaping from its surface, equals the pressure of the surroundings. At that point bubbles of vapor can form inside the liquid instead of only at the surface. For water at sea level, under one standard atmosphere, that happens at 100 °C. Change the surrounding pressure and the **boiling point** moves with it.

Altitude lowers it. Air pressure falls as you climb, and water's boiling point drops by roughly 1 °C for every 300 meters of elevation. In Denver, at about 1,600 meters, water boils near 95 °C. On the summit of Everest it boils around 70 °C, too cool to cook rice or beans properly. Because boiling water can't get hotter than its boiling point (the extra energy goes into [[Latent Heat]]), anything cooked in water takes longer at altitude. Pasta and eggs take longer, [[Starch Gelatinization]] and [[Protein Denaturation]] proceed more slowly, and baking recipes need adjustments because water and gases in the batter behave differently.

Raising the pressure does the opposite. A pressure cooker traps steam until the pressure inside reaches about one atmosphere above normal, raising the boiling point to around 120 °C. The hotter water cooks tough cuts, dried beans, and stocks in a fraction of the usual time.

Boiling point also limits browning. As long as food is wet, its surface can't rise much above 100 °C, well below the temperatures where the [[Maillard Reaction]] and [[Caramelization]] proceed quickly. That is why boiled and steamed food doesn't brown, why patting meat dry improves a sear, and why a crowded pan steams instead of sizzling. Frying oil, by contrast, can go far past water's boiling point, limited only by its [[Smoke Point]]. A drop hitting a pan far above it skates on vapor, the [[Leidenfrost Effect]].

Some popular beliefs don't survive the numbers:

- **Salting the water** raises the boiling point, an effect called *boiling point elevation*, but a typical tablespoon of salt in a pot raises it by only a small fraction of a degree. Salt is for flavor.
- **Superheating** happens when very clean water in a smooth container, heated in a microwave, rises above its boiling point without bubbling, because bubbles need a rough spot or impurity to form. Disturbing it can make it erupt violently. A wooden stick or a scratched mug prevents it.

Refrigeration relies on boiling at the other extreme. A [[Vapor-Compression Refrigeration]] system uses a refrigerant that boils far below room temperature at low pressure. Letting it expand and boil inside the fridge absorbs heat. Compressing it outside raises its boiling point so it condenses and releases that heat.
