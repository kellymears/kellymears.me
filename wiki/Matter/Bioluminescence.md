**Bioluminescence** is the production of light by living organisms. Fireflies, glow worms, certain fungi, and a vast range of ocean life make their own light. In the deep sea, below the reach of sunlight, it's the main source of light, and a large share of the animals there can produce it.

The light comes from a chemical reaction, a kind of **chemiluminescence**. A molecule generically called luciferin reacts with oxygen, a reaction sped up by an enzyme called luciferase; see [[Oxidation]] and [[Catalysis]]. The reaction leaves a product molecule in an excited state, and as it relaxes it releases energy as a photon of visible light; see [[Photon]]. Glow sticks work on the same principle using synthetic chemicals.

Unlike a hot filament, bioluminescence produces almost no heat, which is why it's sometimes called cold light; see [[Black-Body Radiation]]. The firefly reaction is among the most efficient light sources known, converting a large fraction of reactions into photons. Fireflies power it with ATP, the cell's energy currency; see [[Energy Systems]].

Most marine bioluminescence is blue or blue-green, because those wavelengths travel farthest through seawater; see [[Electromagnetic Spectrum]]. Many deep-sea animals can't see red at all. A few fish exploit that by producing red light, illuminating prey that can't detect the beam.

Organisms use light in many ways:

- **Courtship.** Fireflies flash species-specific patterns to find mates. Some predatory fireflies mimic another species' signal to lure and eat them.
- **Hunting.** Anglerfish dangle a glowing lure, often lit by symbiotic bacteria.
- **Camouflage.** Many midwater animals light their undersides to match the faint light from above, erasing their silhouettes from predators below.
- **Defense.** Some squid and shrimp release glowing clouds to distract attackers. Others light up when disturbed, possibly to attract bigger predators to whatever is attacking them.

The bright sparkles in breaking waves at night usually come from single-celled plankton that flash when agitated.

Bioluminescence is distinct from **fluorescence**, which absorbs light at one wavelength and re-emits it at another, and requires an outside light source; see [[Fluorescence]]. The two are often found together. The jellyfish that gave biology its green fluorescent protein uses a bioluminescent reaction to produce blue light, which the protein converts to green.

Both have become laboratory tools. Scientists attach luciferase genes to other genes so cells glow when those genes are active, measuring activity with sensitive cameras; see [[Image Sensor]]. Firefly luciferase also detects ATP, which makes it useful for testing surfaces for bacterial contamination.

Animals seeing these displays depend on eyes adapted to very dim light; see [[Dark Adaptation]]. Some bioluminescent colors come from structures rather than pigments; see [[Structural Color]].
