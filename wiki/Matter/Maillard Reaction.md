The **Maillard reaction** is a family of chemical reactions between amino acids and reducing sugars that turns food brown and creates much of its savory flavor: the crust on seared steak and bread, roasted coffee and cocoa, toasted nuts, fried onions, and grilled anything. The French chemist Louis-Camille Maillard described it in 1912. Cooks used it for millennia before anyone knew what it was.

It is not one reaction but a branching cascade. A sugar and an amino acid combine, rearrange, fragment, and recombine into hundreds of products. Brown polymers called *melanoidins* provide the color. Small volatile molecules such as pyrazines, furans, and thiazoles provide aromas described as roasty, nutty, meaty, and toasty. Which amino acids and sugars are present determines which aromas form, which is why seared beef, toast, and coffee all brown but smell entirely different. Most of that difference reaches us through [[Retronasal Olfaction]].

The practical rules come from the chemistry:

- **Heat speeds it dramatically.** The reaction runs slowly at room temperature but becomes fast around 140 °C and above.
- **Water holds it back.** A wet surface cannot rise much above 100 °C while water is evaporating, so moisture stalls browning. Hence patting meat dry, not crowding the pan (crowded food steams), and why boiled food never browns.
- **Alkalinity speeds it.** A pinch of baking soda browns onions faster, and pretzels are dipped in an alkaline bath for their deep brown crust.
- **Time can substitute for heat.** Aged and dried foods brown slowly at low temperatures over weeks, as in black garlic.

It differs from [[Caramelization]], which involves sugars alone and needs higher temperatures. "Caramelized onions" are mostly Maillard browning, since onions contain amino acids as well as sugar. The reaction also has a downside. In starchy foods cooked very hot and dry, such as fries, crisps, and dark toast, the amino acid asparagine reacts with sugars to form *acrylamide*, a probable carcinogen at high doses, which is a reason to aim for golden rather than dark brown. The same chemistry runs slowly inside the body as *glycation*, in which blood sugar binds to proteins. A common diabetes blood test measures glycated hemoglobin for exactly this reason.
