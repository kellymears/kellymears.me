The **greenhouse effect** is the warming of a planet's surface by gases in its atmosphere that let sunlight in but slow the escape of heat. Without it, Earth's average surface temperature would be about −18 °C instead of about 15 °C. The effect is natural; the concern is that people are strengthening it.

The mechanism turns on wavelength. The Sun, at about 5,500 °C, emits mostly visible and near-infrared light, which passes through air easily. Earth's surface absorbs it and, being much cooler, radiates the energy back as longer-wavelength [[Infrared]]; see [[Color Temperature]]. Gases with three or more atoms per molecule, chiefly water vapor, carbon dioxide, methane, and nitrous oxide, absorb some of that outgoing infrared and re-emit it in all directions, including back down. Nitrogen and oxygen, 99% of the air, are nearly transparent to it. The surface has to warm until it radiates enough to balance what arrives from the Sun; see [[Thermal radiation]].

The name is a loose metaphor. A real greenhouse stays warm mainly because its glass stops warm air from rising and mixing away, not because it traps infrared.

Joseph Fourier argued in the 1820s that the atmosphere must keep Earth warmer than its distance from the Sun alone would allow; see [[Inverse-Square Law]]. Eunice Foote showed in 1856 that carbon dioxide heated more in sunlight than ordinary air, and John Tyndall measured the infrared absorption of individual gases in 1859. In 1896 Svante Arrhenius calculated that doubling carbon dioxide would warm the planet by several degrees.

**Albedo** is the other half of the balance: the fraction of sunlight reflected back to space. Fresh snow reflects about 80%, open ocean under 10%. Clouds both reflect sunlight and trap infrared, which makes them one of the hardest parts of the climate to model.

**Feedbacks** amplify or damp any change:

- **Water vapor.** Warmer air holds more water vapor, itself a greenhouse gas, which adds to the warming; see [[Dew Point]].
- **Ice and snow.** Melting exposes darker water and land, which absorb more sunlight.
- **Radiation.** A warmer planet radiates more heat, pulling it back toward balance, the main [[Negative feedback]] that keeps the climate stable.

The oceans' enormous heat capacity slows the response, so warming lags emissions by decades; see [[Thermal mass]]. Seas rise partly because warmer water takes up more space; see [[Thermal Expansion]].

Venus shows the extreme: its thick carbon dioxide atmosphere holds the surface near 465 °C, hotter than Mercury.

Most added carbon dioxide comes from [[Combustion]] of fossil fuels. Some refrigerants used in air conditioners and heat pumps trap thousands of times more heat per molecule, so leaks matter; see [[Vapor-Compression Refrigeration]]. Because the costs fall on everyone cutting emissions is a textbook problem of [[Collective Action]] and the [[Tragedy of the Commons]].
