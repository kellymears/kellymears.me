An **aurora** is light in the upper atmosphere, usually green, sometimes red or violet, produced when charged particles from space strike air molecules. In the north it is called the aurora borealis, or northern lights, and in the south the aurora australis. Displays usually appear in ovals around the magnetic poles, over places such as Alaska, northern Scandinavia, and Antarctica.

The energy comes from the Sun. It constantly emits the **solar wind**, a stream of charged particles that meets Earth's magnetic field and stretches it into a long tail on the night side; see [[Magnetism]]. Energy builds up in that tail and is released in bursts, accelerating electrons down along field lines into the atmosphere 100 to 300 kilometers up.

The colors identify the gas. Colliding electrons excite oxygen and nitrogen, which then shed the energy as light of specific wavelengths; see [[Fluorescence]] and [[Electromagnetic Spectrum]]. The common green is atomic oxygen around 100 to 150 kilometers. Deep red is also oxygen, but higher up, from a transition so slow, nearly two minutes on average, that it can occur only in air so thin that collisions rarely interrupt it. Blues and purples come from nitrogen.

The Sun's activity rises and falls over a roughly 11-year cycle. Near its peak, large eruptions hurl clouds of charged gas toward Earth, causing **geomagnetic storms** that push the auroral ovals far toward the equator. In May 2024 a strong storm brought auroras to skies across Europe, the southern United States, and northern India. Many people saw them more vividly on their phones than with their eyes: dim light is perceived mostly by color-blind rod cells, while a long camera exposure gathers the color.

The same storms threaten technology:

- **Power grids.** A changing magnetic field drives currents through long conductors on the ground; see [[Electromagnetic Induction]]. In March 1989 a storm collapsed the Hydro-Québec grid in about a minute and a half, leaving six million people without power for nine hours.
- **Satellites.** Storms heat and expand the upper atmosphere, increasing drag on low satellites; see [[Aerodynamic Drag]]. In February 2022 a modest storm caused about 38 newly launched Starlink satellites to fall back and burn up; see [[Satellite Internet]].
- **Navigation and radio.** Disturbances in the charged upper atmosphere throw off satellite positioning, and the May 2024 storm interrupted the GPS guidance farmers rely on for planting; see [[Global Positioning System]].

The largest storm on record, the **Carrington Event** of September 1859, followed a solar flare that the astronomer Richard Carrington happened to observe. Auroras were seen near the tropics, and telegraph lines sparked, some operators sending messages on the storm's induced current alone with their batteries disconnected. A storm of that size today would cost far more.
