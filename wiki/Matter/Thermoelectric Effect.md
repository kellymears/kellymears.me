The **thermoelectric effect** is the direct conversion between a temperature difference and an electric voltage, with no moving parts. It takes three related forms, each named for its discoverer:

- **Seebeck effect.** In 1821 Thomas Seebeck found that a loop of two different metals, with its two junctions at different temperatures, deflected a compass needle. A current was flowing, driven by a voltage that depends on the temperature difference. Seebeck thought he'd found a magnetic effect; see [[Magnetism]].
- **Peltier effect.** In 1834 Jean Peltier found the reverse: passing a current through a junction of two different conductors heats or cools it, depending on the direction of the current.
- **Thomson effect.** In 1851 William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, showed that a single conductor carrying current along a temperature gradient also absorbs or releases heat.

Underneath, heat pushes charge carriers from a conductor's hot end toward its cold end. Different materials do this to different degrees, and the difference between them is the voltage; see [[Heat Transfer]].

**Thermocouples** are the most common use. Two wires of different alloys are joined at the tip, and the small voltage at their free ends, tens of microvolts per degree, reveals the tip's temperature relative to the instrument's. Cheap and rugged, they work from cryogenic temperatures to over 1,000 °C, in kilns, engines, and ovens. Gas furnaces and water heaters use a thermocouple as a safety device: sitting in the pilot flame, its voltage holds the gas valve open, so if the flame goes out, the valve closes; see [[Combustion]].

**Peltier coolers** run the effect the other way. A module of many small semiconductor junctions moves heat from one face to the other when current flows, with no compressor or refrigerant. They cool portable car fridges, some processor coolers, and lasers that need a precise temperature; see [[Semiconductor]] and [[Laser]]. They are far less efficient than compressor refrigerators, so they suit uses where size and silence outweigh energy; see [[Vapor-Compression Refrigeration]].

Power generation is the third use. A **radioisotope thermoelectric generator** places thermocouples around a block of plutonium-238, whose radioactive decay keeps it hot for decades. Only a few percent of the heat becomes electricity, but there is nothing to wear out. They have powered the Voyager probes since 1977 and the Curiosity and Perseverance rovers on Mars; see [[Radioactive Decay]].

The limits come from thermodynamics and materials. No heat-to-electricity converter can beat the efficiency of an ideal heat engine running between the same temperatures; see [[Heat Engine]] and [[Entropy]]. A good thermoelectric material needs to conduct electricity well while conducting heat poorly, so the temperature difference isn't short-circuited. Metals do both together, which is why the best modern devices use semiconductors such as bismuth telluride; see [[Ohm's Law]].
