**Oxidation** is the loss of electrons by an atom or molecule; its partner, **reduction**, is the gain. The two always happen together, so chemists speak of *redox* reactions. The name comes from oxygen, the classic electron-grabber, but the reactions do not require it. Rusting iron, burning wood, a charging phone, and every breath you take are all redox chemistry.

The most familiar case is **rust**. Iron exposed to both oxygen and water slowly turns into flaky iron oxides that take up more space than the metal and fall away, exposing fresh iron underneath. Salt speeds it up by making water more conductive, which is why cars rust faster where roads are salted and why bike chains need oiling after wet rides.

Not every metal suffers the same way. Aluminum oxidizes instantly, but its oxide forms a thin, tight layer that seals the surface, a process called *passivation*. Stainless steel contains chromium that does the same. Copper develops a green patina, as on the Statue of Liberty, that protects the metal beneath.

Protective strategies include:

- **Barriers.** Paint, oil, plating, and wax keep water and oxygen away.
- **Sacrificial metals.** Zinc oxidizes more readily than iron, so galvanized steel's zinc coating corrodes first. Ships and water heaters carry zinc or magnesium blocks, called sacrificial anodes, for the same reason.
- **Avoiding galvanic pairs.** Two different metals in contact with water form a small battery, and the more reactive one corrodes quickly. Aluminum parts bolted to steel or carbon fiber need insulating grease or washers.

In the kitchen, oxidation is often what goes wrong:

- **Rancid fats.** Oxygen attacks unsaturated fats, producing stale, painty off-flavors. Heat and light accelerate it, as does reusing frying oil past its [[Smoke Point]]. Antioxidants such as vitamin E and rosemary extract slow it.
- **Browning fruit.** Cut apples and avocados brown when an enzyme oxidizes compounds in the exposed flesh. Lemon juice works by lowering pH, which slows the enzyme, rather than by blocking oxygen; see [[pH]].
- **Seasoning cast iron.** Heated oil oxidizes and polymerizes into a hard coating that protects the iron and resists sticking.

Controlled redox reactions power the modern world. A [[Lithium-Ion Battery]] stores energy by moving electrons through an external circuit while lithium ions shuttle between electrodes, reversing the process to recharge. [[Combustion]] is rapid oxidation releasing heat and light. Inside cells, [[Mitochondria]] oxidize food in carefully controlled steps, passing electrons down a chain to oxygen to make usable energy. The same chemistry produces reactive by-products that can damage cells, the basis of the oxidative-stress theory of aging, though antioxidant supplements have largely failed to show benefits in trials.
