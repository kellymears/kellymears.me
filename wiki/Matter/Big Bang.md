**The Big Bang** theory describes how the universe expanded from an extremely hot, dense state about 13.8 billion years ago into the cosmos seen today. It's not an explosion at a point in space. Space itself has been expanding everywhere, carrying galaxies apart.

The first strong evidence was **redshift**. Light from a source moving away is stretched toward longer, redder wavelengths, much as a passing siren drops in pitch; see [[Doppler Effect]]. Astronomers identify the shift by comparing the patterns of dark lines in a galaxy's light with those measured in laboratories; see [[Spectroscopy]]. In 1929 Edwin Hubble, building on measurements by Vesto Slipher and theoretical work by Georges Lemaître, reported that more distant galaxies recede faster, a relation now called **Hubble's law**. Run the expansion backward and everything converges on a dense beginning. In cosmology the stretching comes mostly from the expansion of space rather than from motion through it.

Lemaître proposed an expanding universe that began from a "primeval atom" in the late 1920s and early 1930s. The rival steady-state theory, which held that the universe is eternal and creates new matter as it expands, remained popular into the 1960s. The name "Big Bang" was coined by one of its critics, the astronomer Fred Hoyle, in a 1949 radio broadcast.

Two predictions settled the debate:

- **The cosmic microwave background.** About 380,000 years after the beginning, the universe cooled enough for atoms to form, and light could travel freely for the first time. That light, stretched by expansion, now arrives from every direction as microwaves with a temperature about 2.7 degrees above absolute zero; see [[Absolute Zero]], [[Black-Body Radiation]], and [[Electromagnetic Spectrum]]. Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson detected it by accident in 1965 as persistent noise in a radio antenna; see [[Antenna]].
- **Light elements.** In the first few minutes, nuclear reactions forged hydrogen, helium, and traces of lithium in proportions the theory predicts and observations confirm; see [[Nuclear Fusion]]. Heavier elements were made later, in stars.

Satellites have mapped tiny temperature variations in the background radiation in detail. Those variations seeded the galaxies and clusters that formed later through gravity; see [[Gravity]].

The theory leaves open questions. Most of the universe's matter appears to be dark matter, detected only by its gravity. The expansion is accelerating, attributed to dark energy, whose nature is unknown. A brief period of extremely rapid early expansion, called inflation, is widely accepted but not confirmed. Different measurement methods also disagree on the current expansion rate.

The far future depends on that expansion. One scenario ends in a cold, dilute universe where no usable energy remains; see [[Entropy]].
