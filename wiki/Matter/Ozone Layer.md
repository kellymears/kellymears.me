The **ozone layer** is a region of the stratosphere, roughly 15 to 35 kilometers up, where ozone is concentrated. Ozone is a form of oxygen with three atoms per molecule instead of two; see [[Allotrope]]. Even there it's sparse: compressed to sea-level pressure, the whole layer would be only about three millimeters thick. Yet it absorbs nearly all of the Sun's most energetic ultraviolet light, UV-C, and most of UV-B, which damages DNA and causes sunburn and skin cancer; see [[Electromagnetic Spectrum]] and [[Sunburn]].

The layer is continuously made and unmade. Ultraviolet light splits ordinary oxygen molecules into single atoms, which join other oxygen molecules to form ozone. Ozone in turn absorbs ultraviolet light and breaks apart, turning that energy into heat. That heat is why temperature rises with height in the stratosphere, creating a stable lid on the weather below; see [[Photon]] and [[Atmospheric Pressure]].

In 1974 the chemists Mario Molina and Sherwood Rowland warned that **chlorofluorocarbons**, or CFCs, could break this balance. CFCs were valued as refrigerants, aerosol propellants and foaming agents precisely because they're stable and nontoxic; see [[Vapor-Compression Refrigeration]]. That stability lets them drift intact to the stratosphere, where ultraviolet light finally breaks them and frees chlorine. A single chlorine atom acts as a catalyst, destroying ozone molecule after molecule without being used up, potentially tens of thousands before it's taken out of circulation; see [[Catalysis]].

In 1985 scientists from the British Antarctic Survey reported a severe seasonal thinning over Antarctica, which became known as the **ozone hole**. The cause turned out to be polar stratospheric clouds, which form in the extreme cold of the Antarctic winter and convert chlorine into forms that are released all at once when sunlight returns in spring; see [[Seasons]]. Satellite data had shown the thinning too, but it had reportedly been flagged as instrument error.

The response was unusually fast. The **Montreal Protocol**, signed in 1987, committed countries to phase out CFCs and related chemicals, and it became the first treaty ratified by every member of the United Nations. Chlorine in the stratosphere peaked around the turn of the century and is declining. Scientific assessments project the Antarctic ozone hole will recover to 1980 levels around the middle of this century; see [[Tragedy of the Commons]].

The story has a climate side as well. CFCs are potent greenhouse gases, so the treaty also slowed warming. Their main replacements, HFCs, spare the ozone layer but trap heat, and a 2016 amendment began phasing those down too; see [[Greenhouse Effect]]. Ozone near the ground, by contrast, is a pollutant formed from vehicle exhaust in sunlight, harmful to lungs and crops; see [[Oxidation]].
