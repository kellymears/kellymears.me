**Magnetism** is a force produced by moving electric charges. Every magnet has a north and a south pole; opposite poles attract and like poles repel. Cut a bar magnet in half and you get two smaller magnets, each with both poles; no one has ever isolated a single pole.

The space around a magnet is filled with a **magnetic field**, which iron filings make visible as curving lines from pole to pole. Earth has one, generated by molten iron churning in its outer core, and a compass needle aligns with it. The field shields the planet from much of the solar wind, and its interaction with charged particles near the poles produces the aurora.

Magnetism and electricity are two faces of one force. In 1820, Hans Christian Ørsted noticed that a wire carrying current deflected a compass needle: electric current creates a magnetic field. Michael Faraday found the reverse, that a changing magnetic field drives current in a wire; see [[Electromagnetic Induction]]. James Clerk Maxwell unified the two in the 1860s and showed that light itself is a traveling wave of electric and magnetic fields; see [[Electromagnetic Spectrum]].

Permanent magnets get their magnetism from electrons. Each electron behaves like a tiny magnet because of a quantum property called spin. In most materials, those tiny magnets point randomly or pair off and cancel. In **ferromagnetic** materials, chiefly iron, nickel, and cobalt, neighboring electrons line up, forming regions called domains. Magnetizing a piece of iron aligns its domains. Heat disrupts them: above a material's **Curie temperature**, 770 °C for iron, it loses its magnetism abruptly, a kind of [[Phase Transition]]. Strong modern magnets use rare-earth alloys such as neodymium-iron-boron.

An **electromagnet** is a coil of wire, usually around an iron core, that becomes a magnet when current flows and stops when it's switched off. That controllability made magnetism useful:

- **Motors and generators** turn magnetic forces into motion and motion into current; see [[Electric Motor]] and [[Alternating Current]].
- **Transformers and wireless charging** pass energy between coils through a changing field.
- **Data storage.** Hard drives and tape record bits as the direction of tiny magnetized regions. A strong magnet can scramble them.
- **Medicine.** MRI machines use superconducting magnets tens of thousands of times stronger than Earth's field to image soft tissue by aligning hydrogen nuclei.
- **Speakers and induction cooktops.** A speaker's coil pushes against a magnet to move its cone, making [[Sound]]; an induction cooktop's rapidly changing field heats iron pans directly through eddy currents.

Magnetic forces also shape light: certain materials rotate the plane of [[Polarized Light]] in a magnetic field. And moving magnets near conductors induce eddy currents that resist the motion, which is how magnetic brakes on trains and indoor trainers work without contact; see [[Friction]].
