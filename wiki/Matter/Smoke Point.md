The **smoke point** of a cooking fat is the temperature at which it begins to give off a continuous stream of visible smoke. At that point the fat is breaking down. Free fatty acids and glycerol decompose into volatile compounds, including acrolein, which irritates the eyes and lungs and tastes acrid. Beyond the smoke point lie the *flash point*, where vapors can ignite, and the *fire point*, where they keep burning.

Smoke points vary widely, from about 150 °C for butter and some unrefined oils to 250 °C or more for refined oils. Two factors dominate:

- **Refinement.** Refining strips out free fatty acids, proteins, and other small particles that start breaking down early. Refined avocado, peanut, and rice bran oils smoke much higher than their unrefined versions.
- **Free fatty acid content.** Butter smokes early because its milk solids burn. Clarified butter (ghee), with the solids removed, tolerates far more heat. Oil used repeatedly for frying accumulates free fatty acids and its smoke point drops with every use.

The trouble is that smoke point is often used as a single figure of merit for which oil is "safe" at high heat, and it measures only one thing. What matters for health and flavor is also **oxidative stability**: how readily the fat reacts with oxygen and breaks down into harmful compounds while being heated, smoke or no smoke. Fats rich in polyunsaturated fatty acids oxidize more readily even below their smoke point. Extra virgin olive oil, with a moderate smoke point, turns out in heating studies to be among the most stable oils, because it is mostly monounsaturated and rich in natural antioxidants. A high smoke point paired with poor stability can be worse than the reverse. Treating one easily measured number as the whole question is the [[Streetlight Effect]] in the kitchen, and optimizing for it is [[Goodhart's Law]].

The practical guidance is simpler than the numbers suggest. Use refined, stable oils for searing and deep frying, where surfaces run well above 200 °C to drive the [[Maillard Reaction]]. Save flavorful unrefined oils for gentle heat or finishing, where their aromas, carried by [[Retronasal Olfaction]], survive. And treat visible smoke as the sign it is: the pan is hotter than the fat can bear.
