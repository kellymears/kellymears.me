The **photovoltaic effect** is the generation of electric current when light strikes certain materials. It's how solar cells turn sunlight directly into electricity, with no moving parts. Edmond Becquerel observed it in 1839, at age 19, while experimenting with electrodes in solution.

It rests on a related discovery, the **photoelectric effect**: light shining on metal knocks electrons free. Classical physics predicted that brighter light should give electrons more energy, but experiments showed otherwise. Brightness changed how *many* electrons came out; only the light's color changed their energy, and below a threshold frequency none came out at all, however intense the light. In 1905 Albert Einstein explained this by treating light as particles, photons, each carrying energy set by its frequency; see [[Electromagnetic Spectrum]]. It was the work cited for his Nobel Prize, and a foundation of quantum theory.

A solar cell is a [[Semiconductor]], usually silicon, made into a junction between two oppositely doped layers, which creates a built-in electric field. When a photon with enough energy hits, it frees an electron and leaves behind a gap called a hole. The junction's field sweeps the two apart before they can recombine, pushing electrons out through a circuit as current. A single silicon cell produces about half a volt, so panels wire dozens in series.

Efficiency has hard limits:

- **The band gap.** Photons with less energy than silicon's band gap, much of the infrared, pass through unused. Photons with more energy free an electron, but the excess is lost as heat. Together these cap a single-junction silicon cell near 33 percent, the Shockley–Queisser limit, and commercial panels reach about 20 to 23 percent.
- **Heat.** Cells lose efficiency as they warm, roughly half a percent per degree, so panels produce less on hot summer days than on cold sunny ones; see [[Heat Transfer]].
- **Angle and shade.** Output depends on how directly light strikes; the effective intensity falls with the sun's angle, as well as with distance by the [[Inverse-Square Law]] across the solar system. Shading one cell in a series string can cripple the whole panel unless bypass diodes route around it.

Multi-junction cells stack layers with different band gaps, each catching part of the spectrum, and exceed 45 percent in labs.

Panels produce direct current, so homes and the grid need inverters to convert it to [[Alternating Current]]. Since sunlight varies through the day, solar is often paired with storage such as the [[Lithium-Ion Battery]]. The cost of solar electricity fell roughly 90 percent between 2010 and 2020, following a steep learning curve, making it among the cheapest sources of new power.

The same physics runs in reverse in an LED, where current produces light, and in camera sensors, which count photons by the charge they free.
