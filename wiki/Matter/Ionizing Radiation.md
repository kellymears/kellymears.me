**Ionizing radiation** is radiation with enough energy per particle to knock electrons out of atoms, turning them into ions and breaking the chemical bonds they were part of. That includes high-energy light, meaning X-rays, gamma rays, and the most energetic ultraviolet, and fast particles thrown off by unstable nuclei: alpha particles, beta particles, and neutrons; see [[Radioactive Decay]].

The line between ionizing and non-ionizing is set by energy per [[Photon]], not total power. Each photon of visible light, radio, or microwave carries far too little energy to ionize anything, however many there are; they can heat tissue but not break its molecules apart. That is why phones and microwave ovens are regulated for heating, not for cancer risk; see [[Specific Absorption Rate]], [[Microwave Heating]], and [[Electromagnetic Spectrum]].

The harm comes mainly from damage to DNA. A cell can repair most broken strands, but misrepaired damage can kill the cell or cause mutations that lead, years later, to cancer. Rapidly dividing cells, in bone marrow, gut lining, and a developing fetus, are the most vulnerable.

Doses are measured in two units. The **gray** is energy absorbed, one joule per kilogram. The **sievert** weights that by the type of radiation and the tissues exposed, to estimate biological harm. Some reference points:

- The worldwide average dose from natural background radiation is about 2.4 millisieverts a year, much of it from radon gas seeping from the ground.
- A chest X-ray is a small fraction of a millisievert; a CT scan of the abdomen can be around ten.
- Acute radiation sickness begins at about one sievert received at once, and doses of several sieverts are often fatal without treatment.

For low doses, radiation protection agencies assume risk rises in proportion to dose with no safe threshold. That model is deliberately cautious, and how much harm very low doses actually do is still debated.

The types penetrate differently. Alpha particles are stopped by paper or the outer layer of skin, so they are dangerous mainly when the source is inhaled or swallowed. Beta particles pass a few millimeters into tissue. Gamma rays and X-rays need thick lead or concrete. Protection comes down to time, distance, and shielding; distance works because intensity falls with the square of it; see [[Inverse-Square Law]].

The Geiger counter, developed by Hans Geiger and Walther Müller in 1928, detects each ionizing particle as a click when it sets off a discharge in a gas-filled tube.

It is also useful, in X-ray imaging, radiotherapy, and sterilizing medical equipment. Cosmic rays from space are ionizing too; at ground level they are weak, but they can flip bits in computer memory; see [[Single-Event Upset]]. Sunburn, by contrast, comes mostly from ultraviolet light that damages DNA directly without ionizing it; see [[Sunburn]].
