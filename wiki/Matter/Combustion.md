**Combustion** is a fast, self-sustaining reaction between a fuel and an oxidizer, usually oxygen in the air, that releases heat and light. It is [[Oxidation]] running quickly enough that the heat it releases keeps the reaction going. Fire cooked the first meals, drove the industrial revolution, and still produces most of the world's energy.

The **fire triangle** names the three things a fire needs: fuel, oxygen, and heat. Remove any one and the fire goes out. Firefighting methods map directly onto it: water removes heat, a lid or blanket removes oxygen, and a firebreak removes fuel.

Solid fuels do not burn as solids. Heat breaks wood down through *pyrolysis*, releasing flammable gases that burn above the surface as flame, leaving charcoal behind. Liquids burn the same way: it is the vapor that ignites. A fuel's *flash point* is the lowest temperature at which it gives off enough vapor to ignite with a spark; its *autoignition* temperature is where it ignites with no spark at all. Cooking oil heated past its [[Smoke Point]] keeps approaching both.

Flame color tells you what is burning and how well:

- **Blue.** Hot, well-mixed combustion, glowing from excited molecular fragments. A gas stove's flame should be blue.
- **Yellow and orange.** Tiny soot particles heated until they glow, like a filament, with a color set by their temperature, as described under [[Color Temperature]]. Candle and wood flames are yellow for this reason.
- **Other colors.** Metal salts emit specific colors, the basis of fireworks: strontium for red, copper for blue-green, sodium for intense yellow.

Complete combustion of a hydrocarbon produces carbon dioxide and water. Starved of oxygen, it is *incomplete*, producing soot and carbon monoxide, an odorless poison. That is why gas appliances need ventilation and why a yellow, smoky gas flame signals a problem.

A **grease fire** shows the physics clearly. Water poured onto burning oil sinks below it, flashes into steam almost instantly, expanding about 1,700-fold, and throws burning oil into the air as a fireball. The right response is to cut the heat and smother the pan with a lid, removing oxygen.

Flames also depend on gravity. On Earth, hot combustion gases rise by [[Buoyancy]], drawing in fresh air from below and stretching the flame upward. In the microgravity of orbit there is no convection, so flames form slow, dim, blue spheres that can starve themselves of oxygen.

Michael Faraday's 1848 lectures *The Chemical History of a Candle* used a single candle to explain combustion, convection, capillary action, and the products of burning.

In the kitchen, controlled combustion gives the high heat behind searing and grilling, driving the [[Maillard Reaction]], while wood and charcoal add smoke whose compounds flavor food.
