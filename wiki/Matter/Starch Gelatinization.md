**Starch gelatinization** is what happens when starch is heated in water. Starch is how plants store energy, packed into tiny, tightly ordered granules made of two glucose polymers: *amylose*, long and mostly straight, and *amylopectin*, highly branched. Cold water barely affects them. Heated past a threshold, typically 55–85 °C depending on the plant, the granules absorb water, swell to many times their size, lose their crystalline order, and leak amylose into the surrounding liquid. The liquid thickens, its [[Viscosity]] rising sharply, and the starch becomes soft and digestible. Cooking rice, pasta, potatoes, porridge, and gravy all rely on it.

Thickening has its own technique:

- **Lumps form** when starch is added straight to hot liquid: the outside of a clump gelatinizes into a waterproof skin around dry powder. The fix is to separate the granules first, either in cold water (a *slurry*) or coated in fat (a *roux*, butter and flour cooked together).
- **Acid and sugar interfere,** so tomato and fruit sauces need more thickener, and acid added late is gentler.
- **Overstirring and overcooking** shear swollen granules apart and thin the sauce again.
- **Different starches behave differently.** Cornstarch gives an opaque gel that sets firm when cool. Tapioca and arrowroot stay glossy and clear.

The process partly reverses over time, which is called **retrogradation**. As cooked starch cools, the amylose and then the amylopectin chains slowly realign into crystalline regions, squeezing water out. That is why bread goes stale: it firms up mostly through recrystallization, not mainly through drying out. Retrogradation is fastest at refrigerator temperatures, so bread stales faster in the fridge than on the counter. Brief reheating melts the crystals again, which is why toast or a warm oven revives day-old bread. Cooked and cooled rice, potatoes, and pasta form *resistant starch*, which resists digestion and behaves more like fiber, one reason day-old rice fries better and cooked-cooled potatoes raise blood sugar a little less.

Glycogen, the body's own carbohydrate store (see [[Glycogen]]), is a close relative of amylopectin: an even more branched glucose polymer, sometimes called animal starch, whose many branch ends let it be broken down quickly.
