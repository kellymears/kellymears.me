A **supernova** is the explosion of a star, for a few weeks shining as brightly as billions of ordinary stars combined. Supernovae scatter elements stars have made, leave behind the densest objects known, and have served as measuring sticks for the size and history of the universe.

There are two main kinds. A **core-collapse supernova** ends the life of a star at least about eight times the Sun's mass. Such a star fuses progressively heavier elements in its core until it produces iron, from which fusion releases no further energy; see [[Nuclear Fusion]]. With nothing left to hold it up, the core collapses in under a second into a neutron star or a black hole; see [[Neutron Star]] and [[Black Hole]]. The infalling outer layers rebound, and a flood of neutrinos released by the collapse helps drive them outward; see [[Shock Wave]]. Most of the energy in fact leaves as neutrinos. When a supernova appeared in a nearby galaxy in 1987, detectors on Earth caught about two dozen of them, hours before the light arrived.

A **Type Ia supernova** is different. It happens when a white dwarf, the dense remnant of a smaller star, gains enough mass, often from a companion, to ignite runaway fusion throughout and blow itself apart. Because they explode under similar conditions, Type Ia supernovae reach similar peak brightnesses, after correcting for how quickly they fade. Comparing how bright one appears with how bright it really is gives its distance; see [[Inverse-Square Law]]. Objects used this way are called **standard candles**. In 1998, two teams using them found that distant supernovae were dimmer than expected, meaning the expansion of the universe is speeding up, a discovery that earned the 2011 Nobel Prize in physics; see [[Big Bang]].

Astronomers classify supernovae by the elements visible in their light; see [[Spectroscopy]]. Their glow fades over months as radioactive nickel made in the blast decays into cobalt and then iron; see [[Radioactive Decay]].

Supernovae are a source of much of the matter around us. Elements heavier than hydrogen and helium are forged in stars, and explosions spread them through space, to be gathered into later stars and planets. Much of the iron in blood and the oxygen in air was made this way, while some of the heaviest elements come mainly from neutron star mergers.

In the Milky Way supernovae are rare, estimated at a few per century, and most are hidden behind dust. The last one clearly seen by eye was in 1604. A bright one recorded by Chinese astronomers in 1054 left the Crab Nebula, with a pulsar at its center. A supernova within a few dozen light-years could damage Earth's ozone layer, though no star that close is expected to explode soon; see [[Ionizing Radiation]].
