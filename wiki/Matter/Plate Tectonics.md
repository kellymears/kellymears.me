**Plate tectonics** is the theory that Earth's rigid outer layer, the lithosphere, is broken into about a dozen large plates and many smaller ones that move slowly over a hotter, weaker layer beneath. Plates travel a few centimeters a year, about as fast as fingernails grow. Over millions of years, that motion opens oceans, closes them, and builds mountain ranges.

The idea began as **continental drift**. In 1912 the German scientist Alfred Wegener proposed that the continents had once been joined in a single landmass and had since moved apart. The evidence was suggestive: the coastlines of South America and Africa fit together, and matching fossils and rock formations appear on both sides of the Atlantic. Most geologists rejected the theory for decades, largely because Wegener couldn't explain what force could push continents through solid rock; see [[Plausible Mechanism]].

The evidence that changed minds came from the seafloor in the 1950s and 1960s. Ocean surveys found a long ridge running down the middle of the Atlantic. Rocks on either side recorded Earth's magnetic field as they cooled, and because the field periodically reverses, they formed symmetric stripes of alternating magnetization; see [[Magnetism]]. New crust was forming at the ridge and spreading outward. By the late 1960s these findings, combined with maps of where earthquakes occur, had become plate tectonics; see [[Falsifiability]].

Plates meet at three kinds of boundary:

- **Divergent** boundaries, where plates separate and new crust forms, as at mid-ocean ridges and in East Africa's Rift Valley.
- **Convergent** boundaries, where plates collide. Dense oceanic crust sinks beneath another plate in **subduction**, forming deep trenches and chains of volcanoes. Where continents collide, neither sinks easily, and crust crumples into ranges such as the Himalayas.
- **Transform** boundaries, where plates grind past each other, as along California's San Andreas Fault.

Most earthquakes and volcanoes occur along these boundaries; see [[Earthquake]]. The ring of volcanoes and earthquakes around the Pacific marks subduction zones on every side.

The driving forces come from Earth's internal heat, much of it from radioactive decay; see [[Radioactive Decay]]. Over geological time the mantle flows slowly, like an extremely viscous fluid, carrying heat outward; see [[Viscosity]] and [[Heat Transfer]]. Sinking slabs at subduction zones pull their plates along. Continents ride high because their rock is less dense than the mantle beneath; see [[Buoyancy]].

Plate motion can now be measured directly. Satellite positioning shows stations on different plates moving apart or together by millimeters each year; see [[Global Positioning System]].

Plate tectonics also shaped life and climate, by rearranging oceans, recycling carbon, and building the mountains that alter weather; see [[Greenhouse Effect]].
