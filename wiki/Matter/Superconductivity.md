**Superconductivity** is a state in which certain materials, cooled below a critical temperature, conduct electricity with exactly zero resistance and push magnetic fields out of their interior. A current started in a superconducting loop can circulate for years without a power source. Ordinary conductors always waste some energy as heat; see [[Ohm's Law]].

Heike Kamerlingh Onnes discovered it in 1911. Having become the first to liquefy helium three years earlier, he cooled mercury to about 4 kelvin and watched its resistance vanish abruptly rather than fade gradually. The sudden drop marks a genuine change of state, like freezing; see [[Phase Transition]].

Zero resistance is not the whole story. In 1933 Walther Meissner and Robert Ochsenfeld found that a superconductor actively expels magnetic fields, which a merely perfect conductor would not do; see [[Magnetism]]. The **Meissner effect** is why a small magnet can float above a cooled superconductor.

The explanation took until 1957, when John Bardeen, Leon Cooper, and Robert Schrieffer showed that electrons in the metal pair up through slight distortions of the crystal lattice. The pairs move together as one quantum state that can't scatter off impurities the way single electrons do, so nothing slows them. In 1986 Georg Bednorz and Alex Müller found copper oxide ceramics superconducting at much higher temperatures, and within a year a compound worked at 93 kelvin, above the 77-kelvin boiling point of liquid nitrogen, which is cheap; see [[Boiling Point]]. How these ceramics work is still not fully understood.

Uses:

- **Strong magnets.** Superconducting wire carries enormous current without overheating, producing fields no copper coil could sustain. MRI scanners are the largest commercial use, alongside particle accelerators and fusion experiments; see [[Electromagnetic Induction]].
- **Maglev.** Japan's superconducting maglev line holds the train speed record of 603 kilometers an hour, set in 2015.
- **Sensors and qubits.** Superconducting loops make the most sensitive magnetic detectors, and they are the basis of the qubits in several leading quantum computers; see [[Quantum Computing]].

The catch is cold. Most practical magnets still need liquid helium, which is scarce, and a magnet that loses its superconductivity suddenly releases its stored energy as heat, called a quench.

That makes a superconductor working at room temperature and ordinary pressure a long-sought goal, and a magnet for hype. Hydrogen-rich compounds squeezed to pressures like those deep inside the Earth do superconduct near room temperature, but several high-profile claims in that field were retracted. In 2023 a preprint claiming a room-temperature superconductor called LK-99 spread widely online; replication attempts within weeks traced its behavior to impurities. The episode showed both the speed of open replication and how far a claim can travel first; see [[Replication Crisis]].
