**Escape velocity** is the minimum speed an object needs to leave a planet, moon, or star and never fall back, assuming it gets no further push and meets no air resistance. From Earth's surface it's about 11.2 kilometers per second, roughly 40,000 kilometers per hour. From the Moon it's about 2.4 kilometers per second.

The number comes from balancing energy. A thrown object trades its kinetic energy for gravitational potential energy as it climbs; see [[Conservation of Energy]]. Throw it fast enough and it never runs out of kinetic energy, even as gravity weakens with distance; see [[Gravity]] and [[Inverse-Square Law]]. Escape velocity depends on the mass and radius of the body being escaped, not on the mass of the object leaving. A pebble and a spacecraft need equal speeds.

Reaching orbit takes less. An object in low Earth orbit travels about 7.8 kilometers per second sideways, fast enough that the ground curves away beneath it as quickly as it falls; see [[Orbit]] and [[Centripetal Force]]. Escape velocity is always the square root of two, about 1.41, times the circular orbital speed at the same distance.

Rockets don't reach escape velocity in one burst at the surface. They climb gradually, losing some energy to gravity and to the atmosphere along the way; see [[Aerodynamic Drag]]. Mission planners describe the total change in speed a journey requires as its delta-v, and budget it like money.

That budget is expensive because of the **rocket equation**, published by Konstantin Tsiolkovsky in 1903. A rocket accelerates by throwing mass backward; see [[Newton's Laws of Motion]]. Achievable delta-v grows only with the logarithm of the ratio of fueled to empty mass. Adding more speed requires exponentially more fuel, and fuel to lift that fuel. That's why most of a launch vehicle is propellant, why rockets drop empty stages, and why reusable boosters were such a large engineering achievement. Gravity assists, swinging past planets to borrow some of their orbital motion, provide delta-v without fuel.

Escape velocity also shapes planets. Gas molecules move at speeds that depend on temperature and mass; see [[Ideal Gas Law]]. Light gases like hydrogen and helium have fast molecules, a fraction of which exceed a small planet's escape velocity and leak away over time. The Moon's low escape velocity is one reason it has essentially no atmosphere, while Earth retains nitrogen and oxygen; see [[Atmospheric Pressure]].

In 1783 the natural philosopher John Michell imagined a star so dense that its escape velocity exceeded the speed of light, so even light couldn't leave. That idea, later reworked with general relativity, describes a black hole; see [[Black Hole]].

Some places in space let a spacecraft hold its position relative to two bodies with little fuel at all; see [[Lagrange Point]].
