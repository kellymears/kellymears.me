A **colloid** is a mixture in which tiny particles of one substance stay spread through another without settling out or dissolving. The particles range from about a nanometer to a micrometer across, too large to form a true solution, as salt does in water, and too small to sink like sand. The constant jostling of molecules keeps them suspended; see [[Diffusion]]. The chemist Thomas Graham named them in 1861, from the Greek word for glue.

Colloids are classified by what is dispersed in what:

- **Sols.** Solid particles in a liquid, such as paint, ink, and muddy water.
- **Emulsions.** Liquid droplets in another liquid, such as milk and mayonnaise; see [[Emulsion]].
- **Foams.** Gas bubbles in a liquid, such as whipped cream, beer foam, and shaving cream. Solid foams include bread, pumice, and marshmallows.
- **Gels.** A network of solid strands trapping a liquid, such as gelatin dessert, tofu, and set custard.
- **Aerosols.** Liquid droplets or solid particles in a gas, such as fog, mist, smoke, and hairspray; see [[Dew Point]].

There is no gas-in-gas colloid, since gases always mix completely.

Colloids are recognizable by how they treat light. Their particles are large enough to scatter it, which makes a beam visible passing through them, as when headlights cut through fog or sunbeams cross a dusty room. That is the **Tyndall effect**. It makes milk white, and makes skim milk faintly blue, because smaller particles scatter blue light more strongly; see [[Rayleigh Scattering]]. Particles packed into a regular lattice can produce color without pigment, as in opal; see [[Structural Color]].

Stability is the central problem. Particles attract one another at close range and would clump if nothing kept them apart. Two things do: a like electric charge on each particle, which repels its neighbors, or a coating of molecules that act as bumpers; see [[Surfactant]]. Adding salt shields the charge and lets particles clump, which is how tofu is set with mineral salts and why rivers drop their silt where they meet the sea, building deltas.

Kitchens run on colloids. Gravy thickens as swollen starch granules form a network; see [[Starch Gelatinization]]. Egg whites whip into stable foam because beating unfolds their proteins, which then link around each bubble; see [[Protein Denaturation]]. Gelatin melts just below body temperature, which is why it dissolves on the tongue.

Many colloids flow strangely. Ketchup and paint thin when stirred or brushed and thicken again at rest, while a dense cornstarch suspension stiffens under impact; see [[Non-Newtonian Fluid]] and [[Viscosity]].

Colloids also helped prove that atoms exist. In 1908 Jean Perrin tracked the random motion of suspended particles under a microscope and found that it matched Albert Einstein's 1905 prediction for particles being battered by molecules, work recognized with the 1926 Nobel Prize.
