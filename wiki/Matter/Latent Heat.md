**Latent heat** is the energy a substance absorbs or releases when it changes phase, between solid, liquid, and gas, while its temperature stays the same. The Scottish chemist Joseph Black identified it around 1761, after noticing that ice melting in a warm room stays at its melting point until it has all melted, even though heat keeps flowing in. The heat goes into breaking the bonds that hold the solid together, not into raising the temperature.

Water's numbers show how large it is. Warming a gram of water by one degree Celsius takes about 4.2 joules, its [[Specific Heat Capacity]]. Melting a gram of ice takes about 334 joules, as much as heating that water by 80 degrees. Boiling a gram of water away takes about 2,260 joules, more than five times what it took to heat it from freezing to boiling.

This explains many everyday observations:

- **The boiling plateau.** A pot of water at a rolling boil stays at its [[Boiling Point]] no matter how high the burner is turned. Extra heat just makes it boil away faster.
- **Ice keeps drinks cold.** A drink with ice holds near 0 °C until the ice melts, absorbing heat that would otherwise warm it.
- **Steam burns worse than boiling water.** Steam at 100 °C releases its latent heat as it condenses on skin, delivering far more energy than water at the same temperature.

Evaporation is the most important case for living things. Sweat absorbs latent heat from the skin as it evaporates, removing heat far more effectively than [[Heat Transfer]] by conduction or radiation alone. It is the body's main defense against overheating, central to [[Thermoregulation]], and [[Heat Acclimation]] works partly by making people sweat earlier and more. It only works if the sweat can evaporate. In humid air, sweat drips off without cooling, which is why humid heat is more dangerous than dry heat at the same temperature.

Machines exploit the same physics. A [[Vapor-Compression Refrigeration]] system cools by letting a refrigerant evaporate inside the fridge, absorbing latent heat, then compresses and condenses it outside, releasing that heat to the room. Heat pipes in laptops and graphics cards evaporate a fluid at the hot end and condense it at the cool end, moving heat far faster than solid copper could, which delays [[Thermal Throttling]].

In cooking, latent heat caps the temperature of anything wet at about 100 °C until its surface water has boiled off. That is why food must dry before the [[Maillard Reaction]] can brown it, why moist foods resist burning, and why a hot pan briefly loses heat when wet food goes in, sometimes ending a sear before it starts. The extreme case is a drop skating on a vapor cushion, the [[Leidenfrost Effect]].
