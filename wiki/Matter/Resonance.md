**Resonance** is the large response a system gives when it is pushed at, or near, its own natural frequency. Anything that can oscillate, a pendulum, a guitar string, a bridge, an electrical circuit, has frequencies at which it tends to vibrate on its own. Push it in time with one of them and each push adds to the motion already there, so even small forces build up large swings. Push out of time and the pushes partly cancel.

How sharply a system resonates depends on its damping, the losses from [[Friction]], air resistance, and internal flexing. Engineers describe it with the *Q factor*. A high-Q system, such as a tuning fork or a wine glass, rings for a long time at a precise frequency. A low-Q system, such as a car's suspension with good shock absorbers, responds weakly and settles fast. Shock absorbers work by forcing oil through small holes, turning motion into heat through [[Viscosity]].

Resonance can be destructive:

- **Marching on bridges.** In 1831 the Broughton Suspension Bridge near Manchester collapsed under soldiers marching in step. Armies have since told troops to break step when crossing bridges.
- **The Millennium Bridge.** London's footbridge swayed sideways when it opened in 2000. Its natural sideways frequency was close to walking pace, and as it swayed, pedestrians unconsciously fell into step with it, making it worse. It closed after two days and reopened in 2002 with dampers fitted.
- **Speed wobble.** Bicycles and motorcycles can develop a rapid side-to-side shake of the front wheel at certain speeds, an unstable oscillation of the steering. A bike's [[Center of Mass]] and steering geometry influence when it appears.
- **The Tacoma Narrows Bridge**, which tore itself apart in 1940, is often cited as resonance but was aeroelastic flutter, a self-reinforcing twisting driven by wind.

Resonance is also enormously useful. Musical instruments are resonators: a violin body amplifies the string's vibrations at certain frequencies, and an organ pipe's length sets its pitch. Radios select a station by tuning a circuit to resonate at its frequency. Quartz watches count the vibrations of a tiny crystal tuned to 32,768 times a second. MRI scanners detect the resonance of hydrogen nuclei in a magnetic field. Microwave ovens are often said to resonate with water molecules, but they actually heat food through a broad effect called dielectric heating, the same mechanism behind [[Specific Absorption Rate]] limits.

The human body has resonances too. Vertical motion at around 0.2 cycles per second, the slow heave of a ship, is especially likely to cause motion sickness, as the [[Vestibular System]] struggles with conflicting signals. A sound's pitch, and its shift with motion in the [[Doppler Effect]], are both about frequency, the quantity resonance responds to.
