**Radioactive decay** is the process by which an unstable atomic nucleus spontaneously changes into a different one, releasing energy as radiation. Henri Becquerel discovered it in 1896 when uranium salts fogged a photographic plate. Marie and Pierre Curie went on to isolate polonium and radium and coined the word "radioactivity."

Nuclei decay in a few main ways:

- **Alpha decay** ejects a helium nucleus, which a sheet of paper stops but which is dangerous if swallowed or inhaled.
- **Beta decay** turns a neutron into a proton, or the reverse, emitting an electron or positron. A few millimeters of aluminum stops it.
- **Gamma emission** releases a high-energy photon as an excited nucleus settles, often right after alpha or beta decay. It takes lead shielding; see [[Electromagnetic Spectrum]].

The energy comes from mass: the products weigh slightly less than the original nucleus, and the difference leaves as energy; see [[Conservation of Energy]]. Decay deep inside Earth supplies much of the heat that drives plate tectonics; see [[Heat Transfer]].

The key fact is that decay is random and memoryless. No one can predict when a given nucleus will decay, and one that has survived a billion years is no likelier to decay next than a fresh one. Expecting it to be "due" is the [[Gambler's Fallacy]], and atoms are the cleanest counterexample to the [[Lindy Effect]]: their age says nothing about their remaining life.

Across billions of atoms, though, the randomness averages into precise regularity. Each isotope has a fixed **half-life**, the time for half of a sample to decay, ranging from fractions of a second to billions of years. That makes decay a clock:

- **Carbon dating.** Living things take in carbon, including a small, steady fraction of radioactive carbon-14. After death the intake stops and the carbon-14 decays with a half-life of about 5,730 years, so what remains dates bones, wood, and cloth up to about 50,000 years old; see [[Provenance]].
- **Geological dating.** Uranium-lead dating, with half-lives in the billions of years, puts Earth's age at about 4.5 billion years.

The unpredictability has uses too. Some hardware random number generators time radioactive decays to get true randomness, unlike a [[Pseudorandom Number Generator]]. It also causes trouble: alpha particles from trace radioactivity in chip packaging can flip bits in memory, one reason servers use error-correcting memory; see [[Semiconductor]].

Radiation from decay is **ionizing**, energetic enough to knock electrons off atoms and break DNA, which radio waves and microwaves are not; see [[Specific Absorption Rate]]. In the 1920s, factory workers who painted glowing watch dials with radium paint shaped their brushes with their lips. Many developed severe bone damage, and their lawsuits reshaped workplace safety law; see [[Fluorescence]].

Simulating how neutrons scatter through material for the first atomic bombs gave rise to the [[Monte Carlo Method]].
