A **shock wave** is an abrupt jump in pressure, density, and temperature that travels through a medium faster than sound. Ordinary sound is a gentle ripple that moves at a fixed speed; see [[Sound]]. When a disturbance moves faster than the medium can carry the news ahead of it, the compressions pile up into a front only a few molecules thick, across which conditions change almost instantly.

Speed relative to sound is given as the **Mach number**, named for Ernst Mach, who in 1887 photographed the shock waves around supersonic bullets by catching the way the compressed air bends light; see [[Refraction]]. An object at Mach 2 travels at twice the local speed of sound. Its pressure waves can't get ahead of it and merge into a cone trailing behind, narrower the faster it goes, the limiting case of the [[Doppler Effect]].

The **sound barrier** was a real obstacle in the 1940s. Near Mach 1, drag rises steeply as shock waves form over the wings, and aircraft buffeted and lost control; see [[Aerodynamic Drag]]. Chuck Yeager flew the rocket-powered Bell X-1 past it in 1947. Thin wings and swept shapes later made supersonic flight routine for military aircraft.

The **sonic boom** is that cone sweeping over the ground. It is heard as a sharp double crack, and it follows a supersonic aircraft for its entire flight, not only at the moment it passes Mach 1. The United States banned supersonic civil flights over land in 1973, which confined Concorde to ocean routes. NASA's X-59 was built to test whether careful shaping can soften the boom to a dull thump.

Shocks appear wherever energy is released too fast for sound to carry it away:

- **Thunder.** A lightning channel heats air to around 30,000 kelvin in microseconds, and the explosive expansion starts as a shock before decaying into the rumble; see [[Lightning]].
- **Explosions.** A **blast wave** spreads from the burst. In a detonation the combustion itself travels as a shock, unlike the slower flame front of ordinary burning; see [[Combustion]].
- **Whips.** The crack is the tip passing the speed of sound.
- **Reentry.** A capsule returning from orbit compresses the air ahead into a shock hot enough to glow. Blunt heat shields keep that shock standing away from the surface, so less heat reaches the craft; see [[Heat Transfer]] and [[Ideal Gas Law]].
- **Bubbles.** Collapsing vapor bubbles emit tiny shocks that pit propellers; see [[Cavitation]].

Medicine puts them to use: focused shock waves break up kidney stones without surgery.

Light has an analogue. Nothing outruns light in a vacuum, but in water it slows to three quarters of that speed, and particles traveling faster than that emit a cone of blue light, the glow in nuclear reactor pools.
