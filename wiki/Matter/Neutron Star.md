A **neutron star** is the collapsed core of a massive star that has exploded, a ball roughly 20 kilometers across holding more mass than the Sun. It's among the densest objects known: a teaspoon of its material would weigh on the order of a billion tonnes on Earth. Only black holes are more compact; see [[Black Hole]].

Neutron stars form in the explosions of stars much heavier than the Sun; see [[Supernova]]. When such a star's core runs out of fuel, it can no longer support itself against its own weight and collapses in under a second; see [[Nuclear Fusion]] and [[Gravity]]. Electrons are squeezed into protons, forming neutrons, and the collapse stops when the neutrons are packed as tightly as in atomic nuclei. What halts it is a quantum effect: neutrons resist being crowded into the same state, a resistance tied to the limits on how tightly a particle can be confined; see [[Uncertainty Principle]]. If the core is too heavy, nothing stops the collapse, and a black hole forms instead.

The collapse spins the star up and concentrates its magnetic field, much as a skater spins faster when pulling in their arms; see [[Angular Momentum]] and [[Magnetism]]. A newborn neutron star can rotate many times a second, and some rotate hundreds of times. Its magnetic field is typically around a trillion times Earth's, and in **magnetars** far stronger still.

Many neutron stars are **pulsars**. Beams of radio waves stream from their magnetic poles, and when those poles aren't aligned with the spin axis, the beams sweep around like a lighthouse's, reaching a distant observer as regular pulses; see [[Electromagnetic Spectrum]]. Jocelyn Bell Burnell, then a graduate student at Cambridge, noticed the first one in 1967 as an odd, regular blip in survey charts. The signal was so regular that the team half-jokingly labeled it LGM-1, for "little green men," before more were found. The 1974 Nobel Prize for the discovery went to the supervisor, Antony Hewish, and to Martin Ryle, an omission widely criticized since.

Some pulsars keep time with precision rivaling atomic clocks; see [[Atomic Clock]]. A pulsar orbiting another neutron star, found in 1974, was seen to lose orbital energy exactly as general relativity predicts for gravitational waves, the first indirect evidence that they exist; see [[Time Dilation]]. In 2017, detectors recorded the gravitational waves from two neutron stars merging, and telescopes then saw the explosion's light, which showed signs of freshly made heavy elements, including gold.

The material inside, from a crust of iron nuclei to a core whose makeup is still uncertain, behaves unlike anything on Earth. Parts are thought to be superfluid and superconducting; see [[Superconductivity]].
