**Antimatter** is matter built from antiparticles. Every kind of ordinary particle has a partner with the same mass and the opposite electric charge and other opposite quantum properties. The electron's partner is the **positron**, with positive charge; the proton's is the negatively charged antiproton. Combine an antiproton and a positron and you get antihydrogen, an atom of antimatter.

The idea came from mathematics before experiment. In 1928 Paul Dirac wrote an equation describing the electron consistent with special relativity, and it had solutions that seemed to describe a particle with negative energy. Dirac eventually interpreted them as a new particle. In 1932 Carl Anderson photographed tracks in a cloud chamber exposed to radiation from space that curved like an electron's but the wrong way, the first positrons; see [[Cosmic Ray]]. The antiproton followed in 1955 at a particle accelerator in Berkeley.

When a particle meets its antiparticle, the two **annihilate**. Their entire mass becomes energy, typically as gamma-ray photons, in the amount given by E equals mc squared; see [[Photon]] and [[Conservation of Energy]]. Gram for gram, that's about a thousand times more energy than nuclear fission. The reverse also happens: a sufficiently energetic photon passing near a nucleus can turn into an electron and a positron, a process called pair production that converts energy back into matter.

Antimatter is less exotic than its reputation. Some radioactive isotopes decay by emitting positrons. Potassium-40 does this occasionally, so a banana releases the odd positron. Hospitals exploit it in **PET scans**: a patient receives a sugar tagged with fluorine-18, its positrons annihilate with nearby electrons, and detectors catch the pairs of gamma rays flying out in opposite directions to locate active tissue; see [[Isotope]] and [[Radioactive Decay]].

Making antimatter in bulk is another matter. All the antiprotons ever produced at accelerators would amount to a few billionths of a gram, at enormous energy cost, so antimatter engines remain fiction. Researchers at CERN have trapped antihydrogen atoms for minutes using magnetic fields, measured their spectra, and in 2023 confirmed that antihydrogen falls downward under gravity like ordinary matter; see [[Spectroscopy]] and [[Gravity]].

The deepest puzzle is **baryon asymmetry**. The Big Bang should have produced matter and antimatter in equal amounts, which would have annihilated each other almost completely. Instead a small excess of matter, roughly one part in a billion, survived to make everything that exists. Physicists have found small differences in how matter and antimatter behave, but so far too small to explain the imbalance; it's one of the major open questions in physics, alongside dark matter; see [[Big Bang]] and [[Dark Matter]].
