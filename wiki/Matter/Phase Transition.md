A **phase transition** is a change in the state of matter: solid to liquid, liquid to gas, or any of the reverse paths. Ice melting, water boiling, frost forming, and dry ice smoking are all phase transitions. What makes them remarkable is how abruptly they happen. Warming ice from −10 °C to −1 °C changes little, then at 0 °C it falls apart into liquid while the temperature stops rising.

That pause is the signature of a *first-order* transition. Breaking a crystal's bonds, or freeing molecules from a liquid, takes energy without raising temperature, the [[Latent Heat]] of the change. A pot of water stays at its [[Boiling Point]] no matter how high the flame, because the extra energy goes into making steam. The same energy is released in reverse, which is why steam burns are so severe and why freezing water releases heat that protects orchards sprayed before a frost.

A **phase diagram** maps which phase is stable at each combination of temperature and pressure. Lines on it mark transitions, and two points stand out:

- **Triple point.** The single temperature and pressure where solid, liquid, and gas coexist. For water it is 0.01 °C at about 0.6 percent of atmospheric pressure. It was so reproducible that it defined the kelvin until 2019.
- **Critical point.** Above a certain temperature and pressure, the boundary between liquid and gas disappears and the substance becomes a *supercritical fluid*. For water that is 374 °C at 218 atmospheres. Supercritical carbon dioxide dissolves caffeine, which is how much decaffeinated coffee is made.

**Sublimation** skips the liquid entirely. Below its triple-point pressure, a solid turns straight to gas. Dry ice sublimates at −78.5 °C under normal pressure, and freeze-drying works by freezing food and lowering the pressure until its ice sublimates away, leaving structure intact.

Transitions do not always start on schedule. Water can stay liquid well below 0 °C if nothing gives ice a place to begin, and liquids can overheat past boiling, both governed by [[Nucleation]]. Water is also unusual in expanding by about 9 percent as it freezes, so ice floats, an exception to [[Thermal Expansion]] that lets lakes freeze from the top and burst pipes.

Some transitions have no latent heat and happen continuously. Iron loses its magnetism above its Curie point of 770 °C.

The kitchen is full of phase transitions. Butter melting into a sauce, sugar crystallizing in fudge, and fat setting into a stable [[Emulsion]] all depend on them. Chocolate tempering is a solid-to-solid transition: cocoa butter can crystallize in six forms, and only form V gives a glossy snap. Altitude shifts them too, since lower pressure lowers the boiling point, an effect described by the [[Ideal Gas Law]] and its relatives.
