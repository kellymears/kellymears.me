A **chemical bond** is what holds atoms together. Atoms bond because the combination has lower energy than the separate atoms, and the action lies almost entirely in the outermost electrons. The periodic table predicts how many electrons an element tends to gain, lose or share; see [[Periodic Table]].

There are three main kinds:

- **Ionic bonds** form when one atom hands electrons to another. Sodium gives up its single outer electron to chlorine, leaving a positive sodium ion and a negative chloride ion that attract each other. Table salt is a vast, regular array of these ions; see [[Crystal Structure]]. Ionic compounds tend to be hard, brittle and high-melting, and many dissolve in water, which pulls the ions apart; see [[Solubility]].
- **Covalent bonds** form when atoms share pairs of electrons. Two hydrogen atoms share a pair to form a hydrogen molecule; carbon shares four pairs to form methane. Carbon's ability to make four strong bonds, including chains and rings with itself, is the basis of organic chemistry, plastics and life; see [[Polymerization]]. When every atom in a solid is covalently linked to its neighbors, as in diamond, the result is extremely hard; see [[Allotrope]].
- **Metallic bonds** form when metal atoms pool their outer electrons into a shared sea that moves freely among the positive ions. Those mobile electrons let metals conduct heat and electricity and let layers of atoms slide without breaking apart, which is why metals bend rather than shatter; see [[Steel]] and [[Work Hardening]].

The categories blend. Linus Pauling, whose 1939 book on the chemical bond shaped the field, introduced **electronegativity**, a measure of how strongly an atom pulls on shared electrons. When two atoms differ a little, they share unequally and the bond is **polar**, slightly negative at one end. Water is the key example: oxygen pulls harder than hydrogen, which makes the molecule polar and lets it dissolve salts and form hydrogen bonds; see [[Hydrogen Bond]]. When they differ a lot, sharing becomes transfer and the bond is ionic.

Bonds also come in strengths. Double and triple bonds, sharing two or three pairs, are shorter and stronger than single ones and prevent rotation, which fixes molecular shapes. Much weaker attractions between molecules, such as hydrogen bonds and the faint forces that let geckos cling to ceilings, govern boiling points and surface tension; see [[Surface Tension]].

Chemical reactions are bonds breaking and forming. Breaking a bond costs energy and forming one releases it. When the new bonds are stronger than the old, the difference comes out as heat, which is why burning fuel is useful; see [[Combustion]] and [[Oxidation]]. Catalysts lower the energy needed to start the process; see [[Catalysis]]. Running a current through a compound can force the bonds apart instead; see [[Electrolysis]].
