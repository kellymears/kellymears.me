An object is in **free fall** when gravity is the only force acting on it. Near Earth's surface, anything in free fall speeds up by about 9.8 meters per second every second, whatever its mass. A cannonball and a marble dropped together land together; see [[Gravity]] and [[Newton's Laws of Motion]].

That runs against intuition, and for nearly two thousand years the accepted view, from Aristotle, was that heavier objects fall faster. Galileo argued otherwise in the early 1600s, partly through experiments rolling balls down ramps to slow the motion enough to time it. The famous story of dropping balls from the Leaning Tower of Pisa may never have happened. The cleanest demonstration came in 1971, when the Apollo 15 commander dropped a hammer and a falcon feather on the airless Moon and they hit the ground at the same instant.

The reason is that heavier objects are pulled harder but are also harder to accelerate, by exactly the same factor. On Earth, air resistance spoils the equality. Drag depends on shape and speed, not mass, so a feather or a sheet of paper reaches its terminal velocity almost immediately while a stone keeps accelerating; see [[Aerodynamic Drag]] and [[Terminal Velocity]]. A skydiver isn't in true free fall for long. Jumpers from very high altitude, where air is thin, have briefly exceeded the speed of sound; see [[Shock Wave]].

An object in free fall feels **weightless**. Weight as we feel it is the floor pushing back; in free fall nothing pushes, and an accelerometer reads zero; see [[Inertial Measurement Unit]] and [[Vestibular System]]. This is why astronauts float. At the height of the International Space Station, gravity is still about 90 percent as strong as on the ground. The station and everything in it are falling around the Earth, moving sideways fast enough that the ground curves away as quickly as they drop; see [[Orbit]]. "Microgravity" is the technical term, since tiny residual forces remain. Aircraft flying parabolic arcs create about twenty seconds of it at a time for training and experiments, often making passengers sick; see [[Motion Sickness]].

Einstein later described the realization that a falling person doesn't feel any weight as the happiest thought of a lifetime. It became the **equivalence principle**: being in free fall is locally indistinguishable from floating in empty space, and standing on Earth is indistinguishable from being accelerated upward in a rocket. That idea led to general relativity, in which gravity is the curvature of spacetime and free-falling objects follow the straightest possible paths through it; see [[Inertial Frame of Reference]] and [[Time Dilation]].

Tides are what's left over when an extended body falls: the near side is pulled slightly harder than the far side; see [[Tide]].
