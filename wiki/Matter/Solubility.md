**Solubility** is the maximum amount of a substance, the solute, that can dissolve in a given amount of a liquid, the solvent, at a given temperature. Below that amount, more will dissolve. At it, the solution is **saturated**: any extra settles out undissolved, and dissolving and crystallizing go on at equal rates.

Dissolving is a trade of attractions. For sugar to dissolve, water molecules must pull sugar molecules away from each other, which works only if water attracts sugar about as strongly as sugar attracts itself. That gives the chemist's rule of thumb, **like dissolves like**. Water is polar, with slightly charged ends, and forms [[Hydrogen Bond]]s, so it dissolves salts, sugars, and other polar substances. Oils and fats are nonpolar and dissolve in each other but not in water, which prefers bonding to itself. Soap and detergents work by having one polar end and one nonpolar end, letting oil be carried away in water; see [[Surfactant]] and [[Emulsion]].

Temperature matters in opposite ways for solids and gases:

- **Most solids dissolve better hot.** At room temperature, a cup of water holds about twice its weight in sugar, and boiling water holds far more. Table salt is an exception, its solubility barely changing with temperature.
- **Gases dissolve better cold.** Warm soda loses its carbonation faster, and warm water holds less oxygen, which is why heat waves can suffocate fish in lakes. Pressure helps gases dissolve, which is why a sealed bottle stays fizzy; see [[Henry's Law]].

A solution can hold more than it should. Dissolve sugar in hot water, cool it carefully, and the excess stays dissolved in a **supersaturated** state, unstable until something gives crystals a place to start. Then it crystallizes, sometimes all at once. Rock candy is grown this way on a string, honey crystallizes because it is supersaturated with sugars, and reusable hand warmers release heat when a click starts a supersaturated salt solution crystallizing; see [[Nucleation]] and [[Latent Heat]].

How much dissolves is separate from how fast. Stirring, heating, and grinding a solid finer all speed dissolving without changing the final amount, by bringing fresh solvent to the surface; see [[Diffusion]]. Brewing coffee and tea depends on both: different flavor compounds have different solubilities and dissolve at different rates, so water temperature and time change the taste.

Dissolved substances change the solvent's own behavior. They raise its boiling point and lower its freezing point, which is why salted roads stay clear of ice; see [[Freezing Point Depression]] and [[Boiling Point]]. They also drive water across membranes from weaker solutions to stronger ones; see [[Osmosis]].

Particles too large to dissolve but small enough to stay suspended form a [[Colloid]] instead.
