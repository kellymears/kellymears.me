A **surfactant** (from "surface-active agent") is a molecule that gathers at the boundary between two substances, such as water and oil or water and air, and lowers the tension there. Soaps, detergents, and many emulsifiers are surfactants, and so is a substance in the lungs without which breathing would be exhausting.

The trick is a molecule with two different ends. One end is **hydrophilic**, attracted to water, usually because it carries an electric charge. The other is a long hydrocarbon tail that is **hydrophobic**, avoiding water and mixing with oils. In water, these molecules crowd to the surface with their tails sticking out, which interrupts the pull between water molecules and lowers [[Surface Tension]]. Soapy water spreads across a surface and soaks into fabric instead of beading up; see [[Capillary Action]].

Above a certain concentration, surfactants also assemble inside the liquid into **micelles**: tiny spheres with the tails packed inward and the heads facing the water. Grease gets pulled into the oily core, wrapped up, and rinsed away. That's how soap cleans hands and dishes that water alone can't. The same principle keeps oil droplets apart in mayonnaise and salad dressing; see [[Emulsion]].

Soap is one of the oldest chemical products. It's made by heating fats or oils with a strong alkali, a reaction called saponification, and Babylonian recipes date to about 2800 BCE; see [[pH]]. Soap works poorly in hard water, where calcium and magnesium bind to it and form the gray scum of a bathtub ring. Synthetic detergents, developed in Germany during the First World War when fats were scarce, don't form scum and came to dominate laundry and dishwashing after 1945.

Surfactants also take things apart. Many viruses, including influenza and coronaviruses, are wrapped in a fatty membrane that soap breaks open, which is why twenty seconds of washing with soap does far more than water alone. In the 1840s, Ignaz Semmelweis cut deaths on his maternity ward sharply by having doctors wash their hands, and was ridiculed for it. Laboratory detergents unfold proteins so they can be sorted by size; see [[Protein Denaturation]].

In the lungs, a surfactant made of lipids and proteins coats the inside of the tiny air sacs. Without it, the surface tension of their moist lining would collapse the smallest sacs and make every breath a struggle. Babies born very prematurely often don't make enough yet, and treating them with surfactant, standard since the 1990s, sharply cut deaths from respiratory distress.

A soap bubble is a thin layer of water held between two layers of surfactant. Its swirling colors are [[Structural Color]] from the film's changing thickness.

Some surfactants are a problem in themselves. Fluorinated ones used in firefighting foam and stain-resistant coatings barely break down and have spread into water supplies worldwide; see [[Externality]].
