A **photon** is the smallest possible amount of light, the indivisible packet in which electromagnetic radiation is emitted and absorbed. Every photon travels at the speed of light and has no mass, yet carries energy and momentum. Its energy depends only on its frequency: a blue photon carries about one and a half times the energy of a red one, and an X-ray photon thousands of times more; see [[Electromagnetic Spectrum]].

The idea arrived in two steps. In 1900 Max Planck found he could explain the spectrum of glowing objects only by assuming that energy was emitted in discrete chunks proportional to frequency, a trick he regarded as mathematical; see [[Black-Body Radiation]]. In 1905 Albert Einstein proposed that light itself really comes in such chunks, which explained why light below a certain frequency cannot knock electrons out of a metal however bright it is, while dim light above that frequency can. That work on the [[Photoelectric effect]] won him the 1921 Nobel Prize. The chemist Gilbert Lewis coined the name photon in 1926.

Energy per photon, not brightness, decides what light can do to matter:

- **Ionization.** Only high-frequency photons carry enough energy to break molecules, which separates X-rays and gamma rays from the radio waves and microwaves used by phones; see [[Ionizing Radiation]] and [[Specific Absorption Rate]].
- **Chemistry and biology.** Ultraviolet photons can damage DNA directly; visible photons trigger the pigments of vision and photosynthesis; see [[Sunburn]].
- **Electronics.** A solar cell or camera pixel works only with photons energetic enough to free an electron in its material; see [[Image Sensor]].

Photons are also waves. They diffract and interfere, and in the double-slit experiment, photons sent one at a time still build up an interference pattern over many arrivals, though each lands as a single dot; see [[Wave Interference]]. Physicists describe this as wave-particle duality: light travels as a wave of probability and is detected as particles. Electrons and other matter behave the same way.

Atoms emit and absorb photons only at energies matching jumps between their energy levels, which is why each element has its own set of spectral lines; see [[Spectroscopy]] and [[Fluorescence]]. A [[Laser]] works by stimulating atoms to emit photons that are identical copies of one passing by.

Counting photons sets practical limits. In dim light, a camera pixel collects few photons, and their random arrival produces grainy noise that no sensor improvement can remove; see [[Denoising]]. Rod cells in the eye can respond to a single photon, though seeing a flash takes several. Photons also push on what they hit, slightly; solar sails use that pressure for propulsion in space.

Rendering programs that trace light, such as photon mapping, simulate photons as particles bouncing through a scene; see [[Caustics]].
