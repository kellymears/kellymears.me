An **electric motor** converts electrical energy into mechanical rotation using magnetic forces. A current-carrying wire in a magnetic field feels a sideways push. Arrange coils of wire around a shaft between magnets, switch the current so the push always turns the shaft the same way, and it spins; see [[Magnetism]]. Run the same machine backward, turning the shaft by force, and it generates current, the principle of [[Electromagnetic Induction]].

The challenge is keeping the push going in one direction as the shaft turns. Different motor designs solve it differently:

- **Brushed DC motors** use a commutator, a segmented ring on the shaft, and spring-loaded carbon brushes that reverse the coils' current every half turn. They're simple and cheap, used in toys and small tools, but the brushes wear and spark.
- **Brushless motors** put the permanent magnets on the spinning part and switch current in fixed coils electronically, using sensors or measuring the coils to know the shaft's position. With no brushes to wear, they're efficient and long-lived. They power drones, computer fans, e-bikes, and most electric vehicles. The switching is done by [[Semiconductor]] transistors.
- **Induction motors**, invented by Nikola Tesla, use [[Alternating Current]] in fixed coils to create a rotating magnetic field that induces currents in the rotor and drags it around. With no magnets or brushes, they're rugged and cheap, the workhorses of industry, pumps, and appliances.

Electric motors have striking advantages over combustion engines:

- **Efficiency.** Large motors convert over 90 percent of electrical energy into motion. Gasoline engines manage about 25 to 35 percent, losing the rest as heat; see [[Combustion]] and [[Conservation of Energy]].
- **Torque at zero speed.** A motor produces strong [[Torque]] from a standstill, while an engine must rev up and needs a gearbox to cover its narrow band of useful speeds. Most electric cars use a single fixed [[Gear Ratio]].
- **Simplicity.** Few moving parts, little maintenance, no exhaust, and quiet operation.

Running a motor as a generator enables **regenerative braking**: when an electric vehicle slows, the motor resists the wheels and turns the car's motion back into charge for the [[Lithium-Ion Battery]], recovering energy a friction brake would turn into heat. On a bicycle, an e-bike's hub motor can do the same on descents, though the gains are small.

Motors are rated in [[Work and Power]] terms: power is torque times rotational speed, so the same power can mean high torque at low speed or the reverse. Their main losses are heat in the coils' resistance, which grows with the square of current, as [[Ohm's Law]] implies, and they're cooled or limited to stop windings and magnets from overheating, since magnets weaken when hot.

Electric motors use roughly 45 percent of the world's electricity, in everything from refrigerator compressors to hard drives; see [[Vapor-Compression Refrigeration]].
