**Henry's law** states that, at a constant temperature, the amount of a gas dissolved in a liquid is proportional to the pressure of that gas above the liquid. Double the pressure and twice as much dissolves. The English chemist William Henry described it in 1803. In a mixture of gases such as air, each gas dissolves according to its own share of the total pressure, its partial pressure; see [[Atmospheric Pressure]].

Carbonated drinks are the everyday case. Soda is sealed under several atmospheres of carbon dioxide, so far more gas dissolves than air alone could hold. Opening the bottle drops the pressure, the liquid is suddenly holding more gas than it can at equilibrium, and the excess leaves as bubbles. Bubbles need somewhere to start, a scratch, a speck of dust, or a fiber, which is why they stream from fixed points in a glass and why dropping candy into soda produces a fountain; see [[Nucleation]]. Champagne gets its carbon dioxide from a second fermentation inside the sealed bottle; see [[Fermentation]].

Temperature changes the constant. Most gases dissolve less in warmer liquid, which is why warm soda goes flat faster and why heating water produces small bubbles of air well before it boils; see [[Boiling Point]]. The same effect matters in nature: warm water holds less oxygen, so fish in shallow lakes can suffocate during summer heat waves.

The law matters for the body under pressure:

- **Diving.** A diver breathing compressed air at depth absorbs extra nitrogen into blood and tissues. Ascending too quickly lowers the pressure faster than the gas can leave, and it forms bubbles in the body, decompression sickness, known as the bends. Divers ascend slowly and pause at set depths to let it escape gradually.
- **Altitude.** At high elevation the partial pressure of oxygen is lower, so less of it enters the blood in each breath; see [[Altitude Sickness]].

The oceans are the largest example. Seawater dissolves carbon dioxide from the air, and as the atmosphere's concentration has risen the oceans have taken up roughly a quarter of the carbon dioxide released by burning fossil fuels, slowing warming. The dissolved gas forms carbonic acid, making seawater gradually more acidic, which harms corals and shellfish; see [[pH]] and [[Greenhouse Effect]]. Warmer water holds less of it, so a warming ocean absorbs less.

The law is an idealization. It holds best for dilute solutions and for gases that don't react with the liquid. Carbon dioxide partly reacts with water, and gases such as ammonia react so strongly that they dissolve far beyond what the law predicts. It belongs with other simple gas rules that work well in ordinary conditions; see [[Ideal Gas Law]] and [[Diffusion]].
