**Work hardening** is the strengthening of a metal by bending, hammering, rolling, or stretching it at room temperature. Bend a copper wire back and forth, and the bent spot grows stiffer and harder to bend, until eventually it snaps. The metal grows stronger but less able to stretch without breaking; see [[Elasticity]].

Explaining it took a puzzle about crystals. Metals are made of grains, each a crystal of atoms in a regular lattice. Calculations in the early twentieth century predicted that sliding one plane of atoms over another, all at once, should need forces thousands of times greater than real metals withstand. In 1934 Geoffrey Taylor, Egon Orowan, and Michael Polanyi independently proposed the answer: crystals contain line-shaped flaws called **dislocations**, edges of extra half-planes of atoms. A dislocation can glide through the crystal one row of atoms at a time, the way a rug is moved more easily by pushing a ruck along it than by dragging the whole thing.

Dislocations explain both softness and hardening. A well-annealed metal has relatively few, and they move freely, so it bends easily. Deformation creates new dislocations by the million, and as their numbers climb they cross, tangle, and pin each other in place. Each new movement must push through a thicket, so the metal hardens. Eventually no dislocation can move easily, the metal can't deform further, and it cracks; see [[Material Fatigue]].

Other strengthening methods also obstruct dislocations. Alloying atoms of a different size jam the lattice; see [[Steel]]. Smaller grains work too, since dislocations can't easily cross a grain boundary, a relationship named for E. O. Hall and Norman Petch, no relation to the [[Hall Effect]].

**Annealing** reverses hardening. Heating a worked metal lets atoms move; see [[Diffusion]]. Dislocations first rearrange into lower-energy patterns, then entirely new, strain-free grains form and grow, consuming the old ones, a process called **recrystallization**; see [[Nucleation]]. This happens above roughly 40 percent of the metal's melting point measured from absolute zero. Lead's recrystallization point lies below room temperature, so lead never work hardens in daily use and can be bent repeatedly without becoming brittle. The process inspired a search technique, slowly lowering a temperature to settle into a good solution; see [[Simulated Annealing]].

Craft uses both directions:

- **Blacksmiths** forge iron hot, above its recrystallization point, so it can be shaped endlessly without hardening.
- **Wire drawing and cold rolling** harden metal deliberately, giving springs, strong wire, and stiff sheet.
- **Instrument makers and plumbers** anneal brass and copper repeatedly while shaping, reheating whenever the metal stiffens.
- **Shot peening** blasts a surface with small beads, hardening it and adding compressed layers that resist cracking.

Glass has no crystal lattice and no dislocations, so it cannot bend this way at all, and it shatters instead; see [[Glass]].
