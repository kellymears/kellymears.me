**Absolute zero** is the lowest temperature possible, zero on the kelvin scale, or minus 273.15 degrees Celsius. Temperature measures the random motion of a substance's particles, and at absolute zero that motion is at its minimum: the system holds the least energy quantum mechanics allows. Nothing is colder, because no energy is left to remove; see [[Specific Heat Capacity]].

The limit was first inferred from gases. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, experimenters found that a gas held at constant pressure shrinks by the same fraction for each degree it cools. Extending the line downward, the volume would reach zero at about minus 273 degrees; see [[Ideal Gas Law]]. Real gases liquefy first, but the point stuck. In 1848 William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, proposed a temperature scale starting there, and the kelvin unit carries his name. Since 2019 the kelvin has been defined by fixing the value of the Boltzmann constant, which links temperature to energy.

The **third law of thermodynamics**, developed by Walther Nernst in the early twentieth century, says absolute zero can be approached but never reached in a finite number of steps. Each round of cooling achieves less, so the last fraction of a degree recedes indefinitely. It also says a perfect crystal at absolute zero would have zero [[Entropy]].

Zero energy doesn't mean zero motion. Quantum particles keep a minimum jiggle, called zero-point energy. It is strong enough in helium that the element stays liquid at ordinary pressure all the way down to absolute zero; it freezes only under pressure.

Near absolute zero, matter behaves in ways it never does at room temperature:

- **Superconductivity.** Heike Kamerlingh Onnes liquefied helium in 1908, reaching about four kelvin, and in 1911 found that mercury's electrical resistance vanished entirely when cooled with it; see [[Superconductivity]].
- **Superfluidity.** Below about two kelvin, liquid helium flows without friction and creeps up the walls of its container.
- **Bose-Einstein condensates.** In 1995, laser-cooled atoms chilled to billionths of a kelvin merged into a single quantum state. The method of slowing atoms with laser light had won its own Nobel Prize in 1997; see [[Laser]].

Extreme cold has practical uses. MRI magnets run in liquid helium, and many quantum computers operate in refrigerators at around a hundredth of a kelvin, colder than deep space, to keep their qubits stable; see [[Quantum Computing]]. Even deep space is not at zero: the leftover glow of the Big Bang warms it to about 2.7 kelvin; see [[Black-Body Radiation]].

Absolute zero also anchors engine efficiency. The best possible heat engine turns into work the fraction of heat set by the ratio of its cold and hot temperatures measured from absolute zero, so perfect efficiency would require a cold side at zero; see [[Heat Engine]].
