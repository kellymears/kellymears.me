**Centripetal force** is the inward force needed to keep an object moving in a curve. By [[Newton's Laws of Motion]], an object left alone travels in a straight line, so following a circle requires something to keep pulling it toward the center. The force needed equals mass times speed squared, divided by the radius of the curve.

Centripetal force isn't a separate kind of force. It's a role that some real force plays:

- **Friction** between tires and road turns a car or bike.
- **Tension** in a string keeps a whirled ball on its circle.
- **Gravity** holds the moon and satellites in orbit. An orbiting astronaut is in free fall, continuously falling around the Earth.
- **The track itself** pushes inward on a banked turn or a roller coaster loop.

The squared speed term has practical consequences. Taking the same corner twice as fast needs four times the force, and halving the radius doubles it. That's why wet or gravel roads, which reduce available [[Friction]], demand much slower cornering.

**Centrifugal force**, the outward push you feel in a turning car, is a *fictitious* force. It appears only when you describe motion from inside the turning frame. Seen from outside, from an [[Inertial Frame of Reference]], nothing pushes you outward; your body is trying to continue straight, and the car door pushes you inward to make you turn with it. Within the rotating frame, treating centrifugal force as real gives the right answers, which is why engineers use it for spinning machinery.

On a bicycle or motorcycle, riders lean into turns so that the combined force of gravity and the road's inward push runs through the [[Center of Mass]] and the tire contact patch. The required lean grows with speed and tightness of the turn. Riders start a turn by *countersteering*, briefly steering the opposite way to tip the bike into the lean. Velodrome tracks are banked up to about 45 degrees so the track surface supplies much of the inward force, letting riders corner at speed without relying on tire grip alone.

Rotation also creates artificial gravity. **Centrifuges** spin samples so the effective force, many thousands of times gravity in a lab machine, separates components by density far faster than settling would, the same principle as [[Buoyancy]] in a stronger field. Proposed space stations would rotate to simulate gravity for their crews, though a small station spins fast enough to unsettle the [[Vestibular System]].

Roller coaster loops are teardrop-shaped rather than circular so that the force on riders changes gradually instead of spiking at the bottom. Spinning objects carry [[Angular Momentum]], which resists changes to their axis.
