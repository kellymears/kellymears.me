A **gear ratio** is the relationship between two connected gears, or two sprockets linked by a chain, expressed as the ratio of their tooth counts. If a 50-tooth chainring drives a 25-tooth cog, the cog turns twice for each turn of the chainring, a ratio of 2:1. The gears trade speed for force: the smaller gear spins faster with less [[Torque]], the larger one slower with more.

Power stays the same through the trade, apart from friction losses, because power is torque times rotational speed. Gears can't create energy, as [[Conservation of Energy]] requires; they only change its form between fast-and-light and slow-and-strong. The same principle underlies levers and pulleys, where it's called *mechanical advantage*: a longer lever moves through a greater distance with less force. See [[Work and Power]].

Bicycles show why several ratios are useful. A rider produces power most efficiently within a narrow range of pedaling speed, their preferred [[Cadence]], often 80 to 100 revolutions per minute. But road speed varies from walking pace on a steep climb to 60 kilometers per hour on a descent. Gears let the rider keep the legs near that comfortable cadence across the whole range:

- **Low gears**, a small chainring with a large cog, multiply torque for climbing, so each pedal stroke moves the bike a short distance with less force.
- **High gears**, a large chainring with a small cog, cover more ground per pedal stroke for speed on flats and descents.

Cyclists compare gears by *development*, the distance the bike travels per crank revolution, or the older *gear inches*, which express the ratio as the diameter of an equivalent direct-drive wheel. Spinning a lower gear at higher cadence shifts load from muscle strength toward the cardiovascular system, which recovers faster, a trade-off between [[Muscle Fiber Types]].

Drivetrains also lose energy to [[Friction]]. A clean, lubricated bicycle chain is around 95 to 98 percent efficient. Larger sprockets waste less because the chain bends through smaller angles, and *cross-chaining*, using the largest chainring with the largest cog or the smallest with the smallest, angles the chain sideways and adds friction and wear.

Cars face the same problem as cyclists. Combustion engines make useful power only within a band of engine speeds, so transmissions offer many ratios to keep the engine in that band. Electric motors produce strong torque from zero to high speed, so most electric cars need just one fixed ratio.

Gear designers often choose tooth counts with no common factor, so that every tooth on one gear eventually meets every tooth on the other. The extra tooth that guarantees this is called a *hunting tooth*, and it spreads wear evenly instead of letting the same pair of teeth grind together every turn.
