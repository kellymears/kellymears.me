**Lightning** is a giant electrical spark from a thunderstorm, jumping between charged regions of a cloud or between the cloud and the ground. It's the same phenomenon as the snap from a doorknob in winter, at a scale of kilometers and tens of thousands of amperes; see [[Electrostatic Discharge]]. Earth sees about 40 to 50 flashes every second.

The charge builds up inside storm clouds. Strong updrafts carry water droplets high enough to freeze, and collisions between small ice crystals and heavier pellets of soft hail transfer charge between them. Updrafts carry the light, positively charged crystals to the top while the heavier, negative pellets stay lower, splitting the cloud into an enormous battery with a difference of hundreds of millions of volts.

Air is normally an insulator, so a strike happens in stages. A faint channel of ionized air, the **stepped leader**, works its way down from the cloud in jumps of tens of meters. As it nears the ground, the electric field draws upward streamers from trees, towers, and people. When one connects, the circuit closes and a brilliant **return stroke** races back up the channel, carrying a current of around 30,000 amperes; see [[Ohm's Law]]. Several strokes often follow the same path within a second, which is why lightning flickers.

The return stroke heats the channel to about 30,000 °C, several times hotter than the Sun's surface, within microseconds; see [[Black-Body Radiation]]. The air can't expand gradually, so it bursts outward in a shock wave that decays into the rumble of thunder; see [[Thermal Expansion]] and [[Sound]]. Light arrives almost instantly while sound travels about a kilometer every three seconds, so counting the gap gives the distance. A strike into sand can melt it into hollow tubes of natural [[Glass]] called fulgurites.

Benjamin Franklin's experiments in the 1750s showed lightning was electrical and led to the **lightning rod**. A rod doesn't attract strikes; it gives one a low-resistance path to the ground so the current doesn't pass through the building. Cars protect their occupants for a similar reason: the metal body carries the current around the cabin, not, as often claimed, because of the rubber tires.

Damage reaches far beyond the strike point. The huge, rapidly changing current induces surges in nearby power and phone lines, which can destroy electronics kilometers away; see [[Electromagnetic Induction]] and [[Semiconductor]]. The heat splits nitrogen and oxygen molecules, which recombine into nitrogen oxides and ozone, giving storms their sharp smell and fertilizing soil; see [[Oxidation]].

Safety advice is short: if thunder is audible, lightning is close enough to strike. Shelter in a building or car, and wait 30 minutes after the last thunder.
