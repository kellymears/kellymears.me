A **vacuum flask** is a container that keeps hot drinks hot and cold ones cold by surrounding its contents with a vacuum. It was invented in 1892 by the Scottish scientist James Dewar to store liquefied gases at extremely low temperatures, and it's still called a **Dewar flask** in laboratories. A German company began selling it for household use in 1904 under the trade name **Thermos**, which became a common name for the product. Dewar never patented the design and later lost a court case over it.

The design blocks all three ways heat moves; see [[Heat Transfer]]. **Conduction** needs material to carry heat, and the flask's two walls are separated by a space from which nearly all air has been pumped out, so almost nothing is left to conduct through. **Convection** needs a fluid to circulate, and the vacuum removes that too; see [[Atmospheric Pressure]]. **Radiation** crosses a vacuum freely, so the facing surfaces are silvered or polished: a shiny surface emits little thermal radiation and reflects most of what reaches it; see [[Black-Body Radiation]].

What's left are the weak points. The two walls must be joined at the neck, and heat conducts through that join and through the stopper, which is why a flask loses most of its heat through the top. Designers keep the neck narrow and the join thin. Early flasks were glass and fragile. Most modern ones use stainless steel, which conducts heat better than glass but survives being dropped; see [[Steel]].

No flask is perfect, so the contents drift toward room temperature, quickly at first and more slowly as the difference shrinks; see [[Specific Heat Capacity]]. A full flask holds its temperature longer than a half-empty one, because more liquid stores more heat relative to the area losing it. Preheating a flask with hot water helps, since otherwise the inner wall soaks up heat from the drink.

Dewar invented the flask for cryogenics, and it remains essential there. Liquid nitrogen at minus 196 degrees Celsius, and liquid helium only a few degrees above absolute zero, are stored and shipped in large Dewars; see [[Absolute Zero]]. The superconducting magnets in hospital scanners sit inside such vessels; see [[Superconductivity]]. Even a good Dewar lets some liquid boil off, and the vapor must be vented, or pressure would build; see [[Latent Heat]] and [[Boiling Point]].

The same principles appear at larger scales. Double-glazed windows trap still gas between panes, and vacuum-insulated panels appear in some refrigerators and buildings; see [[Vapor-Compression Refrigeration]]. Spacecraft rely on reflective blankets, because in space radiation is the main way heat comes and goes. Animals use related tricks, trapping still air in fur and feathers; see [[Thermoregulation]].
