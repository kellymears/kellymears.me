An **exoplanet** is a planet outside the solar system. Astronomers long assumed other stars had planets but couldn't see them: a planet is billions of times fainter than its star and lies, from Earth's point of view, almost on top of it. The first confirmed exoplanets turned up in 1992 in an unlikely place, orbiting a pulsar, the spinning remnant of an exploded star, revealed by tiny irregularities in its clock-like radio pulses; see [[Neutron Star]].

In 1995 Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz found a planet around 51 Pegasi, an ordinary Sun-like star. It was a shock: a gas giant about half Jupiter's mass circling its star every four days, far closer than Mercury is to the Sun. Such **hot Jupiters** weren't supposed to exist and forced theorists to accept that planets can migrate after forming. The discovery shared the 2019 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Most exoplanets are detected indirectly:

- **Radial velocity.** A star and its planet both orbit their common center of mass, so the star wobbles. Its light shifts slightly toward blue as it approaches and red as it recedes, and spectrographs can detect speed changes of about a meter per second; see [[Center of Mass]], [[Doppler Effect]] and [[Spectroscopy]].
- **Transits.** If a planet's orbit is edge-on to us, it crosses its star once per orbit and dims it slightly, a miniature eclipse; see [[Eclipse]]. The Kepler space telescope stared at about 150,000 stars from 2009 to 2018 and found thousands of planets this way.
- **Direct imaging** blocks the starlight to photograph young, hot planets far from their stars.
- **Microlensing** catches a planet's gravity briefly magnifying a more distant star; see [[Gravity]].

More than 5,000 exoplanets have been confirmed. Each method favors certain kinds, such as big planets in tight orbits, so the catalog is not a fair sample of what exists; see [[Selection Bias]]. Even so, the statistics suggest most stars have planets, and the most common type, between Earth and Neptune in size, has no example in our own system.

Astronomers ask which planets could have liquid water on their surfaces. The **habitable zone** is the band of orbits where a planet would be neither too hot nor too cold, which depends on the star's brightness falling off with distance and on the planet's own atmosphere; see [[Inverse-Square Law]] and [[Greenhouse Effect]]. Many candidates orbit small red dwarf stars so closely that they are probably tidally locked, with one side in permanent day; see [[Tide]].

When a planet transits, a little starlight passes through its atmosphere, and missing wavelengths reveal gases such as water vapor and carbon dioxide. The James Webb Space Telescope, stationed at a gravitationally balanced point beyond the Moon, now makes such measurements routinely; see [[Lagrange Point]].
