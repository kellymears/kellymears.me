**A black hole** is a region of space where gravity is so strong that nothing that enters can get out, not even light. Its boundary is the **event horizon**, the surface beyond which escape is impossible. From outside, an object falling in appears to slow and redden as it approaches the horizon, never quite crossing it, because of extreme gravitational time dilation; see [[Time Dilation]].

The idea has a long history. In 1783 John Michell reasoned that a star dense enough to have an escape velocity above the speed of light would be invisible; see [[Escape Velocity]]. The modern version comes from general relativity, Albert Einstein's theory of gravity as curved spacetime; see [[Gravity]]. In 1916 Karl Schwarzschild found a solution to Einstein's equations describing the field outside a point mass, which implied a horizon. For decades many physicists, including Einstein, doubted that nature would actually produce such objects. The term "black hole" was popularized in the late 1960s.

Black holes form when massive stars exhaust their nuclear fuel and their cores collapse; see [[Nuclear Fusion]]. Supermassive black holes, millions to billions of times the Sun's mass, sit at the centers of most large galaxies, including our own. How they grew so large so early in the universe's history remains an open question; see [[Big Bang]].

Because they emit no light themselves, black holes are detected through their effects:

- **Orbits.** Stars near the center of our galaxy race around an invisible object of about four million solar masses. Tracking them over decades earned a share of the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics; see [[Orbit]].
- **Accretion.** Gas spiraling inward heats up and glows brightly in X-rays.
- **Gravitational waves.** When two black holes spiral together and merge, they shake spacetime itself. The LIGO detectors first measured such waves in 2015, using laser interferometers sensitive to length changes far smaller than a proton; see [[Laser]] and [[Wave Interference]].
- **Images.** The Event Horizon Telescope, linking radio dishes worldwide, released the first image of a black hole's shadow in 2019.

Light passing near a black hole is bent, distorting the view of objects behind it, an effect called gravitational lensing; see [[Refraction]] for the more familiar way light bends.

In 1974 Stephen Hawking showed that, once quantum effects are included, black holes should slowly emit radiation and eventually evaporate; see [[Black-Body Radiation]]. For any black hole formed from a star, this **Hawking radiation** is far too faint to detect. It raised a puzzle, still debated, about whether information about what fell in is lost; see [[Entropy]] and [[Quantum Tunneling]].

Tidal forces near a small black hole would stretch an infalling object along its length, a process nicknamed spaghettification; see [[Tide]].
