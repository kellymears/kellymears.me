**Structural color** is color produced by a material's microscopic shape rather than by pigment. Pigments absorb some wavelengths and reflect the rest. Structural color comes from layers, ridges, or lattices spaced at about the scale of light's wavelength, a few hundred nanometers, which make some reflected wavelengths reinforce each other and others cancel.

The blue morpho butterfly is the classic case. Its wing scales carry tiny tree-shaped ridges whose stacked layers reflect a brilliant blue. Grind the scales to powder and the blue disappears, leaving dull brown, because the structure is gone.

Several mechanisms produce it:

- **Thin films.** A soap bubble or an oil slick on a puddle is a film a few hundred nanometers thick. Light reflecting from its top and bottom surfaces interferes, and which colors survive depends on the thickness and the angle; see [[Thin-film interference]].
- **Multilayer stacks.** Repeating layers, as in beetle shells, mother-of-pearl, and the throat feathers of hummingbirds, act like many thin films at once and reflect one narrow band very strongly.
- **Gratings.** Regularly spaced grooves spread light into its spectrum, as on the surface of a CD; see [[Diffraction]].
- **Photonic crystals.** Opals are close-packed spheres of silica whose regular lattice reflects different colors depending on the viewing direction.
- **Scattering.** The blue of jay and bluebird feathers comes from a spongy keratin network that scatters blue light. Blue eyes contain no blue pigment either; light scatters in the iris, much as [[Rayleigh Scattering]] colors the sky.

The signature of most structural color is **iridescence**, a change of hue with viewing angle, because tilting changes the path difference between the reflected waves. Peacock feathers, mallard necks, and beetles shimmer this way. Some birds and butterflies use disordered structures that give the same color from every angle.

Blue pigment is rare in animals, so nearly every blue animal is blue by structure. Pigments bleach as their molecules break down, but structures persist: fossil beetles 47 million years old still show their original metallic colors.

Some structures also reflect polarized light that certain animals can see and people can't, which beetles and mantis shrimp use for signaling; see [[Polarized Light]]. Showy, expensive ornaments like the peacock's tail are a classic example of [[Costly signaling]].

People have copied the idea in color-shifting paint on banknotes and cars, anti-counterfeiting foils, and dye-free fabrics that never fade. Rendering it is hard for computer graphics, since most [[Shading Model]]s treat light as rays rather than waves; specialized models for thin films and diffraction add iridescence to bubbles and car paint in games and film; see [[Physically Based Rendering]].

Structural color is a reminder that color describes how light interacts with a surface, not a substance in it; see [[Electromagnetic Spectrum]] and [[Metamerism]].
