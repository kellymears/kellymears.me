The **ideal gas law** relates the pressure, volume, temperature, and amount of a gas in one equation: PV = nRT. Pressure times volume equals the amount of gas, in moles, times a universal constant times the absolute temperature. Hold any two quantities fixed and the equation says how the other two trade off.

It combined laws found separately over more than a century:

- **Boyle's law** (Robert Boyle, 1662): at constant temperature, pressure and volume are inversely proportional. Squeeze a gas to half its volume and its pressure doubles.
- **Charles's law** (Jacques Charles, 1780s): at constant pressure, volume grows in proportion to absolute temperature.
- **Gay-Lussac's law** (Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac, 1802): at constant volume, pressure grows in proportion to temperature.

Émile Clapeyron combined them in 1834. The law is called ideal because it assumes gas molecules take up no space and do not attract each other. Real gases follow it well at ordinary conditions and deviate at high pressures or low temperatures near condensation, which Johannes van der Waals corrected for in 1873.

Temperature must be absolute, in kelvin, for the proportions to work. Going from 10 °C to 20 °C is not doubling the temperature; it is about 3.5 percent, from 283 K to 293 K.

The law explains many everyday effects:

- **Tire pressure.** A tire inflated in a warm garage loses pressure on a cold morning, roughly 1 psi for every 10 °F drop.
- **Altitude.** A sealed snack bag swells on a plane or mountain because outside pressure drops while the gas inside stays the same. Lower pressure also lowers water's [[Boiling Point]], so food cooks more slowly at altitude, and air holds fewer oxygen molecules per breath, the basis of [[Altitude Training]].
- **Diving.** At 10 meters underwater, pressure doubles and air in the lungs is compressed to half its volume. A diver who holds their breath while ascending risks lung injury as the air expands again.
- **Hot-air balloons.** Heating air at constant pressure makes it expand and become less dense, so the balloon rises by [[Buoyancy]].

Compression and expansion also change temperature when they happen quickly, without time to exchange heat. A bike pump barrel warms as it compresses air; diesel engines ignite fuel purely by compressing air until it is hot enough. Expanding gas cools, which is the heart of [[Vapor-Compression Refrigeration]] and why a spray can feels cold.

The law connects to deeper physics. Kinetic theory explains pressure as countless molecules striking the walls, and temperature as their average kinetic energy, so heating a gas makes molecules hit harder and more often. Air density from the law sets [[Aerodynamic Drag]], which is lower on hot days and at altitude.
