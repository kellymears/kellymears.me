A **non-Newtonian fluid** is one whose thickness changes with how hard or how fast it's pushed. Water, oil, and honey are **Newtonian**: their [[Viscosity]] at a given temperature stays the same whether they're stirred gently or violently. Many everyday substances don't behave this way, which explains a lot about cooking, painting, and squeezing toothpaste from a tube.

The main types:

- **Shear thinning.** The fluid gets runnier the faster it flows. Ketchup sits in the bottle until shaken or tapped, then pours. Paint thins under a brush, so it spreads easily, then thickens on the wall, so it doesn't drip. Blood thins as it moves faster, which helps it flow through narrow vessels. Long molecules or particles tangle at rest and line up with the flow when sheared.
- **Shear thickening.** The fluid stiffens under sudden force. A thick slurry of cornstarch and water, called **oobleck** after a 1949 Dr. Seuss book, flows like syrup when stirred slowly but cracks like a solid when punched. People can run across a pool of it but sink if they stand still. Under sudden stress the particles jam together faster than water can flow between them.
- **Thixotropy.** The fluid thins the longer it's sheared, and slowly thickens again at rest. Yogurt and some paints and gels behave this way, as does quicksand, which thins when disturbed and then holds whatever sank into it.
- **Yield stress.** Some fluids don't flow at all until pushed past a threshold. Toothpaste stays on the brush and mayonnaise holds its shape on a knife; see [[Emulsion]].

Many of these are **viscoelastic**, partly liquid and partly springy. Silly Putty bounces when dropped but slowly puddles over hours; see [[Elasticity]]. Bread dough stretches and springs back because gluten proteins form an elastic network; see [[Gluten]]. These effects usually come from long chain molecules that stretch and recoil; see [[Polymerization]]. Stir a viscoelastic fluid like egg white or dough with a spinning beater and it can climb up the shaft instead of being flung outward.

The kitchen is full of them. Gravy and custard thicken as starch granules swell and release long molecules; see [[Starch Gelatinization]]. Cream whips into a foam that holds its shape; see [[Surfactant]]. Multigrade motor oils contain polymers that keep them from thinning too much when hot.

Engineers also put these properties to work. Body armor research soaks fabric in shear-thickening fluid, so it stays flexible but stiffens under impact. Concrete pumps and 3D printers depend on mixtures that flow under pressure and hold their shape once placed.

Non-Newtonian behavior makes flows hard to predict, and simulations need models of how each material responds to stress; see [[Fluid Simulation]] and [[Reynolds Number]].
