**Carryover cooking** is the rise in a food's internal temperature after it has been taken off the heat. A roast pulled from the oven at 52 °C in the center may reach 57 °C twenty minutes later. The cause is the thermal gradient. In a hot oven or pan, the outside of the food gets much hotter than the center, and heat flows inward by conduction only gradually. When the food leaves the heat, that stored heat in the outer layers keeps flowing toward the cooler middle until the gradient evens out.

The size of the effect follows from the physics:

- **Bigger, denser foods carry over more.** A thin steak rises a degree or two, while a large roast can rise 5–10 °C.
- **Hotter cooking carries over more.** A roast at a high oven temperature has a steep gradient and a lot of excess heat in its outer layers. One cooked gently at a low temperature has a shallow gradient and little carryover.
- **Timing has to anticipate it.** Since the target is the final temperature, food has to come off the heat *before* reaching it.

The temperature window that matters is narrow. Meat changes texture over a few degrees as different muscle proteins undergo [[Protein Denaturation]], so ignoring carryover is how a carefully watched medium-rare roast arrives at the table medium. *Resting* the meat lets carryover finish and lets the temperature even out, and it modestly reduces juice loss on slicing.

Two techniques flatten the gradient instead of predicting it. **Sous vide** cooks food sealed in a water bath held at exactly the target temperature, so no part can overshoot and carryover disappears. A final sear then provides the browning of the [[Maillard Reaction]]. The **reverse sear** cooks gently first in a low oven, then sears briefly at very high heat, producing an even interior with only a thin overcooked band.

Food safety works on time and temperature together, not on a single number. Bacteria die faster at higher temperatures. Chicken held at 60 °C for about a quarter of an hour is as safe as chicken flash-heated to 74 °C, which is why sous vide can safely serve meat at temperatures that would be risky as a momentary reading.

The underlying behavior shows up wherever a system with stored energy responds with a delay: the output keeps moving after the input stops. Thermostats, braking, and [[Thermal Throttling]] in processors all have to account for it.
