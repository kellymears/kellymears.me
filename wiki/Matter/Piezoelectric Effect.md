The **piezoelectric effect** is the appearance of a voltage across certain crystals when they are squeezed, bent, or twisted, and the reverse: those same crystals change shape slightly when a voltage is applied. Pierre and Jacques Curie discovered the direct effect in 1880; the reverse was predicted mathematically the next year and confirmed by the brothers soon after. The name comes from the Greek for "to press."

It happens in crystals whose structure lacks a center of symmetry, such as quartz, Rochelle salt, and ceramics like lead zirconate titanate. In these, positive and negative charges sit so that squeezing the lattice pulls their centers apart, creating a voltage across the crystal's faces; see [[Elasticity]]. Bone and wood show weak versions of the effect too.

Because it converts in both directions, piezoelectric material can act as a sensor, an actuator, or both:

- **Clocks.** A slice of quartz cut to a precise shape vibrates at one exact frequency, in a watch usually 32,768 hertz, which is two to the fifteenth power and so easy to divide down to one tick per second. A circuit drives the crystal, and the crystal's sharp mechanical [[Resonance]] holds the frequency steady to within a few seconds a month. Nearly every computer, phone, and radio sets its clock from a crystal oscillator. Small differences between oscillators are why networked machines keep correcting their clocks; see [[Network Time Protocol]].
- **Sparks.** The igniter in a gas grill or lighter snaps a spring-loaded hammer against a piezo crystal, generating several thousand volts for an instant, enough to jump a gap; see [[Electrostatic Discharge]].
- **Sound.** Buzzers, cheap speakers, and acoustic guitar pickups use thin piezo discs. Sonar and medical ultrasound send and receive sound with them at frequencies far above human hearing; see [[Sound]].
- **Precise motion.** A voltage moves a piezo element by nanometers, which positions the tips of scanning probe microscopes, focuses some camera lenses, and fires droplets in many inkjet printers.
- **Sensing.** Accelerometers, pressure sensors, and engine knock sensors measure strain by the charge it produces.

A piezo element generates charge only while the force changes; under a constant squeeze, the voltage leaks away. That makes it good at measuring vibration and poor at measuring steady weight, where strain gauges are used instead; see [[Capacitor]].

Floors and roads that harvest energy from footsteps or traffic have been tried repeatedly, but produce very little power. The effect turns small mechanical work into small electrical energy, and [[Conservation of Energy]] sets the limit.

Related effects form a family. **Pyroelectric** crystals produce a voltage when their temperature changes, which is how motion-sensing lights detect body heat. **Magnetostrictive** materials change shape in a magnetic field, the source of the hum from transformers; see [[Magnetism]] and [[Alternating Current]].
