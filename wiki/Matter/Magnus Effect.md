The **Magnus effect** is the sideways force on a spinning object moving through a fluid. A spinning ball curves in the direction its front surface is turning: topspin makes a ball dip, backspin makes it float, and sidespin bends it left or right. Heinrich Gustav Magnus studied it in 1852 to explain why spinning artillery shells drifted.

The cause lies in the thin layer of air that clings to the ball's surface; see [[Reynolds Number]]. On one side, the surface moves along with the air streaming past and carries it farther around the back before it separates. On the other side, the surface moves against the flow, and the air breaks away sooner. The wake behind the ball ends up tilted to one side. Pushing air one way pushes the ball the other; see [[Newton's Laws of Motion]]. The same force can be described as a pressure difference between the faster flow on one side and the slower flow on the other; see [[Bernoulli's Principle]].

Sports are full of it:

- **Tennis and table tennis.** Heavy topspin lets a player hit hard and still land the ball in court, because it dives down after crossing the net.
- **Baseball.** A curveball's spin bends its path by a foot or more on the way to the plate. For decades some argued the curve was an optical illusion; wind-tunnel tests and high-speed cameras settled that it's real, though the sudden late "break" batters report is partly a trick of perspective.
- **Football (soccer).** Free kicks bend around a defensive wall. Roberto Carlos's 1997 free kick against France swerved so late it seemed to change direction in flight; a 2010 analysis showed that a spinning ball that slows down follows a tightening spiral as [[Aerodynamic Drag]] bleeds off its speed.
- **Golf.** Backspin gives a drive much of its lift, and the dimples keep the air attached so it carries farther. Sidespin produces hooks and slices.

The opposite case is instructive. A knuckleball, thrown with almost no spin, has no steady Magnus force, so the seams and the vortices shed behind it push it around unpredictably.

In the 1920s Anton Flettner built ships driven by tall spinning cylinders instead of sails. The idea faded with cheap oil but has returned on some modern cargo ships as "rotor sails" that cut fuel use.

Long-range rifle and artillery tables account for spin drift alongside the [[Coriolis Effect]]. The spin itself stays stable in flight for the same reason a gyroscope does; see [[Angular Momentum]].

Video games that model ball flight, from golf to football, include a Magnus term so that spin feels right, and getting it wrong is one of the first things players notice; see [[Physics engine]] and [[Game Feel]].
