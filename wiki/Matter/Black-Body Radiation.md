**Black-body radiation** is the light every object emits because of its temperature. A perfect black body absorbs all light that hits it and emits a spectrum that depends only on how hot it is. Real objects come close enough that the idea explains why stove coils glow, why stars have colors, and why a thermal camera can see people in the dark; see [[Heat Transfer]].

Two rules describe the glow. Hotter objects emit far more in total, rising with the fourth power of absolute temperature, so doubling the temperature gives 16 times the output. And the peak shifts to shorter wavelengths as temperature rises; see [[Electromagnetic Spectrum]]. A human body, around 310 kelvins, peaks in the infrared at about 10 micrometers, invisible to the eye but bright to thermal cameras. Much of the heat a resting body loses leaves this way; see [[Thermoregulation]]. Iron starts to glow a dull red around 500 °C, then turns orange, yellow, and white as it heats, the colors blacksmiths used to judge temperature; see [[Steel]]. The Sun's surface, near 5,800 kelvins, peaks in visible light.

This scale gives white light its vocabulary. A light's [[Color Temperature]] names the black-body temperature whose glow it matches: candles near 1,900 K, incandescent bulbs near 2,700 K, daylight near 6,500 K. Oddly, in this scale, the "warm" colors are the cooler temperatures. Incandescent bulbs are true black-body sources, which is why they render colors so well, and also why they're inefficient: a filament hot enough to glow sends most of its energy out as infrared heat; see [[Color Rendering Index]]. Lighting designers use the warm end in the evening for comfort; see [[Circadian Lighting]].

Explaining the curve broke classical physics. Calculations from the physics of waves predicted that a hot object should emit unlimited energy at short wavelengths, a failure later called the ultraviolet catastrophe. In 1900 Max Planck fixed it by assuming energy is emitted only in discrete packets, or quanta, with higher frequencies needing bigger packets. He considered it a mathematical trick, but Albert Einstein used the same idea in 1905 to explain how light knocks electrons out of metals, and quantum mechanics grew from there; see [[Photovoltaic Effect]].

The same physics sets Earth's climate. The planet absorbs sunlight and radiates its own heat as infrared. Gases that absorb infrared slow that loss, warming the surface; see [[Greenhouse Effect]].

The most perfect black-body spectrum ever measured is the faint microwave glow left over from the early universe, at about 2.7 kelvins. NASA's COBE satellite measured it in 1990, matching the theoretical curve so precisely that the data points sat inside the line's thickness.
