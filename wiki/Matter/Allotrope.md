**Allotropes** are different forms of the same element, made of identical atoms arranged or bonded differently. The arrangement, not the ingredient, decides most of a material's properties, and carbon proves it most dramatically.

In **diamond**, each carbon atom bonds to four neighbors in a rigid three-dimensional framework of tetrahedra. Every bond is strong and there's no easy plane for the crystal to slide along, so diamond is the hardest natural material. It conducts heat better than copper, because vibrations travel quickly through the stiff lattice, yet it barely conducts electricity, because all of its electrons are locked into bonds; see [[Heat Transfer]]. Its high refractive index and strong dispersion give a cut stone its sparkle; see [[Refraction]] and [[Dispersion]].

In **graphite**, carbon atoms bond to three neighbors in flat hexagonal sheets, stacked loosely. The fourth electron of each atom is free to wander within its sheet, so graphite conducts electricity. The sheets slide over one another easily, which makes graphite soft, slippery and useful as a dry lubricant and in pencils; see [[Friction]].

A single sheet of graphite is **graphene**. Andre Geim and Konstantin Novoselov isolated it in 2004 by repeatedly peeling graphite with adhesive tape, work recognized with the 2010 Nobel Prize in Physics. Graphene is one atom thick, extremely strong for its weight, and conducts electricity remarkably well; see [[Semiconductor]]. Rolled into tubes it becomes carbon nanotubes, and curled into a closed cage of sixty atoms it becomes buckminsterfullerene, discovered in 1985.

At ordinary pressure, graphite is the more stable form, so diamond is technically on its way to becoming graphite. The conversion is so slow at room temperature that it never happens on any human timescale, an example of a form being stable in practice without being the lowest energy state; see [[Phase Transition]] and [[Catalysis]]. Diamonds are made industrially by squeezing carbon at high pressure and temperature, first reproducibly in 1954, or grown from carbon-rich gas.

Other elements have allotropes too. Oxygen exists as ordinary paired oxygen and as three-atom ozone, which shields the planet from ultraviolet light; see [[Ozone Layer]]. Phosphorus comes in a white form that ignites in air and a stable red form used on matchbox strikers; see [[Combustion]]. Iron switches between crystal structures as it heats, which is what makes heat treatment of steel work; see [[Steel]]. Tin turns into a crumbly grey powder after long exposure to cold, a decay called tin pest; a popular story blames it for Napoleon's army losing its buttons in Russia, though historians doubt it.

Allotropes differ from isotopes, which are variations in an atom's nucleus rather than in how atoms bond; see [[Isotope]].
