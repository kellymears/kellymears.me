**Time dilation** is the effect, predicted by Einstein's theories of relativity, that clocks run at different rates depending on how they move and where they sit in a gravitational field. Every process, from atomic vibrations to aging, runs at the rate of the clock beside it.

There are two kinds:

- **Speed.** Special relativity (1905) says that a clock moving relative to an observer runs slow as measured by that observer. The effect is negligible at everyday speeds and grows without limit as the clock approaches the speed of light; see [[Inertial Frame of Reference]].
- **Gravity.** General relativity (1915) says that a clock deeper in a gravitational field, closer to a large mass, runs slower than one farther out. A clock on the ground floor ticks slightly slower than one on the roof; see [[Gravity]].

Both have been measured directly. Muons, unstable particles created when cosmic rays hit the upper atmosphere, decay in a few millionths of a second, too fast for most of them to reach the ground at nearly light speed. Many reach it anyway, because from the ground their internal clocks are running slow; see [[Radioactive Decay]]. In 1971 Joseph Hafele and Richard Keating flew atomic clocks around the world on commercial airliners and found them out of step with clocks left on the ground, by the amounts relativity predicted.

The best-known practical case is GPS. Its satellites orbit about 20,000 kilometers up at nearly 4 kilometers per second; see [[Orbit]].

- Their speed makes their clocks lose about 7 microseconds a day relative to the ground.
- Their height, farther from Earth's mass, makes them gain about 45 microseconds a day.
- The net gain is about 38 microseconds a day.

GPS works by timing signals that travel at the speed of light, about 300 meters per microsecond. Left uncorrected, the error would build up to roughly 10 kilometers of position error each day. The satellite clocks are set to run slightly slow before launch to compensate; see [[Global Positioning System]].

The **twin paradox** is the classic thought experiment. One twin travels to a distant star at near light speed and returns to find the other has aged more. The traveler is the one who turns around, switching frames of reference, which breaks the apparent symmetry. Astronauts who spend a year on the International Space Station return a few milliseconds younger than they would have been, as their speed outweighs their reduced gravity.

International Atomic Time combines clocks from labs around the world, each corrected for its altitude; see [[Clock synchronization]]. The same physics shifts the frequency of light climbing out of gravity, a cousin of the familiar shift from motion; see [[Doppler Effect]].
