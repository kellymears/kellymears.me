**Nuclear fusion** is the joining of two light atomic nuclei into a heavier one. When the product weighs slightly less than the pieces that made it, the missing mass is released as energy, and for light elements the release per kilogram of fuel is several times greater even than fission's; see [[Conservation of Energy]].

Whether a nuclear reaction releases energy depends on where the elements sit relative to iron. Nuclei around iron and nickel are the most tightly bound. Fusing anything lighter, or splitting anything much heavier, moves toward that peak and releases energy; see [[Nuclear Fission]].

The obstacle is that nuclei are positively charged and repel each other strongly. They must collide fast enough to get within the tiny range where the strong nuclear force takes over, which requires temperatures of millions of degrees. At such temperatures matter is a plasma, electrons stripped from nuclei.

Fusion powers the stars. In the Sun's core, at about 15 million kelvin, hydrogen fuses into helium through a chain of reactions, turning some four million tonnes of matter into energy each second. Even that heat isn't enough classically; fusion happens because protons occasionally slip through the repulsion by [[Quantum Tunneling]]. The energy takes many thousands of years to work outward and leaves the surface as sunlight; see [[Black-Body Radiation]]. Stars build heavier elements as they age, up to iron. Most heavier elements form in exploding stars and neutron star collisions, so the carbon, oxygen, and iron in a body were forged inside stars.

On Earth, fusion arrived first as a weapon. The United States tested the first hydrogen bomb in 1952, using a fission bomb to trigger fusion.

Fusion power has been pursued since the 1950s. The easiest reaction combines deuterium and tritium, two heavy forms of hydrogen, and needs a plasma around 100 million degrees, hotter than the Sun's core, because Earth can't match the Sun's crushing density. Two approaches lead:

- **Magnetic confinement.** A **tokamak**, a doughnut-shaped chamber designed in the Soviet Union in the 1950s, holds the plasma away from the walls with strong magnetic fields, often from superconducting coils; see [[Magnetism]] and [[Superconductivity]]. The international ITER project in France is the largest.
- **Inertial confinement.** Lasers crush a tiny fuel pellet so fast that it fuses before flying apart. In December 2022, the National Ignition Facility in California got more fusion energy out of a pellet than its lasers put in, though far less than the facility drew from the grid; see [[Laser]].

The engineering problems remain hard: fast neutrons damage the reactor walls and make them radioactive, tritium must be bred from lithium, and the plasma is prone to instabilities. The old joke that fusion power is always thirty years away endures; see [[Hofstadter's Law]].
