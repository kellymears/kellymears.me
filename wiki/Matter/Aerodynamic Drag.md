**Aerodynamic drag** is the force that air, or any fluid, exerts against an object moving through it. The **drag equation** gives it as one half times air density times speed squared times a drag coefficient times frontal area. The coefficient (Cd) captures shape and the area (A) captures size. In cycling the two are usually measured together as **CdA**, the "drag area", because the rider's posture changes both at once.

The key fact is the exponent. Drag force grows with the **square** of speed, and the power needed to push through it, force times speed, grows with the **cube**. Riding 10% faster against still air takes about 33% more power for the aerodynamic part. Doubling speed takes eight times the power. That is why speed records come so slowly, why a headwind feels so punishing, and why at road racing speeds air resistance is most of the total. Past about 25 km/h on flat ground it accounts for the large majority of a cyclist's resistance, leaving [[Rolling Resistance]] and drivetrain [[Friction]] as the minor terms.

Drag has two sources. *Skin friction* comes from the fluid's [[Viscosity]] shearing along the surface. *Pressure drag* comes from the low-pressure wake behind a bluff body where flow separates. For a human on a bicycle, pressure drag dominates, and the rider's body makes up roughly three quarters of the total, more than the bike. So position beats equipment: lowering the torso, narrowing the arms, and keeping the head down cut more drag than most purchasable parts. Counterintuitively, a rougher surface sometimes reduces drag by making the boundary layer turbulent, which delays separation and shrinks the wake. Golf ball dimples and textured skinsuit fabrics both exploit this.

Air density matters too. Thinner air at altitude reduces drag, which is why several hour records have been set at high-altitude velodromes such as Mexico City, trading less oxygen for less air resistance.

Drag also sets [[Terminal Velocity]]: a falling object stops accelerating once drag equals weight. And because the wake is a region of low pressure and moving air, a second object tucked into it faces much less resistance, which is the basis of [[Drafting]].
