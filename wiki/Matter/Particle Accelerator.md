A **particle accelerator** pushes charged particles, such as electrons, protons or whole atomic nuclei, to high speeds using electric fields, and steers and focuses them with magnets. The basic idea is old and ordinary: the cathode-ray tube in an old television accelerated electrons through a few thousand volts and bent them with magnets to paint the screen; see [[Racing the Beam]] and [[Magnetism]].

Physics drove the large machines. In 1932 John Cockcroft and Ernest Walton used about 700,000 volts to fire protons at lithium and split its nuclei, the first nuclear reaction caused by artificially accelerated particles. Building ever-higher voltages had limits, so Ernest Lawrence's **cyclotron**, developed in the early 1930s, gave particles many small kicks instead, curling them outward in a spiral between two magnet poles.

As particles approach the speed of light they stop gaining much speed and instead gain energy and momentum; see [[Speed of Light]] and [[Time Dilation]]. **Synchrotrons** handle this by keeping particles in a fixed ring and raising the magnetic field in step with their energy. Circular machines reuse the same accelerating sections on every lap, but bending light particles such as electrons makes them radiate energy. That loss, **synchrotron radiation**, turned out to be a gift: dedicated rings now produce intense X-ray beams for studying materials, catalysts and protein structures; see [[Crystal Structure]].

The largest machine is the **Large Hadron Collider** at CERN, near Geneva, a ring 27 kilometers around. Two beams of protons circle in opposite directions and collide inside detectors the size of buildings. Its bending magnets are superconducting and cooled to 1.9 degrees above absolute zero; see [[Superconductivity]] and [[Absolute Zero]]. Shortly after startup in 2008, a faulty electrical connection caused a large helium release that damaged dozens of magnets and delayed operations for about a year. In 2012 the LHC found the Higgs boson; see [[Standard Model]]. Colliders also create antimatter, and CERN uses its antiprotons to make antihydrogen; see [[Antimatter]]. The World Wide Web was invented at CERN as a way for physicists to share documents; see [[World Wide Web]].

Most accelerators, though, are small and practical. Hospitals use compact linear accelerators to aim high-energy X-rays at tumors, and some centers use proton beams that deposit most of their energy at a chosen depth; see [[Ionizing Radiation]]. Cyclotrons in or near hospitals produce short-lived radioactive isotopes for PET scans; see [[Isotope]]. Industry uses beams to implant atoms in chips, sterilize medical supplies and harden plastics.

Nature still outdoes all of them. The most energetic cosmic rays carry millions of times more energy per particle than the LHC can provide; see [[Cosmic Ray]].
