**Dark matter** is the name for an unseen form of matter that astronomers infer from its gravitational pull. Galaxies and clusters of galaxies behave as if they contain far more mass than can be seen in stars, gas, and dust. On the standard picture, ordinary matter makes up only about a sixth of the matter in the universe, and dark matter the rest. What it actually is remains unknown.

The first strong hint came in 1933, when Fritz Zwicky measured the speeds of galaxies in the Coma Cluster and found them moving far too fast to be held together by the visible mass. The case hardened in the 1970s, when Vera Rubin and Kent Ford measured how fast stars orbit in spiral galaxies. By Newton's law, stars far from a galaxy's bright center should orbit more slowly, the way outer planets orbit the Sun more slowly than inner ones; see [[Orbit]] and [[Inverse-Square Law]]. Instead, the speeds stayed roughly flat far out, producing what's called a flat **galaxy rotation curve**. The speeds come from shifts in the light of stars and gas; see [[Doppler Effect]].

Other evidence points the same way. Mass bends light, so a massive cluster distorts the images of galaxies behind it, and the amount of bending reveals the total mass, visible or not; see [[Gravity]]. In the Bullet Cluster, a pair of colliding clusters, the hot gas that holds most of the ordinary matter sits apart from where the bending places most of the mass. Slight temperature variations in the faint glow left from the early universe also require much matter that doesn't interact with light; see [[Big Bang]].

Dark matter is dark in the strict sense: it neither emits, absorbs, nor reflects light at any wavelength observed so far; see [[Electromagnetic Spectrum]] and [[Photon]]. Searches for dim compact objects, including black holes, have largely ruled them out as the main ingredient; see [[Black Hole]].

The leading idea is a new particle that interacts through gravity and perhaps weakly otherwise. The most studied candidate, the **weakly interacting massive particle**, has been sought for decades by underground detectors waiting for a rare collision with a nucleus. None has been convincingly seen. Other candidates include much lighter particles called axions.

A minority of physicists argue instead that gravity itself behaves differently at very low accelerations. Such theories fit rotation curves well but struggle with clusters and the early universe. The debate shows how science handles an anomaly: add an unseen ingredient, or change the law; see [[Falsifiability]] and [[Occam's Razor]]. The first approach once found Neptune from irregularities in the orbit of Uranus. The same reasoning failed for a supposed planet near Mercury, where the fix turned out to be a new theory of gravity; see [[Time Dilation]].
