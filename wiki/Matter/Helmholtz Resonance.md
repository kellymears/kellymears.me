**Helmholtz resonance** is the hollow tone made when air vibrates in and out of the neck of a container, the sound of blowing across the top of a bottle. It's named for Hermann von Helmholtz, the German physicist who in the mid-nineteenth century used tuned hollow spheres, now called **Helmholtz resonators**, to pick out individual pitches within complex sounds; see [[Timbre]].

The mechanism is like a mass on a spring. The air in the neck acts as a plug with mass. The air in the body acts as the spring: when the plug moves inward, it compresses the air inside, which pushes back, and when it moves outward, the pressure drops and pulls it back in. The plug bounces at a natural frequency; see [[Simple Harmonic Motion]] and [[Resonance]]. A larger body gives a softer spring and a lower note, which is why a bottle's pitch rises as it fills with liquid. A longer or narrower neck also lowers the note; see [[Sound]].

Unlike a flute or organ pipe, whose pitch comes from waves bouncing along its length, a Helmholtz resonator can be much smaller than the wavelength it produces. An ocarina works this way, which is why its pitch depends on the total area of uncovered holes rather than on which ones are open. The body of an acoustic guitar also has a Helmholtz resonance through its sound hole that reinforces the low notes.

Loudspeaker designers use the effect deliberately. A **bass reflex** cabinet has a tuned port, a tube open to the outside, and the air in the port resonates with the air in the box to boost bass near the tuning frequency.

The effect also shows up uninvited. Opening one rear window of a moving car can produce a deep, throbbing buffeting: the cabin is the body, the window is the neck, and the air rushing past excites the resonance; see [[Vortex Shedding]] and [[Bernoulli's Principle]]. Opening a second window usually stops it. Rooms and ducts can rumble the same way; see [[Reverberation]].

Engineers also use resonators to remove sound. A cavity tuned to a nuisance frequency absorbs energy there, and such cavities hide as slots or perforated panels in car intakes, jet engines, and auditorium walls. Unlike active noise cancellation, which plays an inverted copy of a sound, they need no power; see [[Wave Interference]].

Before electronics, the resonators were scientific instruments. Helmholtz held a sphere to the ear to hear whether a sound contained its particular frequency, a mechanical way of breaking sound into its parts; see [[Fourier Transform]]. The work fed into an influential 1863 book on hearing and music theory. The ear canal itself resonates, boosting the frequencies most important for speech; see [[Hearing Loss]].
