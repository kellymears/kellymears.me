The **Hall effect** is the sideways voltage that appears across a conductor carrying an electric current when a magnetic field crosses it. Charges moving through a magnetic field are pushed at right angles to both their motion and the field, a push called the **Lorentz force**. This crowds charge toward one edge, building a voltage proportional to the field's strength; see [[Magnetism]] and [[Ohm's Law]].

Edwin Hall discovered it in 1879 as a graduate student at Johns Hopkins, years before the electron was known. The effect later became a probe of what carries current inside materials. The sign of the voltage shows whether the moving charges are negative or positive, and in some metals and in many semiconductors it came out positive, a puzzle resolved by the idea of holes, missing electrons that behave like positive charges; see [[Semiconductor]].

The voltage is tiny in metals but much larger in semiconductors, so practical **Hall effect sensors** are small silicon chips with built-in amplifiers, common since the 1970s. They sense a magnetic field, including a perfectly steady one, without touching anything. Induction-based sensors, by contrast, respond only to a changing field; see [[Electromagnetic Induction]].

Uses:

- **Motors.** Brushless motors use Hall sensors to track the rotor's magnets and switch current through the coils at the right moment; see [[Electric Motor]].
- **Position and speed.** Cars sense wheel speed for antilock brakes and crankshaft position for ignition timing. Laptops and phone covers detect when they are closed from a magnet in the lid. Bicycle cadence sensors count a magnet passing on each pedal stroke; see [[Cadence]].
- **Current.** A sensor near a wire measures the current from its magnetic field without breaking the circuit, which suits battery packs and clamp meters; see [[Lithium-Ion Battery]].
- **Compasses.** Phones measure Earth's field to find north, often with Hall-type magnetometers, and fuse the reading with motion sensors; see [[Inertial Measurement Unit]].

Because nothing rubs, Hall sensors don't wear out, which has made them popular in gaming hardware. Conventional analog sticks read position with sliding contacts that wear, and worn contacts produce stick drift, unwanted movement with the stick at rest; see [[Friction]]. Hall effect sticks avoid that. Hall effect keyboards measure exactly how far each key is pressed, so players can set the actuation point, and a "rapid trigger" mode resets a key the instant it starts to rise, letting repeated presses register faster; see [[Input Latency]].

At very low temperatures and strong fields, the effect becomes quantized. In 1980 Klaus von Klitzing found that the Hall resistance of a thin layer settles into exact plateaus set only by fundamental constants, regardless of the material's flaws. The **quantum Hall effect** won a Nobel Prize in 1985 and now serves as the world's standard of electrical resistance.
