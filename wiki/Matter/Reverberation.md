**Reverberation** is sound that lingers in a space after its source stops, built up from countless reflections off walls, floor, ceiling, and objects. A single reflection arriving late enough to hear separately, more than about a twentieth of a second after the original, is an **echo**. Reverberation is the dense wash of thousands of reflections arriving too close together to tell apart, each a little quieter than the last; see [[Sound]].

The science of it began with a lecture hall. In the 1890s Harvard asked a young physicist, Wallace Clement Sabine, to fix a new auditorium in which speech was unintelligible. Using seat cushions borrowed from a nearby theater as absorbers and timing by ear how long a sound took to die away, he found a simple rule: the decay time grows with a room's volume and shrinks with the total absorption of its surfaces. He then helped design Boston's Symphony Hall, opened in 1900 and still among the best-regarded concert halls in the world.

The standard measure is the time sound takes to fade by 60 decibels, about a millionth of its starting intensity. Good concert halls for orchestral music sit near two seconds. Lecture rooms want well under one second, because long reverberation smears consonants together and makes speech hard to follow, especially for people with impaired hearing; see [[Hearing Loss]]. Stone cathedrals can ring for five to ten seconds, and slow plainchant suits them.

Controlling it:

- **Absorption.** Soft, porous materials such as curtains, carpet, and foam turn sound energy into a little heat. Bass is much harder to absorb, needing thick or resonant traps; see [[Resonance]].
- **Diffusion.** Irregular surfaces scatter reflections in many directions, smoothing the sound without deadening it.
- **Room modes.** In small rooms, low frequencies form standing waves between parallel walls, so some bass notes boom and others vanish depending on where the listener sits; see [[Wave Interference]].

Unlike direct sound, reverberant sound doesn't fall off with distance in the usual way; see [[Inverse-Square Law]]. Far from the source, it dominates what a listener hears.

Recordings are usually made dry and given reverberation afterward. Studios once used dedicated echo chambers, then large suspended steel plates and the springs still found in guitar amplifiers. Digital reverb began with Manfred Schroeder's work at Bell Labs in the early 1960s, using networks of delays to imitate a reflection pattern. **Convolution reverb** goes further: record a room's response to a single sharp click, its **impulse response**, then combine any dry recording with it to hear that recording as if played in that room; see [[Convolution]]. Games increasingly trace sound paths through a level with the same techniques used for light; see [[Ray Tracing]].

Reverberation also shapes how instruments sound, since a room adds its own color to every note; see [[Timbre]].
