**Quantum tunneling** is the ability of a particle to pass through a barrier it doesn't have enough energy to cross. A ball rolled at a hill too slowly will always roll back. An electron sent at an energy barrier too low to surmount usually reflects, but sometimes appears on the other side, without having gone over.

The explanation is that quantum particles are described by waves, which give the probability of finding the particle in each place. Where a classical particle would be forbidden, inside the barrier, the wave doesn't stop abruptly; it fades exponentially. If the barrier is thin enough, some of the wave survives to the far side, and the particle has a chance of being found there. The chance falls off steeply with the barrier's width and height, so tunneling matters at the scale of atoms and is negligible for anything much larger; see [[Wave Interference]].

Tunneling shows up in places that would otherwise be puzzling:

- **Alpha decay.** In 1928 George Gamow, and independently Ronald Gurney and Edward Condon, explained how alpha particles escape nuclei they seemingly can't leave. The particle tunnels out, and the barrier's thickness explains why half-lives range from fractions of a second to billions of years; see [[Radioactive Decay]].
- **The Sun.** Protons in the Sun's core repel each other too strongly to fuse at its temperature, by classical physics. Tunneling lets a small fraction get close enough anyway, and that fraction powers the star; see [[Nuclear Fusion]].
- **Flash memory.** A flash cell stores a bit as electrons trapped on an insulated gate. Writing and erasing push electrons through the insulator by tunneling, and the gradual damage this does to the insulator is why flash wears out after many cycles.
- **Microscopes.** The scanning tunneling microscope, invented by Gerd Binnig and Heinrich Rohrer at IBM Zurich in 1981, holds a sharp tip just above a surface and measures the tunneling current, which changes so sharply with distance that it maps individual atoms. They shared the 1986 Nobel Prize for it.

Tunneling is also a nuisance. As transistors shrank, the insulating layers in them became only a few atoms thick, and electrons began leaking through, wasting power and generating heat. Chipmakers moved to new insulating materials and transistor shapes partly to fight this, and it is one of the physical limits behind the slowing of [[Moore's Law]]; see [[Semiconductor]].

Some devices are built on it. Leo Esaki's tunnel diode of 1957 earned him a share of the 1973 Nobel Prize, and Josephson junctions, in which pairs of electrons tunnel between superconductors, are the basis of many quantum computers' qubits; see [[Superconductivity]] and [[Quantum Computing]].

Game physics borrows the name for a fast object skipping through a thin wall between frames; see [[Tunneling]].
