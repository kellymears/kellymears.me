**Electrolysis** uses a direct electric current to drive a chemical reaction that would not happen on its own. Two electrodes connected to a power source sit in an electrolyte, a liquid that conducts because it contains ions. At the positive electrode, the anode, substances give up electrons; at the negative electrode, the cathode, substances gain them. It reverses a battery, where such reactions run spontaneously to produce current; see [[Oxidation]] and [[Lithium-Ion Battery]].

The first case was water. In 1800, weeks after Alessandro Volta announced his battery, William Nicholson and Anthony Carlisle used one to split water into hydrogen and oxygen. Hydrogen bubbles up at the cathode and oxygen at the anode, twice as much hydrogen by volume, matching the formula H₂O. Pure water barely conducts, so a salt or acid is added to carry the current. Near the electrodes the liquid becomes acidic at one and alkaline at the other; see [[pH]].

Michael Faraday worked out the rules in the 1830s. The amount of substance produced is proportional to the electric charge passed through, so a current measures out chemistry precisely. With William Whewell he also coined the words electrode, anode, cathode, electrolyte, and ion.

Electrolysis underlies several industries:

- **Aluminum.** Aluminum is the most abundant metal in Earth's crust but binds so tightly to oxygen that it was a precious metal until the 1880s. In 1886 Charles Martin Hall in the United States and Paul Héroult in France independently learned to electrolyze aluminum oxide dissolved in a molten mineral. The price collapsed, but the process uses so much power that smelters are built near cheap power such as hydroelectric dams.
- **Chlorine.** Electrolyzing salt water yields chlorine, sodium hydroxide, and hydrogen, the base of much of the chemical industry.
- **Electroplating.** Making the object the cathode in a solution of metal ions deposits a thin layer of metal onto it, as in chrome bumpers and gold-plated jewelry.
- **Anodizing.** Making aluminum the anode thickens its natural oxide layer into a hard coating that resists corrosion and can be dyed, which gives aluminum laptops and water bottles their colors.

Electrolytic hydrogen is also a candidate for storing energy. Running electrolyzers on surplus solar or wind power gives "green hydrogen," which can be burned or used in fuel cells without releasing carbon dioxide; see [[Photovoltaic Effect]] and [[Combustion]]. The obstacle is cost: converting electricity to hydrogen and back loses much of the energy, and the best electrolyzers need catalysts made from scarce metals such as platinum and iridium; see [[Catalysis]] and [[Conservation of Energy]].

Electrolysis needs direct current, so grid-fed cells first convert alternating current, and the cell's chemistry sets a minimum voltage before any current flows; see [[Alternating Current]] and [[Ohm's Law]].
