**Freezing point depression** is the lowering of a liquid's freezing point when something is dissolved in it. Seawater freezes at about −2 °C instead of 0 °C; a strong salt brine stays liquid down to about −21 °C. It's one of the **colligative properties**, which depend on how many dissolved particles there are, not on what they are. The others are [[Boiling point elevation]], lowered vapor pressure, and osmotic pressure; see [[Osmosis]].

The explanation lies in [[Entropy]]. Freezing happens at the temperature where liquid and solid are in balance. Dissolved particles stay in the liquid and are mostly shut out of the growing crystal, which makes the liquid more disordered and so more stable relative to the solid. The liquid has to be cooled further before freezing wins; see [[Phase Transition]].

For dilute solutions, the drop is proportional to the concentration of particles. A compound that splits into ions counts more than once: table salt breaks into two ions and calcium chloride into three, so each dissolved unit has roughly two or three times the effect of a sugar molecule.

Everyday uses:

- **Road salt.** Salt on ice makes a thin layer of brine that stays liquid at temperatures where pure water would freeze. Below about −10 °C, rock salt works slowly, so colder regions use calcium chloride. The costs land elsewhere: salt speeds [[Corrosion]] of cars and bridges and washes into streams and wells, a textbook [[Externality]].
- **Antifreeze.** Engine coolant mixes water with ethylene or propylene glycol, lowering its freezing point and raising its boiling point at the same time; see [[Vapor-Compression Refrigeration]] for the related job of moving heat.
- **Ice cream.** Hand-crank freezers pack the canister in ice and rock salt; the brine gets well below 0 °C and pulls heat out of the cream quickly. Sugar in the mix lowers its freezing point too, keeping it scoopable rather than rock hard; see [[Emulsion]].
- **Living things.** Some Antarctic fish make antifreeze proteins that bind to tiny ice crystals and stop them growing, and wood frogs survive being partly frozen by loading their cells with glucose. These work largely by blocking crystal growth rather than through colligative effects alone; see [[Nucleation]].

A solution doesn't freeze all at once. As pure ice forms, the remaining liquid gets saltier and its freezing point drops further. Sea ice is mostly fresh with pockets of brine, and freezing cider and removing the ice concentrates it into applejack; see [[Fermentation]]. Melting ice also absorbs [[Latent Heat]], which is why a mixture of salt and ice gets colder than either alone.

The same arithmetic, run in reverse, lets chemists find an unknown compound's molecular weight by measuring how much a known mass of it lowers a solvent's freezing point, a technique called cryoscopy.
