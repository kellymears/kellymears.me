A **lever** is a rigid bar that turns on a fixed pivot called the **fulcrum**. Push on one part of the bar and another part moves, with more force but less distance or less force but more distance, depending on where the fulcrum sits. Almost every tool that grips, cuts, pries, or lifts contains one.

The governing rule is the **law of the lever**, first proved by Archimedes in the third century BCE: a lever balances when each side's force times its distance from the fulcrum is equal. A child two meters from the pivot of a seesaw balances an adult one meter from it who weighs twice as much. Force times distance from a pivot is [[Torque]], so a lever is a device for converting one torque into an equal and opposite one. Pappus of Alexandria later credited him with the boast that, given a place to stand, he could move the Earth.

The gain is called **mechanical advantage**, the ratio of output force to input force. It isn't free. The end of a crowbar that you push travels much farther than the end lifting the rock, so the work done on each side, force times distance moved, is the same, minus a little lost to friction; see [[Work and Power]] and [[Conservation of Energy]]. Gears, pulleys, and bicycle drivetrains make the same trade by other means; see [[Gear Ratio]].

Levers are grouped by what sits in the middle:

- **First class: the fulcrum.** Seesaws, crowbars, pliers, and scissors, which are two first-class levers sharing a pivot.
- **Second class: the load.** Wheelbarrows, nutcrackers, and bottle openers. The effort always moves farther than the load, so they always multiply force.
- **Third class: the effort.** Tweezers, tongs, fishing rods, and most of the human body. These lose force but gain speed and range of motion.

The body is mostly third-class levers. The biceps attaches a few centimeters from the elbow, so lifting a weight held in the hand takes a muscle force many times the weight's own; see [[Strength Training]]. The payoff is that a small contraction moves the hand a long way, fast, which suits throwing and swinging.

Renaissance engineers listed six simple machines from which they believed all others were built: the lever, the wheel and axle, the pulley, the inclined plane, the wedge, and the screw. A wheel and axle is a lever that can turn all the way around.

The word has a figurative life too. Leverage in finance means borrowing to amplify returns, and systems thinkers speak of places where a small intervention shifts a large system; see [[Leverage points]]. Balancing a lever also locates a body's [[Center of Mass]].
