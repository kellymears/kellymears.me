In a crystal, atoms are arranged in a pattern that repeats in every direction. The smallest box that captures the pattern, the **unit cell**, is copied over and over like bricks in a wall, so a grain of salt is a cube of sodium and chloride ions extending for millions of repetitions. Salt grains are cubes because the unit cell is; snowflakes have six arms because ice's lattice has six-fold symmetry; see [[Hydrogen Bond]].

The possible patterns are limited by geometry. There are only 14 basic lattice types in three dimensions and 230 ways to combine them with symmetry operations, worked out by mathematicians in the 1890s before anyone could see an atom. Many common metals pack atoms in a few simple arrangements, such as stacked layers or cubes with an extra atom at the center or the faces.

Atoms can't be seen directly with light, but crystals can be probed with X-rays, whose wavelength is comparable to the spacing of atoms. In 1912 Max von Laue showed that a crystal scatters X-rays into a pattern of spots, and the following year William Henry Bragg and William Lawrence Bragg showed how to read atomic spacings from the angles; see [[Diffraction]] and [[Wave Interference]]. **X-ray crystallography** has since revealed the structures of minerals, drugs and proteins. Rosalind Franklin's X-ray images of DNA fibers were central to working out the double helix in 1953.

Real crystals have flaws, and the flaws matter as much as the pattern:

- **Dislocations** are misaligned rows of atoms that can glide through a metal, letting it bend instead of shatter. Bending multiplies and tangles them, which makes metal harder; see [[Work Hardening]].
- **Grain boundaries** separate the many small crystals in most metals. Smaller grains generally make stronger metal, and heat treatment controls them; see [[Steel]].
- **Impurities** added on purpose change a crystal's electrical behavior. Chips are built on single silicon crystals grown nearly flawless, then doped in controlled ways; see [[Semiconductor]].

The same substance can crystallize in different structures, which can behave very differently. Diamond and graphite are both pure carbon; cocoa butter has six crystal forms, and tempering chocolate selects the one that snaps and shines; see [[Allotrope]] and [[Chocolate Tempering]]. Crystals lacking a center of symmetry, such as quartz, generate a voltage when squeezed; see [[Piezoelectric Effect]].

Crystals form when atoms have time to settle into order, usually starting from a tiny seed; see [[Nucleation]] and [[Phase Transition]]. Cool a melt too quickly and the atoms freeze in disorder, giving an amorphous solid such as [[Glass]]. In 1982 Dan Shechtman found **quasicrystals**, ordered but never exactly repeating, which many scientists at first refused to accept; the discovery won the 2011 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
