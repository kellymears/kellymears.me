**Protein denaturation** is the unfolding of a protein's three-dimensional structure. Proteins are long chains of amino acids folded into precise shapes held together by weak bonds. Heat, acid, salt, alcohol, and mechanical force can break those bonds, and the chain unravels. Denatured proteins then tend to bond to each other and tangle into a network, which is *coagulation*. Raw egg white is clear and runny; cooked, it is opaque and firm. The chemistry is the same unfolding and tangling, triggered by heat.

Different proteins unfold at different temperatures, which is why cooking temperature matters so much:

- **Eggs.** Some egg white proteins begin setting around 62 °C, and others only near 80 °C. The yolk thickens around 65–70 °C. That gap is what makes a soft egg, a jammy yolk, or a precisely set custard possible.
- **Meat.** Muscle proteins called myosin denature from about 50 °C, firming the meat while it stays juicy. Actin denatures around 66–73 °C and squeezes water out, which is where meat turns dry and tough. Most of the difference between medium-rare and well-done lives in that window.
- **Collagen.** The connective tissue in tough cuts, the same protein that makes up [[Tendon and Ligament]], shrinks when heated and then, held above roughly 70 °C for hours in moist heat, slowly breaks down into *gelatin*. That is why braises and stocks turn tough meat silky, and why their liquid sets when chilled.

Heat is only one route. Acid denatures fish in ceviche without cooking it. Salt loosens muscle proteins in brining (see [[Osmosis]]). Whisking egg whites unfolds proteins mechanically at the air bubble surfaces, where they knit into foam. Proteins at oil-water boundaries help stabilize an [[Emulsion]], and heat that denatures them too far breaks it.

Denaturation is mostly irreversible in cooking. You can't unboil an egg, though chemists have managed it in narrow laboratory conditions. The same fragility applies inside the body. Proteins essential to life begin to fail at temperatures only a few degrees above normal, which is why [[Thermoregulation]] defends core temperature so tightly and why heat stroke is dangerous. Denaturation also sets the stage for browning: unfolded proteins expose the amino acids that take part in the [[Maillard Reaction]].
