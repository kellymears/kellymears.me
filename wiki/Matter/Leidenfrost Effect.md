The **Leidenfrost effect** happens when a liquid touches a surface much hotter than its boiling point. The bottom of the drop vaporizes instantly, and the vapor forms a thin cushion that holds the rest of the drop off the surface. Because vapor conducts heat poorly, the drop is insulated and lasts far longer than it would on a cooler surface. The German physician Johann Gottlob Leidenfrost described it in 1756, though others had noticed it earlier.

The result is counterintuitive. Drip water on a pan at about 150 °C and it hisses and vanishes in a second or two. Raise the pan above roughly 200 °C, the *Leidenfrost point* for water on metal, and a drop beads up, skates around, and can survive for a minute or more. The exact point depends on the surface, its roughness, and the liquid, and the transition from boiling on contact to floating is called *film boiling*.

The vapor layer also removes almost all [[Friction]], which is why Leidenfrost drops glide across the pan and why they can be steered. On a surface machined with sawtooth ridges, drops propel themselves along, even uphill, a result first demonstrated in 2006. The drop's [[Surface Tension]] holds it in a bead while the vapor flows out from underneath.

Cooks use it as a temperature gauge. The "mercury ball test" for stainless steel pans calls for heating until a drop of water forms a single rolling ball rather than sizzling or splitting. At that temperature, oil added to the pan spreads into an even film and food is less likely to stick, partly because steam from the food's surface briefly holds it off the metal while its proteins set (see [[Protein Denaturation]]). The same principle explains why wet food dropped into very hot oil is surrounded by a jacket of steam, which limits browning until the surface dries (see [[Maillard Reaction]] and [[Latent Heat]]). An oil's [[Smoke Point]] sets the upper bound on how hot the pan can usefully go.

Liquid nitrogen poured on a floor at room temperature skitters around in beads, because the floor is about 200 degrees above nitrogen's [[Boiling Point]].

In engineering, the effect is usually a problem. When metal is quenched in water to harden it, a vapor blanket slows cooling until it collapses, which can leave soft spots. In boilers and nuclear reactors, if heat flux rises too high, the surface becomes covered in vapor instead of bubbles, heat transfer collapses, and the metal can overheat rapidly. Engineers call this limit the *critical heat flux*, and designs keep well clear of it. It is the clearest case where better contact for [[Heat Transfer]] depends on the surface being cooler, not hotter.
