A **rainbow** is a circle of colored light formed when sunlight enters raindrops, reflects off their back surfaces, and leaves again. It always appears opposite the Sun, centered on the point directly opposite it, the shadow of the observer's head. Its red outer edge sits about 42 degrees from that point, its violet inner edge about 40 degrees.

The geometry comes from what happens inside one drop. Light bends as it enters, reflects once off the back, and bends again on the way out; see [[Refraction]]. Rays striking the drop at different heights leave at different angles, but they bunch up near one particular angle, the one at which the exit direction changes least, so much more light emerges there than anywhere else. The rainbow is that concentration of light, a natural caustic; see [[Caustics]]. Because water bends violet slightly more than red, each color concentrates at a slightly different angle, spreading the spectrum into bands; see [[Dispersion]] and [[Electromagnetic Spectrum]].

René Descartes worked out the angle in 1637 by tracing rays through a glass globe filled with water, an early form of what graphics programs now do; see [[Ray Tracing]]. Isaac Newton explained the colors by showing that white light is a mixture. He named seven, adding orange and indigo partly to match the seven notes of a musical scale.

Consequences of the geometry:

- **Every observer sees a different rainbow,** formed by different drops. There is no fixed object to reach.
- **The Sun must be low.** If it is higher than 42 degrees, the bow falls below the horizon, which is why rainbows are morning and late-afternoon sights. From an aircraft, the full circle can appear.
- **Double rainbows.** Light reflecting twice inside the drop forms a fainter secondary bow at about 51 degrees, with its colors reversed. The sky between the two bows is noticeably darker, a gap called **Alexander's band** after the Greek philosopher who described it around 200 CE.
- **Polarization.** Rainbow light is strongly polarized along the arc, so polarized sunglasses can make parts of it disappear; see [[Polarized Light]].

Ray geometry doesn't explain everything. Faint pastel bands sometimes repeat just inside the main bow, called **supernumerary bows**, and they arise from light waves taking slightly different paths through the drop and interfering; see [[Wave Interference]]. They were early evidence that light behaves as a wave. In fog, the droplets are so small that light spreads around them and the colors smear together into a white **fogbow**; see [[Diffraction]].

The rainbow's order of hues has a poorer reputation in data visualization. Rainbow color maps vary unevenly in perceived brightness, inventing sharp boundaries in smooth data and hiding real ones, and they fail for many color-blind readers; see [[Color Scale]].
