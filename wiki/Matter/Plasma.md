**Plasma** is a gas in which a significant share of the atoms have lost electrons, leaving a mix of free electrons and positively charged ions. Because its particles carry charge, plasma conducts electricity and responds strongly to electric and magnetic fields. It behaves so differently from an ordinary gas that it's often called the **fourth state of matter**; see [[Phase Transition]]. The American chemist Irving Langmuir gave it the name in 1928, borrowing a word for the liquid part of blood.

Making plasma takes energy to strip electrons from atoms. High temperature does it: at thousands of degrees, collisions are violent enough to knock electrons loose. So does a strong electric field, which accelerates the few free electrons present until they knock out more in a cascade; see [[Electrostatic Discharge]]. A lightning bolt is a channel of plasma heated to around 30,000 degrees Celsius; see [[Lightning]]. Ordinary flames are only weakly ionized and mostly ordinary hot gas; see [[Combustion]].

On Earth plasma is uncommon, but in the universe it's the norm. Stars are balls of plasma, heated by fusion in their cores; see [[Nuclear Fusion]]. The Sun's outer atmosphere streams away as a thin plasma, and where it meets Earth's magnetic field, particles are funneled toward the poles and make the upper atmosphere glow; see [[Aurora]]. For hundreds of thousands of years after its beginning, the whole universe was plasma, opaque to light until it cooled enough for atoms to form; see [[Big Bang]].

Each kind of atom in a plasma emits light at particular colors as its electrons drop back to lower energies, which is why neon signs glow red-orange and sodium streetlights glow yellow; see [[Spectroscopy]]. Fluorescent lamps use a mercury plasma that emits ultraviolet light, converted to visible light by a coating inside the tube; see [[Fluorescence]].

Industry relies on plasma heavily. Plasma etching carves the microscopic patterns on computer chips, and plasma torches cut through thick metal; see [[Semiconductor]]. Grapes in a microwave oven can spark a small ball of plasma, a kitchen trick that has been the subject of published research; see [[Microwave Heating]].

Containing hot plasma is the central challenge of fusion power. At the temperatures needed, over 100 million degrees, no material can touch it, so experimental reactors hold it with powerful magnetic fields; see [[Superconductivity]]. Plasma is notoriously unstable, wriggling out of magnetic traps, and controlling it remains a major engineering problem.

Plasma also interferes with radio. Spacecraft reentering the atmosphere are wrapped in plasma that blocks communication for minutes, and solar storms that disturb the charged upper layers of the atmosphere can disrupt radio and navigation signals; see [[Global Positioning System]].
