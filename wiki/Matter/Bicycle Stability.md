**Bicycle stability** is the question of why a moving bicycle stays upright. A stationary bike falls over immediately, yet a bike rolling at moderate speed can balance itself, even without a rider: pushed off riderless, it often wobbles, straightens up, and rolls on until it slows. Explaining this has been surprisingly hard, and the popular explanations turned out to be incomplete.

Two answers were long repeated:

- **The gyroscopic effect.** Spinning wheels resist tilting, through [[Angular Momentum]], and when tipped they steer into the lean.
- **Trail.** The front wheel touches the ground behind the line of the steering axis, so the wheel is pulled behind its pivot like a caster on a shopping cart, which tends to straighten it.

In 1970 the chemist David Jones built bikes to test these ideas and found that cancelling the gyroscopic effect with a counter-rotating wheel left a bike rideable. Then, in 2011, a team led by Jodi Kooijman and Andy Ruina published a study in *Science* describing a bicycle with tiny counter-rotating wheels, no gyroscopic effect, and slightly negative trail. It still balanced itself. Neither popular explanation was necessary.

What actually matters is how steering responds to a lean. A self-stable bike steers into a fall: when it tips left, the front assembly turns left, bringing the wheels back under the center of mass. Gyroscopic effects, trail, and the placement of mass in the front assembly all contribute, and the right combination produces stability within a range of speeds.

A rider adds active balance on top. Mostly unconsciously, riders steer the wheels under their [[Center of Mass]], using signals from the [[Vestibular System]] and [[Proprioception]]. This is a learned skill, an example of [[Motor Learning]], and why balance bikes teach children faster than training wheels, which prevent the very leaning that steering responds to.

Turning reveals the most counterintuitive part, **countersteering**. To turn left at speed, a rider briefly pushes the handlebar left, steering the front wheel right. The wheels move out from under the bike, which leans left, and only then does the rider steer into the turn. Most cyclists do this without knowing.

The lean angle in a steady turn balances gravity against the [[Centripetal Force]] needed to curve, set by speed and turn radius. Faster or tighter turns require more lean, up to the limits of tire grip, governed by [[Friction]].

Frame geometry shapes how a bike feels. More trail makes steering slower and steadier, suited to descending and loaded touring; less trail makes it quicker and more nervous. Speed matters too: very slow bikes are unstable and need constant correction, and at some speeds a bike can develop a high-speed wobble, an oscillation related to [[Resonance]] that can be frightening on fast descents.
