**Sound** is a pressure wave traveling through a medium such as air, water, or a solid. A vibrating object, a guitar string or a speaker cone, pushes on neighboring air molecules, compressing them, then pulls back, leaving them sparser. These compressions and rarefactions pass from molecule to molecule outward, while the air itself only jiggles in place. Sound needs a medium; in a vacuum there's none.

Its main properties:

- **Speed.** In air at room temperature, sound travels about 343 meters per second, about a kilometer every three seconds, which is why counting seconds after lightning estimates its distance. It's faster in warmer air, since molecules move faster, roughly four times faster in water, and faster still in steel, which is stiffer; see [[Elasticity]].
- **Frequency.** Vibrations per second, in hertz, which we hear as pitch. Human hearing spans roughly 20 to 20,000 Hz, with the upper limit falling with age. Musical notes are fixed frequencies, and an octave doubles the frequency.
- **Timbre.** Real sources vibrate at many frequencies at once, a fundamental plus overtones, and their mix makes a violin and a flute playing the same note sound different. Instruments shape this by [[Resonance]] in their bodies and air columns, and the pure tone of a tuning fork is [[Simple Harmonic Motion]].

**Loudness** is measured in **decibels**, a logarithmic scale. Every 10 dB is ten times the sound intensity; 20 dB is a hundred times. Human hearing spans an enormous range, from the threshold of hearing at 0 dB to pain near 120 to 130 dB, a trillion-fold range in intensity, which a linear scale would handle awkwardly. Perceived loudness roughly doubles with every 10 dB. Prolonged exposure above about 85 dB damages hearing; see [[Hearing Loss]].

Sound behaves like other waves:

- It spreads and fades, its intensity from a point source falling with the [[Inverse-Square Law]], so doubling distance drops it about 6 dB.
- It reflects, making echoes and the reverberation that gives rooms their character.
- It bends around obstacles, since audible wavelengths range from centimeters to meters, which is why you hear around corners but can't see around them; see [[Diffraction]].
- It bends through layers of different temperature by [[Refraction]], carrying sound farther over cool water at night.
- Waves combine; noise-canceling headphones play an inverted copy of incoming sound, a use of [[Wave Interference]].
- A moving source shifts pitch, the [[Doppler Effect]] of a passing siren.

Digital audio samples sound pressure many thousands of times a second. CD audio uses 44,100 samples per second, just over twice the upper limit of hearing, which is the minimum needed to capture it without [[Aliasing]]. Lossy formats such as MP3 discard sounds that louder ones mask; see [[Data Compression]].
