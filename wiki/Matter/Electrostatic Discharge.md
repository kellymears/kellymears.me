**Electrostatic discharge** (ESD) is the sudden flow of electric charge between two objects at different voltages, usually as a spark. Walk across a carpet in winter, reach for a doorknob, and feel the snap: that is ESD. The charge built up on your body finds a path to ground the moment your finger gets close enough for the air gap to break down.

The charge comes from the **triboelectric effect**. When two materials touch and separate, electrons tend to move from one to the other, depending on how tightly each holds its electrons. Rubbing helps by increasing contact. Materials can be ranked in a *triboelectric series*: wool, hair, and nylon tend to give up electrons, while rubber, polyester, and PTFE tend to take them. Shoe soles on carpet, a sweater pulled over the head, and a plastic slide all separate charge efficiently.

Charge leaks away through moisture on surfaces and in the air, so static problems depend heavily on humidity. In humid summer air, charge drains as fast as it builds. In dry winter air, especially indoors when heating lowers the relative humidity tracked by [[Dew Point]], a person can reach 10,000 volts or more. The voltage sounds alarming, but the total charge is tiny, so the current lasts nanoseconds and carries little energy. People generally don't feel a discharge below about 3,000 volts.

Electronics are far more fragile. The insulating layers in modern chips are only a few nanometers thick, and discharges of under 100 volts, well below what a person can feel, can punch through them. Some damage is immediate; some is *latent*, weakening a part so it fails weeks later, a hidden cousin of the slow wear-out caused by [[Electromigration]]. That is why components ship in antistatic bags, technicians wear grounded wrist straps, and assembly floors control humidity and use conductive mats. Chips also include protection circuits at every pin that shunt sudden voltage spikes away from the delicate interior, following the same logic as [[Ohm's Law]]: give the current an easier path.

Sparks are also an ignition source. ESD can ignite gasoline vapor at a fuel pump, solvent fumes, or fine dust in grain silos and flour mills, where suspended particles can explode, a case of [[Combustion]] with an enormous surface area. Industries handling flammable materials ground and bond all equipment so charge can't accumulate.

Static electricity is also put to work. Laser printers and photocopiers use charged drums to attract toner to exactly where the image should be. Electrostatic precipitators pull soot from smokestack exhaust, and electrostatic spray painting wraps paint around a grounded object with little waste. Lightning is the same phenomenon at the scale of a storm cloud, where colliding ice particles separate charge until the air breaks down over kilometers.
