**Vortex shedding** is the way a fluid flowing past a blunt object, such as a cylinder, peels off swirling vortices alternately from one side and then the other. Behind the object, the vortices form two staggered rows of opposite spin, a pattern called a **Kármán vortex street** after the Hungarian-American engineer Theodore von Kármán, who analyzed it in 1911 and 1912.

It happens because the flow can't follow the back of a blunt body. Fluid sliding along the surface slows and separates, leaving a low-pressure region behind; see [[Bernoulli's Principle]] and [[Aerodynamic Drag]]. The layers peeling from each side roll up into vortices, and a vortex forming on one side disturbs the other, so they detach in turn rather than together. The pattern appears only within a range of flow conditions: at very low speeds the flow stays smooth, and at high speeds the wake becomes turbulent, though shedding often persists within it; see [[Reynolds Number]].

The shedding rate is predictable. For a cylinder over a wide range of flows, vortices peel off at a frequency proportional to the flow speed divided by the diameter, with a constant of proportionality, the Strouhal number, close to 0.2. Faster wind or thinner wires mean higher frequencies.

Each vortex leaving one side pushes the object sideways, so shedding produces an alternating side force. Telephone wires singing in the wind and the hum of a car's roof rack are vortex shedding made audible; see [[Sound]]. If the shedding frequency matches a structure's natural frequency, the small pushes add up; see [[Resonance]]. Tall chimneys, towers, and cables can then swing dangerously, a problem called **vortex-induced vibration**, which can also crack them through repeated stress; see [[Material Fatigue]]. Many tall steel chimneys carry a spiral fin around their upper part, which breaks up the pattern so vortices don't shed in step along the height.

The 1940 collapse of the Tacoma Narrows Bridge is often blamed on vortex shedding, but engineers generally attribute the final failure to a related effect, aerodynamic flutter, in which the bridge's own twisting drew ever more energy from the wind; see [[Feedback Loop]].

Vortex streets appear at every scale. Satellite images show them in clouds downwind of mountainous islands, with vortices tens of kilometers across. Fish exploit them: a trout holding station behind a rock can slalom between the vortices and save effort, and many fish swim by shedding a vortex street of their own, with the spins reversed; see [[Drafting]]. A spinning object sheds vortices unevenly, producing the sideways force that curves a spinning ball; see [[Magnus Effect]].

The effect is useful too. Some flow meters measure the speed of a fluid in a pipe by counting the vortices that peel off a bar placed across it.
