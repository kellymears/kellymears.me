**Tempering** chocolate means taking it through a sequence of temperatures so that its cocoa butter sets in one particular crystal structure. Well-tempered chocolate is glossy, breaks with a clean snap, shrinks slightly from its mold, and melts in the mouth but not at the touch. Chocolate that is simply melted and left to cool sets dull, soft, and streaky.

The reason is that cocoa butter can crystallize in six different forms, numbered I to VI. The fat molecules are the same in each, but they pack together differently, much as carbon can form graphite or diamond. Each form has its own melting point, rising from about 17 °C for form I to about 36 °C for form VI. Form V, which melts at about 34 °C, just below body temperature, gives the qualities chocolatiers want; see [[Phase Transition]].

Left alone, cooling chocolate forms a mixture of unstable crystals. Tempering steers it to form V:

1. **Melt** completely, to about 45–50 °C for dark chocolate, erasing every existing crystal.
2. **Cool** to about 27 °C while stirring, so that crystals begin to form, both stable and unstable.
3. **Rewarm** to about 31–32 °C, which melts the unstable forms and leaves form V crystals behind.

The surviving crystals act as templates. When the chocolate sets, new fat molecules line up on them in the same form; see [[Nucleation]]. A common shortcut, called seeding, is to stir finely chopped tempered chocolate into melted chocolate as it cools, supplying form V crystals directly. Milk and white chocolate are tempered a few degrees lower, because milk fat interferes with cocoa butter's crystals. Crystallizing releases heat, so chocolate can warm itself while setting; see [[Latent Heat]].

Form V packs more densely than liquid fat, so tempered chocolate contracts as it sets, which lets it release cleanly from molds; see [[Thermal Expansion]]. The tight crystal packing also gives a smooth, light-reflecting surface, which is the gloss.

Two kinds of bloom spoil chocolate over time:

- **Fat bloom** is a gray-white streaking. Through temperature swings or long storage, form V slowly converts to the more stable form VI, and fat migrates to the surface in crystals large enough to scatter light.
- **Sugar bloom** is a dry, dusty coating. Moisture, often condensation after refrigeration, dissolves sugar at the surface, and it recrystallizes as the water evaporates; see [[Dew Point]].

Bloomed chocolate is safe to eat and can be melted and tempered again.

The word is shared with metalworking, where tempering steel means reheating hardened steel to make it less brittle; see [[Steel]]. In both cases the goal is to control crystal structure through heat. Chocolate's flavor is set earlier, by fermenting and roasting the cocoa beans; see [[Fermentation]] and [[Maillard Reaction]].
