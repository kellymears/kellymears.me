**pH** measures how acidic or alkaline a water-based solution is. It is the negative logarithm of the concentration of hydrogen ions, so lower numbers mean more acidity. Pure water at 25 °C has a pH of 7, called neutral; acids sit below it and bases, or alkalis, above. The Danish chemist Søren Sørensen introduced the scale in 1909 while studying proteins in beer brewing at the Carlsberg Laboratory.

Because the scale is logarithmic, each step is a factor of ten. A solution at pH 3 has ten times the hydrogen ions of one at pH 4 and a hundred times that of pH 5. Small-looking changes are therefore large: the ocean's average pH falling from about 8.2 to 8.1 since the industrial era is roughly a 26 percent increase in acidity, a gap between how a number looks and what it means that also trips people up with a [[Percentage Point]].

Some reference points:

- **About 1.5 to 3.5:** stomach acid.
- **About 2:** lemon juice; vinegar is close behind.
- **About 5:** black coffee.
- **7.35 to 7.45:** human blood, held in a narrow band.
- **About 8.3:** baking soda in water.
- **13 to 14:** lye, used for pretzels and drain cleaner.

Blood stays in its narrow range thanks to **buffers**, mixtures that absorb added acid or base with little change in pH. The bicarbonate system, working with the lungs and kidneys, is a textbook case of [[Homeostasis]]; strenuous exercise pushes against it as muscles produce acid faster than it is cleared, part of what [[Lactate Threshold]] describes.

pH quietly controls a great deal of cooking:

- **Browning.** The [[Maillard Reaction]] speeds up in alkaline conditions. A pinch of baking soda browns onions faster, and dipping pretzels in lye gives their deep mahogany crust.
- **Leavening.** Baking soda is a base that releases carbon dioxide when it meets an acid such as buttermilk, yogurt, or the acid salts in baking powder.
- **Protein.** Acid denatures proteins, which is how lime juice "cooks" fish in ceviche and how a splash of vinegar helps poached eggs set, an instance of [[Protein Denaturation]].
- **Color.** Red cabbage turns blue in alkaline water and pink in acid, a natural pH indicator.
- **Fermentation and preservation.** Lactic acid bacteria in sourdough, yogurt, and pickles lower pH until spoilage organisms cannot grow, the basis of [[Fermentation]] as preservation.

Taste tracks pH too: sourness is the tongue detecting hydrogen ions, one of the basic tastes alongside [[Umami]]. The gut depends on pH changes along its length, from the acidic stomach to the more neutral intestines where the [[Gut Microbiome]] lives.

Acids and bases react with metals and other substances in ways closely tied to [[Oxidation]].
