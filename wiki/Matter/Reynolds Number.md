The **Reynolds number** predicts how a fluid will flow: smoothly, in parallel layers, or chaotically, in swirling eddies. It is a ratio of two effects. *Inertia* keeps moving fluid moving and tends to amplify disturbances. *[[Viscosity]]*, the fluid's internal friction, damps them out. The Reynolds number is the fluid's density times its speed times a typical length, divided by its viscosity. When it is low, viscosity wins and the flow is **laminar**. When it is high, inertia wins and the flow becomes **turbulent**.

Osborne Reynolds demonstrated this in 1883 by injecting a thin stream of dye into water flowing through a glass pipe. At low speeds the dye stayed a neat straight line. As he increased the speed, the line began to waver and then suddenly burst into clouds that filled the pipe. In a pipe, flow is reliably laminar below a Reynolds number of about 2,300 and turbulent above roughly 4,000. Rising smoke that abruptly breaks into curls shows the same transition.

Because the number has no units, it lets very different situations be compared. Two flows with the same Reynolds number behave alike, which is why wind-tunnel tests on scale models work only if the conditions are adjusted to match the full-size number.

The extremes are strikingly different worlds:

- **Low.** A swimming bacterium has a Reynolds number around 0.0001. Inertia is irrelevant: stop swimming and it stops within a fraction of an atom's width. The physicist Edward Purcell, in a 1977 lecture called "Life at Low Reynolds Number," showed that a back-and-forth stroke, like a scallop opening and closing, gets nowhere, so microbes use rotating corkscrew flagella.
- **High.** A cyclist, a car, or a plane operates in the hundreds of thousands to millions, deep in the turbulent regime, where [[Aerodynamic Drag]] dominates and [[Drafting]] in a wake pays off.

Near any surface sits a thin **boundary layer** where the fluid slows to a stop against the wall, and whether it is laminar or turbulent changes drag dramatically. Golf ball dimples deliberately make it turbulent. A turbulent boundary layer clings to the ball longer before separating, which shrinks the wake behind it and roughly halves drag. Textured cycling skinsuits use the same trick.

In the body, blood flow is mostly laminar. Turbulence in narrowed arteries or faulty valves is audible: heart murmurs are turbulence, and the sounds heard while measuring [[Blood Pressure]] come from blood spurting turbulently through a squeezed artery. Turbulence also mixes far better than laminar flow, which matters for [[Heat Transfer]] and for [[Combustion]], where engines create turbulence to burn fuel quickly.

Turbulence remains famously unsolved. Its governing equations are known, but whether their solutions always stay well behaved is an open Millennium Prize problem, and predicting turbulent flow still means heavy simulation.
