**Steel** is iron mixed with a small amount of carbon, usually less than 1%. That little carbon transforms the metal. Pure iron is soft and bends easily; steel can be made strong enough for bridge cables, hard enough for knife edges, or tough enough for car frames that crumple without shattering. It's the most widely used metal and the most recycled material in the world.

The carbon works by getting in the way. Metals bend when planes of atoms slip past each other along defects in their crystals. Carbon atoms, much smaller than iron atoms, wedge into the gaps between them and pin those defects in place, so it takes more force to deform the metal. An **alloy** like this, a metal mixed with other elements, is often stronger than any of its ingredients. More carbon makes steel harder but more brittle; cast iron, with 2 to 4%, is hard but cracks rather than bends.

What makes steel remarkable is heat treatment. Iron's crystal structure rearranges above about 727 °C into a form that dissolves much more carbon; see [[Phase Transition]]. What happens on cooling decides the result:

- **Slow cooling** lets the carbon move out into separate layers, giving soft, workable steel.
- **Quenching** in water or oil traps the carbon in a strained, very hard structure, but one too brittle for most uses.
- **Tempering** reheats quenched steel to a few hundred degrees, letting some strain relax, trading a little hardness for much more toughness.
- **Annealing** heats and cools slowly to soften steel and relieve internal stresses. The process inspired a search algorithm of the same name; see [[Simulated Annealing]].

Blacksmiths judged these temperatures by the color of the glow; see [[Black-Body Radiation]]. Carbon can also be added only to the surface by heating steel in carbon-rich surroundings, giving a hard skin over a tough core; see [[Diffusion]].

Cheap steel arrived with Henry Bessemer's process in 1856, which blew air through molten iron to burn off excess carbon; see [[Combustion]]. Steel railways and skyscrapers followed within decades.

Ordinary steel rusts; see [[Oxidation]]. **Stainless steel** adds at least about 10.5% chromium, which forms a thin, self-healing oxide film. Common kitchen stainless grades aren't magnetic, which is why fridge magnets slide off some appliance fronts; see [[Magnetism]].

Two properties surprise people. Every steel has nearly the same stiffness, whatever its strength, so a stronger steel flexes just as much under a load but survives more before bending permanently; see [[Elasticity]]. And steel has a fatigue limit: loads below it can repeat almost forever without causing cracks, unlike aluminum, one reason steel bicycle frames last for decades; see [[Material Fatigue]].
