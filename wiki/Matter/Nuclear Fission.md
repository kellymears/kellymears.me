**Nuclear fission** is the splitting of a heavy atomic nucleus into two lighter ones. When a nucleus of uranium-235 absorbs a slow neutron, it becomes unstable and breaks apart, releasing about two hundred million electron volts of energy, tens of millions of times more than any chemical reaction per atom, plus two or three free neutrons; see [[Radioactive Decay]].

It was discovered in Berlin in December 1938, when the chemists Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann found barium among the products of uranium bombarded with neutrons. The physicists Lise Meitner and Otto Frisch explained what had happened weeks later and named it fission, after cell division. Hahn alone received the 1944 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, an omission widely regarded as one of the prize's injustices.

The energy comes from mass. The fragments together weigh slightly less than the original nucleus and neutron, and the missing mass becomes energy, mostly as the fragments' motion, which turns into heat; see [[Conservation of Energy]].

The spare neutrons make a **chain reaction** possible. If on average more than one neutron from each fission goes on to cause another, the reaction grows exponentially; if fewer, it dies out; see [[Positive feedback]]. The smallest amount of material that sustains a chain reaction is the **critical mass**, which depends on shape, density, and surroundings.

Two applications follow:

- **Bombs** assemble a supercritical mass suddenly, so the reaction runs away in a millionth of a second. Weapons need uranium enriched to around 90 percent uranium-235, or plutonium; natural uranium is only 0.7 percent.
- **Reactors** keep the reaction exactly critical. A moderator, such as water or graphite, slows neutrons so uranium absorbs them more readily, and control rods absorb neutrons to hold the rate steady. Power reactors use uranium enriched to a few percent. The heat boils water to drive turbines; see [[Heat Engine]].

Control is possible because of a quirk: a small fraction of neutrons, under one percent for uranium-235, are released seconds after the fission rather than instantly. Those **delayed neutrons** slow the reaction's response enough for mechanical controls to keep up. The first reactor, built under Enrico Fermi beneath a stadium at the University of Chicago, went critical on 2 December 1942.

Nature got there first. About two billion years ago, uranium deposits at Oklo in Gabon, richer in uranium-235 than today's, sustained natural chain reactions in groundwater for hundreds of thousands of years.

Fission products are intensely radioactive, and they keep producing heat after a reactor shuts down. That decay heat caused the 2011 meltdowns at Fukushima, where the reactors had stopped but the cooling failed. The products also make spent fuel dangerous for millennia; see [[Ionizing Radiation]]. In exchange, fission generates large amounts of electricity with almost no carbon emissions; see [[Greenhouse Effect]].
