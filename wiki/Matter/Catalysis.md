**Catalysis** is the speeding up of a chemical reaction by a substance, the **catalyst**, that takes part in the reaction but comes out unchanged. Because it's regenerated, a small amount of catalyst can convert enormous quantities of material.

Most reactions need a push to get started, called the **activation energy**. Molecules must collide with enough energy to break existing bonds before new ones form. Wood and oxygen release lots of energy when they react, but a log doesn't burst into flame at room temperature; it needs a match to supply the activation energy first; see [[Combustion]]. Heating speeds reactions by giving more molecules enough energy, which is why cooking chemistry, from the [[Maillard Reaction]] to [[Caramelization]], depends so strongly on temperature.

A catalyst works by providing a different route with a lower activation energy, often by holding reacting molecules in just the right orientation, or by temporarily bonding with them to weaken bonds that must break. Importantly, it doesn't change the energy released or which products are favored at equilibrium; it only changes how quickly the reaction gets there. It speeds the reverse reaction equally. Catalysts can't make an unfavorable reaction happen, which would violate [[Conservation of Energy]] and the second law; see [[Entropy]].

**Enzymes** are biological catalysts, mostly proteins folded into precise shapes with an *active site* that fits specific molecules. They're extraordinary, speeding reactions by factors of millions or more, at body temperature. Nearly every reaction in a living cell, from digestion to energy production in [[Mitochondria]], is enzyme-catalyzed. Their dependence on shape makes them fragile: heat or extreme acidity unfolds them and destroys their activity; see [[Protein Denaturation]] and [[pH]]. Fever and cooking both work partly through this. The liver's processing of drugs and toxins is also enzymatic; see [[Xenobiotic Metabolism]].

Enzymes matter in the kitchen:

- Yeast and bacteria use enzymes to turn sugars into alcohol, gas, and acids; see [[Fermentation]].
- Amylases in flour break starch into sugars that feed yeast and help crusts brown.
- Pineapple and papaya contain protein-digesting enzymes that tenderize meat and keep gelatin from setting, unless the fruit is cooked first.
- Cut apples brown because an enzyme oxidizes compounds exposed to air; lemon juice slows it by lowering pH; see [[Oxidation]].

Industrial chemistry depends on catalysts. The Haber–Bosch process, which makes ammonia for fertilizer over an iron catalyst, feeds roughly half the world's population. Catalytic converters in cars use platinum and palladium to turn carbon monoxide and unburned fuel into less harmful gases. Plastics production relies on catalysts to control [[Polymerization]].
