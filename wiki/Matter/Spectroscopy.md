**Spectroscopy** is the study of how matter absorbs and emits light at different wavelengths. Every element and molecule interacts with light in its own pattern, so splitting light into its colors and reading the pattern reveals what the light passed through or came from, whether a flame or a distant star.

Isaac Newton split sunlight with a prism in the 1660s; see [[Dispersion]]. In 1814 Joseph von Fraunhofer, a German optician, looked closely and found the solar spectrum crossed by hundreds of dark lines. Around 1860 Robert Bunsen and Gustav Kirchhoff explained them. Each element heated in a flame emitted light only at specific wavelengths, its **emission spectrum**, and the same element in cooler gas absorbed those same wavelengths, producing dark lines, its **absorption spectrum**. Sodium's bright yellow pair matched a dark pair in sunlight, so there was sodium in the Sun. With the method Bunsen and Kirchhoff discovered two new elements, cesium and rubidium.

In 1835 the philosopher Auguste Comte had given the chemical makeup of the stars as an example of something we could never know. Within three decades spectroscopy was measuring it. In 1868, astronomers studying the Sun found a yellow line matching no known element, and named it helium after the Greek word for the Sun. It wasn't found on Earth until 1895.

The lines come from the structure of atoms. Electrons can occupy only certain energy levels, and moving between two levels emits or absorbs light with exactly the energy difference, which fixes its wavelength. The pattern of levels is unique to each element; see [[Electromagnetic Spectrum]].

Spectral lines are everywhere once you look:

- **Lamps.** Sodium streetlights glow yellow and neon signs red, from their elements' strongest lines. Lights with narrow, spiky spectra can make colors look wrong; see [[Color Rendering Index]] and [[Metamerism]].
- **Fireworks.** Strontium gives red, barium green, and copper blue; see [[Combustion]].
- **Aurora.** The common green glow is a line emitted by oxygen high in the atmosphere; see [[Aurora]].

Lines also measure motion. Light from a receding source shifts toward red, and the shift of a known line gives the speed. That is how astronomers found that distant galaxies are moving away, and how they detect planets around other stars from the tiny wobble they cause; see [[Doppler Effect]].

Molecules have spectra too. Their bonds vibrate at characteristic frequencies, mostly in the infrared, so infrared spectroscopy identifies compounds in chemistry labs. The same absorption by carbon dioxide and water vapor is what traps heat in the atmosphere; see [[Greenhouse Effect]].

Hot, dense objects such as the Sun's surface or a stove element give a continuous spectrum, with no lines; see [[Black-Body Radiation]]. Instruments today usually spread light with a finely ruled grating rather than a prism; see [[Diffraction]].
