The **dew point** is the temperature to which air must be cooled for its water vapor to start condensing into liquid. Air at 25 °C with a dew point of 10 °C holds a modest amount of moisture; cool that air to 10 °C and dew forms on grass, fog fills valleys, and cold drinks bead with water. When the dew point is below freezing, the result is frost instead.

How much water vapor air can hold depends strongly on temperature, rising about 7 percent for every degree Celsius. **Relative humidity** expresses actual vapor as a percentage of that capacity at the current temperature. That makes it a slippery number. Air at 50 percent humidity on a cold winter day is dry; at 50 percent on a hot summer afternoon it is oppressive.

The dew point does not depend on the current temperature, so it measures the moisture itself. It is also a better guide to comfort:

- **Below 10 °C.** Dry and comfortable, sometimes too dry for skin and sinuses.
- **10 to 16 °C.** Comfortable for most people.
- **16 to 21 °C.** Noticeably humid, sticky during exercise.
- **Above 21 °C.** Oppressive; sweat barely evaporates.

The reason is that the body sheds heat mainly by evaporating sweat, drawing on the [[Latent Heat]] of vaporization. Evaporation slows as the air approaches saturation, so in humid air sweat drips off without cooling. [[Thermoregulation]] then struggles, heart rate climbs, and endurance suffers; cyclists and runners feel high dew points far more than high temperatures alone. [[Heat Acclimation]] helps, but only partly, and replacing what drips off is a matter of [[Fluid Balance]].

The *wet-bulb temperature*, read from a thermometer wrapped in wet cloth, combines heat and humidity into a measure of how much evaporative cooling is possible. A 2010 study proposed a wet-bulb temperature of 35 °C as the theoretical limit of human survival for prolonged exposure; later experiments on people suggest real limits are lower, around 31 °C.

Condensation explains many everyday puzzles:

- **Fogged glasses and mirrors.** A surface colder than the room's dew point collects water.
- **Sweating windows and mold.** Cold window glass and poorly insulated walls fall below the indoor dew point in winter, which is why bathrooms need ventilation.
- **Electronics.** A camera or laptop brought from cold outdoors into a warm room condenses water inside.
- **Air conditioning.** Cold coils in [[Vapor-Compression Refrigeration]] systems cool air below its dew point, wringing out water, so air conditioners also dehumidify.

Dew forms on clear, still nights because surfaces lose heat by radiation to the open sky, a form of [[Heat Transfer]], and cool below the air around them.
