A **Faraday cage** is an enclosure made of conducting material, solid or mesh, that blocks external electric fields and electromagnetic waves from reaching its interior. Michael Faraday demonstrated it in 1836 by lining a room with metal foil, driving high-voltage discharges against the outside, and showing with an electroscope that nothing inside became charged.

It works because charges in a conductor move freely. When an outside field reaches the cage, electrons in the metal shift until their own field cancels it inside. For a static field the cancellation is complete. For a radio wave, the wave drives currents in the metal that radiate a field opposite to it, and the wave is largely reflected; see [[Electromagnetic Spectrum]].

A cage doesn't need to be solid. A mesh works as long as its holes are much smaller than the wavelength being blocked. The door of a microwave oven is the familiar case: its metal mesh has holes a few millimeters across, while the oven's waves are about 12 centimeters long, so the microwaves stay in and visible light, with wavelengths far smaller than the holes, passes through so you can watch the food; see [[Microwave Heating]] and [[Diffraction]].

Everyday encounters:

- **Lost signal.** Elevators, metal-roofed buildings, and underground garages weaken phone and Wi-Fi signals by partly enclosing you in metal; see [[Cellular Network]] and [[Wi-Fi]].
- **Cars and planes in lightning.** A lightning strike on a car flows through the metal body around the occupants and into the ground. Commercial airliners are struck routinely and are built to carry the current around the cabin; see [[Lightning]]. A convertible or fiberglass body offers no such protection.
- **Shielded cables.** Coaxial cable surrounds its signal wire with a conducting sleeve, which keeps interference out and keeps the signal in; see [[Antenna]].
- **Sensitive rooms.** MRI scanners sit in shielded rooms because stray radio signals would corrupt their images.
- **Key fob pouches.** Pouches lined with metal fabric block thieves who relay the signal from a car's keyless entry fob to unlock it from a distance.

The protection has limits. It doesn't block static or slowly changing magnetic fields: a compass works inside a metal box. Shielding those needs thick layers of special high-permeability alloys; see [[Magnetism]]. Any gap or seam larger than the wavelength leaks, and a wire running through the wall can carry signals in or out like an antenna.

Sensitive electronics are protected from static the same way: bags made with a thin conductive layer keep outside charges from reaching the chips inside; see [[Electrostatic Discharge]]. At high frequencies, currents flow mostly in a thin surface layer of a conductor, so even very thin metal coatings shield well; see [[Electromagnetic Induction]].
