**Specific heat capacity** is the amount of energy needed to raise the temperature of one kilogram of a material by one degree. Materials differ enormously. Water takes about 4,184 joules per kilogram per degree, one of the highest values of any common substance. Aluminum takes about 900, iron about 450, and copper about 385. Heat a kilogram of water and a kilogram of iron with the same energy and the iron gets roughly nine times hotter.

**Heat capacity**, or *thermal mass*, is the total for an actual object: its mass times its specific heat. An object with large thermal mass warms slowly, cools slowly, and resists temperature swings. That property matters as much as how fast heat moves through it, its conductivity (see [[Heat Transfer]]), and the two are often confused.

Cooking shows the difference:

- **Cast iron versus aluminum.** Iron has a lower specific heat than aluminum, but cast-iron pans are thick and heavy, so they store a large amount of heat in total. Drop a cold steak in and the pan's temperature barely falls, so the sear continues and the [[Maillard Reaction]] keeps going. A thin aluminum pan heats quickly and evenly but loses temperature the moment food touches it.
- **Foil from the oven.** Aluminum foil can be touched seconds after leaving a hot oven, while the potato inside can burn. The foil has so little mass that it holds almost no heat.
- **Pizza steels and stones.** A thick slab stores enough heat to cook a crust in minutes. Steel conducts that heat into the dough faster than stone.
- **Resting meat.** The stored heat in a large roast keeps cooking it after it leaves the oven, the reason for [[Carryover Cooking]].

Water's high specific heat shapes much of the world. Oceans warm and cool slowly, so coastal climates have milder seasons than inland ones. A body made mostly of water resists rapid temperature change, which buys time for [[Thermoregulation]]. A pot of water or a sous vide bath holds a steady temperature, and water's high capacity is why it is the standard coolant for engines, power plants, and liquid-cooled computers. When water changes phase, it absorbs far more still, as [[Latent Heat]].

In electronics, thermal mass buys time rather than capacity. A heavy heatsink absorbs a short burst of work without the chip heating up, so a brief benchmark may never trigger [[Thermal Throttling]], while a sustained load eventually saturates it. That is why long runs are a better test of cooling than short ones. Temperature changes also change size, through [[Thermal Expansion]], and how energy ultimately spreads out as heat is governed by [[Entropy]].
