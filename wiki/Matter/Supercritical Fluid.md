A **supercritical fluid** is a substance pushed past its **critical point**, the temperature and pressure above which liquid and gas can no longer be told apart. Below that point, heating a sealed container of liquid produces a visible surface with vapor above it. As the critical point approaches, the liquid expands and thins while the vapor gets denser, until their densities match and the surface between them vanishes. What remains is a single fluid with properties between the two; see [[Phase Transition]] and [[Boiling Point]].

For water, the critical point is about 374 degrees Celsius at 221 times atmospheric pressure. For carbon dioxide it's far more accessible, about 31 degrees Celsius at 74 atmospheres, which is why supercritical carbon dioxide is the workhorse of industry; see [[Atmospheric Pressure]] and [[Ideal Gas Law]].

The useful combination is that a supercritical fluid can be dense enough to dissolve substances like a liquid, while having the low viscosity and fast diffusion of a gas, so it seeps into porous material quickly; see [[Viscosity]], [[Diffusion]] and [[Solubility]]. Better still, its dissolving power changes sharply with pressure. Raise the pressure and it picks up a compound; lower it and the compound drops out, while the carbon dioxide simply turns back into gas and leaves no residue.

The best-known use is **decaffeination**. Moistened green coffee beans are soaked in supercritical carbon dioxide, which selectively dissolves caffeine while leaving most flavor compounds behind; the caffeine is then removed and the gas recycled; see [[Caffeine]]. The same approach extracts hop oils for brewing, essential oils, and flavors, and has replaced toxic solvents in some dry cleaning.

It also solves a problem caused by surface tension. Drying a delicate wet gel in air destroys it, because the retreating liquid surface pulls fine structures together with enormous force. Replacing the liquid with a supercritical fluid and then venting it avoids any liquid surface at all. That's how aerogels, solids that are mostly air, are made; see [[Surface Tension]].

In energy, power plants run steam above the critical point of water to raise efficiency, since a hotter working fluid lets a heat engine convert more of its heat to work; see [[Heat Engine]]. Carbon dioxide heat pumps and refrigeration systems operate partly above its critical point, a so-called transcritical cycle, and avoid synthetic refrigerants harmful to the climate; see [[Vapor-Compression Refrigeration]] and [[Heat Pump]].

Nature has supercritical fluids too. The atmosphere at the surface of Venus, about 92 times Earth's pressure and over 460 degrees Celsius, is supercritical carbon dioxide; see [[Greenhouse Effect]]. Deep-sea hydrothermal vents can release water above its critical point.
