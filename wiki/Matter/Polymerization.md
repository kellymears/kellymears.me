**Polymerization** is the chemical process of joining many small molecules, called *monomers*, into long chains or networks, called *polymers*. A polymer can contain thousands to millions of repeating units. Plastics are the familiar examples, but polymers are everywhere in nature too: starch and cellulose are chains of glucose, proteins are chains of amino acids, and DNA is a chain of nucleotides.

There are two main routes. In *addition* polymerization, monomers with double bonds open up and snap together end to end, as ethylene becomes polyethylene, the plastic in shopping bags. In *condensation* polymerization, each link releases a small molecule, usually water, as when nylon or polyester forms.

What a polymer does depends heavily on how its chains connect:

- **Thermoplastics** have separate chains that slide past each other when heated, so they soften and can be remelted and remolded. PET bottles and 3D-printer PLA are examples.
- **Thermosets** are **cross-linked**: chains are bonded to each other at many points into one continuous network. They don't melt, they char. Epoxy, the resin in carbon fiber, and cured rubber are thermosets.

Cross-linking turns soft materials into durable ones. Natural rubber is sticky in heat and brittle in cold. In 1839, Charles Goodyear found that heating it with sulfur creates bridges between chains, and the result, *vulcanized* rubber, stays springy across temperatures, giving tires their useful [[Elasticity]].

Kitchens and studios rely on a slower version. Oils rich in unsaturated fats, such as linseed and flaxseed oil, are *drying oils*: exposed to air, they undergo [[Oxidation]] at their double bonds, which then cross-link into a hard, dry film. Oil paint "dries" this way, by polymerizing rather than evaporating, which is why it takes days. The reaction gives off heat, so oily rags left in a pile can warm up until they catch fire, a well-known cause of workshop [[Combustion]].

**Seasoning cast iron** is the same chemistry applied deliberately. A thin layer of oil is heated past its [[Smoke Point]], where it breaks down, oxidizes, and polymerizes into a hard, slick coating bonded to the metal. Repeated thin layers build a durable nonstick surface that also protects the iron from rust. Thick layers stay sticky, because oxygen can't reach the oil underneath to finish cross-linking.

Polymers have a property small molecules lack: a *glass transition*. Below a certain temperature, the chains are frozen in place and the material is rigid and brittle; above it, they can wriggle and the material turns leathery or rubbery. Unlike melting, it is a gradual change, not a sharp [[Phase Transition]]. It is why chewing gum softens in the mouth.

Food chemistry is polymer chemistry too: [[Gluten]] forms a stretchy protein network, [[Starch Gelatinization]] swells starch polymers with water, and [[Protein Denaturation]] unfolds chains that then bond into new networks.
