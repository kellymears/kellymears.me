A **laser** is a device that produces light in which all the waves have the same wavelength, travel in the same direction, and stay in step with each other. The word began as an acronym for "light amplification by stimulated emission of radiation." Ordinary sources emit many wavelengths with random phases in all directions. Laser light can be focused to a tiny spot, stay narrow over kilometers, and make sharp interference patterns; see [[Wave Interference]].

The physics comes from Albert Einstein, who showed in 1917 that a passing photon can trigger an already excited atom to release an identical photon, with the same wavelength, direction, and phase. This is **stimulated emission**. Normally few atoms are excited, so light is absorbed more than amplified. A laser "pumps" its medium, with a flash lamp, an electric current, or another laser, until more atoms are excited than not. Then each photon is likelier to trigger another than to be absorbed, and the light builds up.

Mirrors at each end bounce the light back and forth through the medium many times, and only wavelengths that fit the cavity a whole number of times are reinforced; see [[Resonance]]. One mirror lets a small fraction through, and that becomes the beam. Many lasers emit [[Polarized Light]] as well.

Theodore Maiman built the first working laser, a ruby rod pumped by a flash lamp, in 1960. It was called "a solution looking for a problem." The problems turned up:

- **Communication.** Almost all long-distance data travels as laser pulses through [[Optical Fiber]]. Some satellite constellations link satellites to each other with lasers across space; see [[Satellite Internet]].
- **Reading data.** Barcode scanners and disc players. A disc's tracks are spaced so tightly that they split white light into rainbows; see [[Diffraction]].
- **Measuring.** Lidar times laser pulses to map terrain and give self-driving cars a 3D view of the road. Lasers bounced off reflectors left on the Moon measure its distance to within millimeters.
- **Cutting and medicine.** Industrial lasers cut sheet steel. Eye surgery reshapes the cornea to correct [[Myopia]].
- **Projection.** Lasers emit nearly pure single colors, so laser projectors can reach a wider range of colors than lamps; see [[Color Gamut]].

Most lasers today are tiny chips in which current flowing across a junction does the pumping; see [[Semiconductor]]. An LED works on similar principles without the mirrors, so its light is incoherent; see [[Fluorescence]] for how white LEDs get their color.

A beam still spreads a little. One aimed at the Moon is several kilometers wide when it arrives, but that is far less spreading than an ordinary lamp's; see [[Inverse-Square Law]].

That concentration is also the hazard: the eye focuses even a weak beam onto one point of the retina, which can burn it.
