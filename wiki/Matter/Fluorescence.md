**Fluorescence** is the emission of light by a material that has just absorbed light. A molecule absorbs a photon, jumps to an excited energy state, loses a little energy as vibration, and then drops back, emitting a photon of lower energy and therefore longer wavelength. The whole process takes billionths of a second, so fluorescence stops the instant the source is removed. George Stokes named and explained it in 1852, and the shift to longer wavelengths is called the *Stokes shift*. The energy difference becomes heat, as [[Conservation of Energy]] requires.

**Phosphorescence** is the slow version. The excited state is one the material can leave only through a quantum-mechanically "forbidden" route, so it releases light over seconds to hours. Glow-in-the-dark stars and watch dials work this way.

Fluorescence is everywhere in lighting:

- **White LEDs.** Most white LEDs are blue LEDs coated with a yellow phosphor. Some blue light passes through and the rest is converted to yellow, and the mix looks white. The phosphor blend sets the [[Color Temperature]] and how well colors render, measured by the [[Color Rendering Index]]. Cheap phosphors leave gaps in the red. The strong blue peak that remains is a concern in [[Circadian Lighting]] for evening use.
- **Fluorescent tubes.** An electric current excites mercury vapor, which emits mostly ultraviolet light. A phosphor coating inside the glass converts it to visible light. Their spiky spectra make colors that match under daylight look different, a [[Metamerism]] failure familiar from clothes bought under store lights.
- **Displays.** Quantum dots, tiny semiconductor crystals whose emission color depends on their size, convert blue backlight into narrow, saturated red and green, widening a screen's [[Color Gamut]].

Ultraviolet *blacklights* make fluorescence visible in everyday materials. Highlighter ink glows; tonic water glows blue from its quinine. Laundry detergents add **optical brighteners** that absorb ultraviolet and emit blue, offsetting the yellowing of fabric so whites look whiter than white. Paper and some plastics are treated the same way, which is why they flare under a blacklight.

Science relies heavily on fluorescence. Green fluorescent protein, found in a jellyfish, can be attached to other proteins so researchers can watch them inside living cells; its discoverers shared the 2008 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Fluorescence microscopy, DNA sequencing, and many medical tests tag molecules with dyes that glow at specific wavelengths. Banknotes and passports use fluorescent security marks.

Fluorescence differs from the other ways objects color light. [[Dispersion]] separates existing wavelengths by refraction, and [[Rayleigh Scattering]] redirects short wavelengths more strongly, but neither creates new ones. A fluorescent material actually converts light from one color to another, which is why a fluorescent orange safety vest can look brighter than the light shining on it, emitting extra orange made from blue and ultraviolet.
