**Cavitation** is the formation of vapor bubbles in a liquid when its pressure drops low enough, followed by their violent collapse. It's boiling caused by low pressure instead of heat: water boils at 100 °C at sea level, but if the pressure falls far enough, it boils at room temperature; see [[Boiling Point]].

The pressure drop usually comes from speed. Fast-moving liquid has lower pressure, so the back of a spinning propeller blade, the inlet of a pump, or the narrow throat of a valve can fall below the liquid's vapor pressure; see [[Bernoulli's Principle]]. Bubbles start at tiny impurities or pockets of trapped gas, since pure water resists tearing apart; see [[Nucleation]].

The trouble starts when the bubbles drift back into higher pressure. They collapse in microseconds, with nothing inside to cushion them, and the inrushing liquid sends out shock waves and fires thin jets at nearby surfaces; see [[Surface Tension]]. Each collapse is tiny, but millions of them, repeated, pit metal like a sandblaster. Pitted ship propellers, eroded pump impellers, and damaged dam spillways are classic cavitation damage; see [[Material Fatigue]]. Engineers design pumps with a margin of pressure at the inlet to prevent it, and modeling the mixed liquid and vapor flow is one of the harder problems in simulation; see [[Fluid Simulation]] and [[Reynolds Number]].

Cavitation is loud. The crackle of collapsing bubbles is one of the main noises a submarine makes, and since pressure rises with depth, a submarine can go quieter by diving deeper; see [[Sound]].

Some of its uses:

- **Ultrasonic cleaners** shake a liquid fast enough to form and collapse bubbles against an object, scrubbing dirt from crevices in jewelry and surgical tools.
- **Medicine** uses focused shock waves and cavitation to break up kidney stones without surgery.
- **Supercavitating torpedoes** wrap themselves in a bubble of vapor, cutting drag enough to travel underwater at hundreds of kilometers per hour.

The pistol shrimp has a claw that snaps shut so fast it shoots out a jet of water, forming a cavitation bubble. Its collapse stuns or kills small prey and is one of the loudest sounds in the ocean. In 2001, researchers found it also emits a brief flash of light, an example of **sonoluminescence**: a collapsing bubble compresses its contents so quickly that they briefly reach thousands of degrees.

Cavitation is probably behind the crack of knuckles. A 2015 study filmed a finger joint in an MRI scanner and saw a gas cavity form in the joint fluid at the moment of the sound.
