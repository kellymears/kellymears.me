The **Coriolis effect** is the apparent deflection of a moving object when it's observed from a rotating frame of reference. Seen from the ground, a long-range shell, a mass of air, or an ocean current drifts sideways from the straight path it seems it should follow: to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern. Gaspard-Gustave de Coriolis described it in 1835.

Nothing actually pushes the object. Viewed from space, it travels in a straight line, as [[Newton's Laws of Motion]] require, while Earth turns underneath it. The "force" appears only because the ground is not an inertial frame. To keep Newton's laws working in the rotating frame, physicists add a fictitious Coriolis force, the same way they add centrifugal force; see [[Inertial Frame of Reference]] and [[Centripetal Force]].

Earth's surface at the equator moves east at about 1,670 kilometers per hour; near the poles it barely moves. Air flowing north from the equator keeps its faster eastward speed and so runs ahead of the ground beneath it, curving east. This follows from [[Angular Momentum]] being conserved as the air moves closer to Earth's axis.

The effect is weak, proportional to the object's speed and the rate of rotation, so it matters only over large distances or long times:

- **Weather.** Air flowing toward a low-pressure center is deflected, so instead of filling the low directly it spirals around it. That's why hurricanes turn counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and clockwise in the Southern, and why they never form right at the equator, where the effect vanishes; see [[Atmospheric Pressure]].
- **Ocean currents.** The great circulating ocean gyres and the trade winds owe their direction to it.
- **Long-range shooting.** Naval gunners in the First World War had to correct for it, and snipers firing over a mile still do.
- **Foucault's pendulum.** In 1851, Léon Foucault hung a heavy pendulum in the Panthéon in Paris. The plane of its swing slowly rotated over the day, the first direct laboratory demonstration that Earth turns; see [[Simple Harmonic Motion]].

The best-known claim about the effect is false. Sinks and toilets do not drain in opposite directions in the two hemispheres. Across a basin, the Coriolis effect is millions of times weaker than the swirl left from filling it or the shape of the bowl. The myth survives by repetition; see [[Illusory Truth Effect]].

Rotating frames fool the body too. A pilot who tilts their head during a sustained turn can feel a sudden, violent tumble, the "Coriolis illusion," because fluid in the inner ear's canals is set moving on two axes at once; see [[Vestibular System]].

Weather and ballistics simulations include the term. Most game engines leave it out; see [[Physics engine]].
