A **lens** is a shaped piece of glass, plastic, or other transparent material that bends light to form an image. It works by [[Refraction]]: light slows as it enters the material and bends at the surface, and a curved surface bends rays by different amounts depending on where they strike. A converging lens, thicker at the middle, brings parallel rays together at a point; a diverging lens, thinner at the middle, spreads them apart.

The key number is **focal length**, the distance from the lens to where it focuses parallel light. Shorter focal lengths bend light more strongly. Optometrists measure strength in **diopters**, one divided by the focal length in meters, so prescriptions read like minus two.

The eye is a two-lens system. The cornea does most of the bending, and the flexible inner lens changes shape to focus at different distances. Nearsighted eyes focus distant light too soon and are corrected with diverging lenses; see [[Myopia]]. With age the inner lens stiffens and can no longer focus close, which is why reading glasses become common past forty; see [[Presbyopia]]. Eyeglasses appeared in northern Italy around 1286. Telescopes and microscopes followed in the early 1600s.

In cameras, the **aperture** is the opening that admits light. Its size is given as an **f-number**, the focal length divided by the opening's diameter, so f/2 is wide and f/16 is narrow. Each full step on the scale halves the light. A wide aperture gathers more light but focuses only a thin slice of the scene sharply; see [[Depth of field]].

No real lens is perfect. Its flaws are called aberrations:

- **Chromatic.** Glass bends colors by different amounts, so a simple lens can't focus all of them at once, leaving colored fringes; see [[Dispersion]]. Cementing two lenses of different glasses together largely cancels this, a fix dating to the 1730s.
- **Spherical.** A spherical surface, easy to grind, focuses edge rays slightly short of center rays.
- **Distortion.** Straight lines bow outward or inward toward the frame's edges.

Modern camera lenses stack a dozen or more elements to cancel these, and even a perfect lens can't resolve detail finer than its aperture allows, because light spreads as it passes through any opening; see [[Diffraction]].

Some designs break the rules. In 1822 Augustin-Jean Fresnel collapsed a thick lighthouse lens into concentric rings of thin prisms, keeping the bending surfaces and discarding the bulk; see [[Glass]]. **Fresnel lenses** now appear in overhead projectors and older virtual reality headsets.

Graphics usually pretends lenses don't exist, imagining a perfect pinhole camera with everything in focus; see [[Perspective Projection]]. Realism comes from adding the flaws back, with depth of field, flare, and fringes simulated in post-processing or by tracing rays through modeled lens elements; see [[Ray Tracing]].
