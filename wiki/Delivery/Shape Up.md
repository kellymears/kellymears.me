**Shape Up** is a method for product development described by Ryan Singer in a free book published by Basecamp in 2019, based on how that company had worked for years. It rejects most of the machinery of Scrum, including estimates, a long backlog, and short repeating sprints, in favor of longer fixed cycles and work defined before it starts; see [[Agile Software Development]].

The core ideas:

- **Six-week cycles.** Teams work on a project for six weeks, followed by two weeks of cooldown for fixes, exploration, and deciding what comes next. Six weeks is long enough to build something real and short enough to see the end from the start; see [[Timeboxing]].
- **Appetite, not estimates.** Instead of asking how long a feature will take, the team decides how much time it is worth, such as two weeks or six, and shapes the work to fit. Time is fixed and scope varies, the reverse of estimating a fixed scope; see [[Planning Fallacy]] and [[Hofstadter's Law]].
- **Shaping.** Before a project is offered, senior people work out the solution: concrete enough that the team knows what to build, rough enough to leave room for design. **Breadboarding** lists places, the things a user can act on, and the connections between them, without visual design. **Fat marker sketches** are drawn too thick to allow detail; see [[Wireframe]]. The result is a written pitch, naming risks and what is out of scope.
- **The betting table.** Before each cycle, a small group chooses which pitches to bet on. There is no central backlog; pitches that aren't chosen are not kept in a queue, though anyone may bring one back.
- **The circuit breaker.** If a project isn't done by the end of its cycle, it doesn't get extended by default. The bet was six weeks, and running over means the shaping was wrong, so the work goes back to be reshaped; see [[Sunk Cost Fallacy]].
- **Hill charts.** Progress is tracked as dots on a hill: the uphill side is figuring out what to do, the downhill side is doing it. A dot stuck uphill exposes unknowns early.

Teams work on scopes, slices that can be finished and demonstrated on their own, rather than layers; see [[Walking Skeleton]]. Scope cutting is expected, and the question is always what can be left out within the appetite; see [[Scope Creep]] and [[Minimum Viable Product]].

Critics note that it depends on senior people with time to shape well, and that a two-week cooldown is a luxury many companies won't fund. The fixed appetite also uses [[Parkinson's Law]]: work expands to fill the time available, so that time is set deliberately. Other teams often adopt parts, such as appetites or hill charts; see [[Story Points]] and [[Issue Tracker]].
