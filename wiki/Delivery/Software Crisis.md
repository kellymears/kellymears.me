The **software crisis** was a name given in the late 1960s to a widely shared sense that the building of software was out of control. Computers were growing rapidly more powerful, and the programs written for them were growing larger and more ambitious. Projects were routinely late, over budget, and full of errors, and some were never finished at all.

The term is closely associated with a conference sponsored by NATO's Science Committee, held in Garmisch, Germany, in October 1968. Its organizers chose the title "Software Engineering" deliberately, as a provocation: the phrase suggested that writing programs should be grounded in the theory and practical discipline of established engineering fields, which it clearly wasn't yet. The conference brought together academics and industry practitioners to discuss the problems openly, and its report records frank admissions of failure. A follow-up conference in Rome the next year was less harmonious. The name stuck, and "software engineering" became the label for a whole field.

The computer scientist Edsger Dijkstra gave a memorable account of the crisis in a 1972 lecture. As long as there were no machines, programming was no problem at all; when there were a few weak computers, it became a mild problem; and now that there were gigantic computers, programming had become an equally gigantic problem. Hardware power was racing ahead, and the ability to write correct programs for it was not keeping up; see [[Moore's Law]].

The best-known case study came from IBM's OS/360 operating system, a vast project of the mid-1960s that ran far behind schedule. Its manager, Fred Brooks, drew lessons from it in a 1975 book of essays, including the observation that adding people to a late project makes it later; see [[Brooks's Law]] and [[Second-System Effect]].

Responses to the crisis shaped much of what followed:

- **Structured programming** sought to make programs easier to reason about by restricting how control could flow; see [[Structured Programming]].
- **Formal methods** aimed to prove programs correct mathematically; see [[Formal Verification]].
- **Process models** tried to impose order through defined phases and documents; see [[Waterfall]] and [[Spiral Model]].

Whether the crisis ever ended is debated. Software remains hard to estimate and frequently late, but it has also grown enormously in scale and reliability; see [[Planning Fallacy]]. Some argue the crisis simply became the normal condition of the field, and that programs keep growing to absorb whatever capability becomes available; see [[Lehman's Laws of Software Evolution]].

In a 1986 essay, Brooks argued there was no single technique that would improve software productivity tenfold, because much of the difficulty lies in the inherent complexity of the problems themselves, not the tools; see [[Technical Debt]].
