An **engineering ladder** is a written framework of levels that describes what an organization expects of its engineers at each stage of a career. It sets out the scope of work, technical skill and influence expected for each level, and is used for hiring, promotion decisions and pay bands. Typical titles run from junior or associate engineer through engineer and senior engineer, then on to staff, principal and distinguished engineer, though names and numbering vary widely between companies.

For a long time the main way to keep advancing as an engineer was to stop engineering and become a manager. That pushed some of the strongest technical people into roles they did not want and were not suited for; see [[Peter Principle]]. The **dual career ladder**, used by industrial research laboratories since at least the mid-twentieth century, addresses this with two parallel tracks. The management track grows in responsibility for people and teams. The **individual contributor** track grows in technical scope, reaching levels with pay and influence equivalent to senior management, without direct reports.

The senior levels of the individual contributor track are the hardest to define. A senior engineer is usually expected to deliver complex work independently. A **staff engineer** works beyond a single team: setting technical direction across several, untangling problems nobody owns, writing the design documents that others build from, and raising the skills of those around them; see [[Architecture Decision Record]]. Writers on the subject describe several shapes the role takes, such as the technical lead for a large team, the architect responsible for a domain, the specialist sent to solve hard problems, and the partner who extends a senior leader's reach.

Ladders create recurring problems:

- **Invisible work.** Coordinating teams, mentoring, reviewing code and improving processes keep projects healthy but produce little that shows up in a promotion packet. This is sometimes called glue work, and it often falls to people who are then judged as less technical; see [[Code Review]] and [[Developer Experience]].
- **Metrics gaming.** When a level requires evidence of "impact," engineers may favor launching new projects over maintaining old ones; see [[Goodhart's Law]] and [[Software Rot]].
- **Level inflation.** Titles drift upward over time and differ between companies, so the same title can mean very different things.
- **Self-assessment.** People at every stage misjudge their own level, in both directions; see [[Dunning-Kruger Effect]] and [[Impostor Syndrome]].

A good ladder also spreads knowledge deliberately. Senior expectations commonly include reducing how much a team depends on any single person, including the senior engineer, through pairing, documentation and shared ownership; see [[Bus Factor]] and [[Pair Programming]].
