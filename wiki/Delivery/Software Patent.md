A **software patent** grants exclusive rights, typically for twenty years from filing, to an invention implemented in software. Unlike copyright, which protects only a particular text of code, a patent protects the method itself. Anyone who independently arrives at the same method can still infringe, which is why patents worry programmers in a way copyright does not; see [[Clean Room Design]].

Whether software should be patentable at all has been contested from the start. Algorithms resemble mathematics, which is not patentable, yet software can control machines that clearly are. In the United States, courts drew the line repeatedly. A 1981 Supreme Court case upheld a patent on a rubber-curing process that used a computer program. In the 1990s, appeals court rulings opened the door wide, including to business methods, and software patent filings surged. In 2014, the Supreme Court's decision in Alice Corp. v. CLS Bank held that taking an abstract idea and saying "do it on a computer" is not enough, and many such patents have since been invalidated. In Europe, the governing convention excludes computer programs "as such," but software that produces a further technical effect can be patented.

Patents have shaped the formats people use daily. Unisys held a patent on LZW compression, used by the GIF image format, and began seeking license fees in the mid-1990s. The backlash helped spur the creation of PNG as an unencumbered replacement; see [[GIF]] and [[Data Compression]]. Patent pools around audio and video formats long made some codecs awkward to ship in free software, and royalty-free codecs were later developed partly to escape them; see [[Video Compression]].

A further problem is litigation by **non-practicing entities**, often called patent trolls, which make no products and acquire broad patents in order to sue or threaten companies that do. Because defending a suit can cost more than settling, even weak patents can extract payments.

Open source licensing responded. The Apache License 2.0 includes an explicit patent grant from every contributor, and terminates it for anyone who sues claiming the software infringes their patents; see [[Permissive License]]. Version 3 of the GPL added similar provisions; see [[Copyleft]] and [[License Compatibility]]. The **Open Invention Network**, founded in 2005, holds patents and asks members to cross-license patents covering Linux and related software without royalties, building a defensive commons; see [[Tragedy of the Commons]].

Engineers working in companies are often advised not to read patents casually, because knowingly infringing can carry higher damages than infringing unknowingly. That advice undercuts the patent system's stated bargain, which is that inventors disclose their inventions to the public in exchange for a temporary monopoly. Supporters counter that patents protect real investment in research and give small companies leverage against large ones; see [[Open Core]] and [[Free Software]].
