An **open source foundation** is a nonprofit organization that hosts open-source projects. It gives them a legal identity that a loose group of volunteers lacks: something that can own a trademark, sign contracts, accept donations, pay for servers, and defend the project in court. Above all, it offers neutrality. When a project belongs to a foundation rather than a company, competitors can contribute without worrying that one rival will take control; see [[Open Source Sustainability]].

The **Apache Software Foundation**, founded in 1999, grew out of the Apache Group, the developers maintaining the Apache web server. It became a model for volunteer-led, community-governed foundations. Its projects are run by committees of contributors who earned their role through sustained work, and decisions are made in public on mailing lists. New projects pass through an incubator before graduating. The foundation's motto, "community over code," reflects its view that a healthy community outlasts any particular codebase; see [[Bus Factor]]. Subversion became an Apache project in 2010; see [[Subversion]].

The **Linux Foundation**, formed in 2007 from a merger of two earlier consortia, follows a different model. It's a trade association funded largely by corporate members, and it employs some key developers, including the Linux kernel's creator. It now hosts hundreds of projects and sub-foundations, among them the Cloud Native Computing Foundation, home of Kubernetes. Critics note that companies paying the bills shape priorities; supporters point out that the money keeps critical infrastructure maintained.

Smaller organizations act as **fiscal sponsors**, handling money and legal paperwork for member projects without running their governance. Git, for example, is a member project of one such group.

Foundations show their value when a company changes a project's license. Several widely used projects relicensed under terms that don't meet the Open Source Definition, usually to stop cloud providers from selling them as a service; see [[Open Core]] and [[Open Source Definition]]. In response, contributors forked the last open version and moved the fork to a foundation, so the same thing couldn't happen again; see [[Software Fork]]. A foundation-held project can't be relicensed by any single participant.

Foundations don't solve everything. They can't make volunteers maintain unglamorous code, and some critical projects have long had one or two unpaid maintainers despite foundation homes; see [[XZ Utils Backdoor]] and [[Heartbleed]]. Their processes can feel slow compared with a single company's. And they hold trademarks rather than copyrights in many cases, so license changes still depend on contributors; see [[License Compatibility]] and [[Developer Certificate of Origin]].

Projects without a foundation often rely on a single founder as final decision-maker, a model with its own risks; see [[Benevolent Dictator for Life]]. Foundations also typically require a code of conduct for hosted projects; see [[Code of Conduct]].
