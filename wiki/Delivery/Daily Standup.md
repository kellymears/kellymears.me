A **daily standup** is a short meeting, usually fifteen minutes or less, held at the same time each day, in which a team coordinates its work. Participants traditionally stand, on the theory that people who are standing don't let meetings drag. Extreme Programming included it as a practice, and Scrum made it one of its fixed events as the **Daily Scrum**; see [[Extreme Programming]] and [[Agile Software Development]].

The classic format has each person answer three questions:

1. What did I do yesterday?
2. What will I do today?
3. Is anything blocking me?

The third question is the important one. The meeting exists to surface problems early, while someone else on the team can still help, and to keep people working toward the same goal rather than in parallel isolation. Later editions of the Scrum Guide dropped the three questions as a requirement, describing the meeting instead as a check on progress toward the sprint's goal and leaving the format to the team.

A common alternative is **walking the board**: the team goes through its task board from the right, starting with work closest to done, and asks what is needed to move each item along. The focus shifts from people reporting on themselves to the work and what is stuck; see [[Kanban]].

Standups go wrong in predictable ways:

- **Status report.** Team members speak to a manager rather than to each other, and the meeting becomes a daily performance review; see [[Psychological Safety]].
- **Problem-solving in the meeting.** A discussion between two people holds up everyone else. The usual rule is to note it and continue afterward with only the people involved.
- **Creep.** A fifteen-minute meeting stretches to forty-five as people fill the time; see [[Parkinson's Law]].
- **Ritual.** People recite what they did without listening to anyone else, which makes the meeting a cost with no benefit.

The meeting has a cost beyond its length. Set in the middle of the morning, it breaks the day into pieces too short for deep work, and programmers often schedule around it rather than start something they'll have to put down; see [[Maker's schedule]] and [[Flow]]. Many teams put it at the start of the day for that reason.

Distributed teams across time zones often replace it with written updates posted to a shared channel, which anyone can read at their own start of day. Written standups lose the quick back-and-forth but keep the record, and they work better when the third question still gets a prompt answer from someone.

Whether a standup is worth holding depends on whether people learn something they would otherwise have found out too late. A team that already talks all day may not need one; see [[Team Topologies]].
