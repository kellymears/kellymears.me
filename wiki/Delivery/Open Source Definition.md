The **Open Source Definition** is a list of ten criteria a software license must meet to be called open source. It's maintained by the **Open Source Initiative**, a nonprofit founded in 1998, which reviews licenses against it and publishes a list of approved ones.

The term itself dates from early 1998. Netscape had just announced it would release the source code of its web browser, and a group of free software advocates wanted a label that businesses would find less confusing than "free software," which was often misheard as meaning no-cost. Christine Peterson suggested "open source," and Eric Raymond and Bruce Perens founded the OSI to promote it; see [[Free Software]] and [[The Cathedral and the Bazaar]].

The definition was adapted from the Debian Free Software Guidelines, which Perens had drafted in 1997 to decide what software the Debian project would include; see [[Linux Distribution]]. Its criteria cover:

- **Free redistribution**: the license can't forbid selling or giving the software away, or charge a royalty for it.
- **Source code**: the source must be available in a form programmers would actually use, not deliberately obscured.
- **Derived works**: modifications must be allowed and distributable.
- **Integrity of the author's source**: a license may require changes to ship as patches or under a different name, protecting the original's reputation.
- **No discrimination against persons or groups.**
- **No discrimination against fields of endeavor**: a license can't ban commercial use, or use in a particular industry.
- **Distribution of license**: the rights apply to everyone who receives the software, without extra agreements.
- **Not specific to a product, not restricting other software, and technology-neutral.**

Both permissive and copyleft licenses fit within it; see [[Permissive License]] and [[Copyleft]]. The OSI also discourages creating new licenses that duplicate existing ones, since every new license adds questions about which can be combined; see [[License Compatibility]].

The definition's sharpest edge is the rule against restricting fields of use. Licenses that forbid running the software as a commercial cloud service, or that bar uses their authors consider unethical, fail it. Companies that relicensed popular projects under such terms have argued their software is still effectively open, and the OSI and much of the community have answered that it's now **source-available**, not open source; see [[Open Core]] and [[Protestware]]. Those disputes often end in a fork that keeps the old license; see [[Software Fork]].

The OSI's authority is social rather than legal. "Open source" isn't a registered trademark for software, so nothing stops anyone from using the phrase loosely. Its weight comes from the community, package registries, and corporate legal teams that treat OSI approval as the line between licenses they understand and ones they must review; see [[Software Bill of Materials]].
