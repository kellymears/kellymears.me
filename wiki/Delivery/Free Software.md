**Free software** is software that respects its users' freedom to run, study, change and share it. The "free" refers to liberty, not price. The movement's standard phrase is "free as in free speech, not as in free beer." Free software can be sold, and plenty of no-cost software isn't free in this sense, because its source is hidden or its license forbids changes.

The idea was formalized by Richard Stallman, a programmer at the MIT Artificial Intelligence Lab. Stallman had worked in a culture where programmers freely shared and modified code, and watched it erode in the late 1970s and early 1980s as software became a commercial product sold without source.

In September 1983 Stallman announced the **GNU Project**, to build a complete Unix-compatible operating system made entirely of free software; see [[Unix]]. The name is a recursive acronym, "GNU's Not Unix." The **Free Software Foundation** followed in 1985 to support the work. By the early 1990s GNU had a compiler, editor, shell and most of the tools of a Unix system, but no working kernel. Linux, released in 1991, filled that gap, and the combination became the basis of most Linux distributions; see [[Linux Distribution]]. The FSF asks that the result be called GNU/Linux, a request that's widely ignored.

The **four freedoms** define the term, numbered from zero in programmer fashion:

- **Freedom 0**: to run the program for any purpose.
- **Freedom 1**: to study how it works and change it, which requires access to the source code.
- **Freedom 2**: to redistribute copies.
- **Freedom 3**: to distribute modified versions, so others benefit from changes.

To keep software free as it passed between hands, Stallman devised the GNU General Public License in 1989. It uses copyright to require that anyone distributing a modified version share it under the same terms; see [[Copyleft]].

In 1998 a group including Eric Raymond and Bruce Perens promoted the term "open source" as a more business-friendly label for much the same software; see [[Open Source Definition]] and [[The Cathedral and the Bazaar]]. The FSF rejected the change of framing. Open source argues from practical benefits, such as better code from more reviewers. Free software argues that users are entitled to control their computing, and treats proprietary software as a social problem rather than a weaker engineering choice. The licenses mostly overlap; the motives differ. The umbrella term "free and open-source software" covers both.

Most permissively licensed software is also free software by the FSF's definition, though the FSF prefers copyleft; see [[Permissive License]]. Mixing licenses raises its own puzzles; see [[License Compatibility]]. The movement's concern with who controls software echoes in debates over vendor control and relicensing; see [[Open Core]] and [[Software Fork]].
