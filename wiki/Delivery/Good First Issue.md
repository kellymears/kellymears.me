A **good first issue** is a task in a project's issue tracker that maintainers have marked as suitable for someone contributing for the first time. The label, used widely on open source projects and recognized by major code hosting platforms, signals that the task is small, well understood, and doesn't require deep knowledge of the codebase; see [[Issue Tracker]] and [[Software Forge]].

Contributing to an unfamiliar project is daunting. A newcomer has to set up a development environment, understand the code's structure and conventions, figure out how to submit a change, and navigate the expectations of strangers. Research on newcomers to open source has identified many such barriers, and a large share of would-be contributors give up before their first change is accepted; see [[Onboarding]] and [[Developer Experience]].

A good first issue reduces the problem to one manageable step. The best ones share some traits:

- **Small scope.** A typo, a small bug, a missing test, or a minor improvement to documentation.
- **Clear description.** What's wrong, where to look, and what a fix would roughly involve.
- **Low risk.** A mistake won't break anything important.
- **A named helper.** Ideally a maintainer has offered to guide the contributor.

The aim isn't really the fix. A maintainer could often make the change faster themselves. The aim is to walk a newcomer through the full contribution process once, from cloning the code to having a change reviewed and merged, so the second contribution is easier; see [[Pull Request]] and [[Code Review]]. Educational psychologists describe this kind of supported first step as scaffolding: support that lets a learner succeed at something just beyond their current ability; see [[Scaffolding]].

Projects have reasons to invest in this. Most open source work is done by a few people, and many projects depend on one or two maintainers; see [[Pareto Principle]] and [[Bus Factor]]. Bringing in new contributors is one of the few ways to spread that load; see [[Open Source Sustainability]].

The label has limits. Popular issues can be claimed within minutes, sometimes by people who never finish. Easy tasks can attract low-effort contributions aimed at credit rather than engagement. The **Hacktoberfest** event, which since 2014 has rewarded people for opening pull requests in October, drew a flood of trivial, unwanted changes in 2020, and organizers made participation opt-in for projects in response.

Welcoming newcomers involves more than labels: clear contribution guides, respectful review, and enforced standards of conduct all matter; see [[Code of Conduct]]. Companies applying open source practices internally use similar techniques to let engineers contribute across team boundaries; see [[Inner Source]].
