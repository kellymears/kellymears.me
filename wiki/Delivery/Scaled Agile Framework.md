The **Scaled Agile Framework**, usually written **SAFe**, is a set of roles, events and artifacts for applying agile methods across a large organization. Dean Leffingwell published the first version in 2011, and it has since gone through several major revisions. It is sold with training and certification, and surveys of large enterprises have often found it the most used of the scaling frameworks.

The problem it addresses is real. Agile methods were designed for a single small team working closely with its customers; see [[Agile Software Development]] and [[Two-Pizza Team]]. A product built by fifty teams has dependencies between them, shared release dates, budgets set years ahead, and executives who want predictable plans. Something has to coordinate the teams.

SAFe's main answer is the **Agile Release Train**, a long-lived group of roughly five to twelve teams, often 50 to 125 people, aligned to a stream of value for customers; see [[Value Stream Mapping]]. The train runs on a fixed cadence called a **program increment**, typically eight to twelve weeks long. Each increment begins with **PI planning**, a two-day event in which every team on the train plans together in one room, maps dependencies between teams on a shared board, and commits to objectives. The idea of a fixed cadence that teams synchronize to echoes the [[Release Train]] used in release engineering. Above the train sit further layers for large solutions and for portfolio budgeting, with dedicated roles at each level.

Supporters point out that regular joint planning gives large organizations a way to see cross-team dependencies they previously discovered too late, and that SAFe gives conservative companies a recognizable path away from annual plans; see [[Waterfall Model]].

Critics, including several signatories of the original agile manifesto, argue that SAFe reintroduces what agile set out to remove: heavy process, central planning, fixed scope committed months ahead, and many roles between developers and users. The framework diagram itself, dense with boxes and acronyms, has become a symbol of that complaint. Teams report spending large amounts of time in planning and estimation ceremonies, and velocity numbers rolled up to management tend to be gamed; see [[Story Points]] and [[Goodhart's Law]]. Others note that a framework adopted wholesale, for its certificates and vocabulary, risks becoming a [[Cargo Cult]].

Alternatives take different positions. Large-Scale Scrum, or LeSS, scales by adding as little structure as possible, with many teams sharing one backlog and one product owner. Team Topologies focuses on designing team boundaries to reduce the coordination needed in the first place; see [[Team Topologies]] and [[Conway's Law]]. The informal Spotify model emphasized autonomous squads instead of shared plans; see [[Spotify Model]].
