A **code of conduct** is a written statement of how members of a community are expected to behave, how to report problems, and what happens when someone crosses the line. In software it usually lives in a file at the root of a repository beside the license and contributing guide, and it covers the project's issue tracker, chat, mailing lists, and events.

Codes of conduct spread through tech conferences first, around 2012, after a run of harassment incidents at events. Open-source projects followed. The **Contributor Covenant**, written by Coraline Ada Ehmke in 2014, became the common template and has been adopted by tens of thousands of projects. Linux replaced its terse "code of conflict" with it in 2018, the same month Linus Torvalds stepped back to work on how he treated contributors; see [[Benevolent Dictator for Life]].

A useful code has three parts:

- **Expectations.** Plain statements of acceptable and unacceptable behavior, such as harassment, personal attacks, and publishing someone's private information.
- **Reporting.** A named, private channel, ideally with more than one person behind it, so someone can report the very person who would otherwise receive the report.
- **Enforcement.** Who decides, what the range of responses is, from a private warning to a permanent ban, and whether decisions can be appealed.

The arguments for one are practical. Projects depend on volunteers, and people quietly leave communities where they are insulted or where nobody intervenes; see [[Open Source Sustainability]]. A written standard tells newcomers what to expect before they invest time, and it gives maintainers something to point at rather than improvising each judgment in public. The underlying goal is a group where people can ask naive questions and disagree about technical choices without fear; see [[Psychological Safety]].

The arguments against tend to be about vagueness and enforcement. Critics worry that broad wording invites selective use, or that the document becomes a weapon in disputes that are really about code. Defenders reply that every community already has rules, and writing them down makes them visible and open to challenge. Both sides tend to agree on one point: a code that is posted and never enforced is worse than none, because it promises protection that doesn't arrive. Small, visible responses to early problems matter more than the text; see [[Broken Windows Theory]] for the related, and disputed, argument about disorder.

Communities that grow quickly face this sharply, as a wave of newcomers arrives without the old culture's habits; see [[Eternal September]]. Norms that once passed by example stop working past the size where everyone knows everyone; see [[Dunbar's Number]]. The same standards apply in [[Code Review]], where the tone of comments on a [[Pull Request]] is much of what a new contributor experiences of a project.
