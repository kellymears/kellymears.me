**A devil's advocate** argues against a proposal, not necessarily from belief, but to test it before anyone commits. The term comes from the Catholic Church. When a candidate was considered for sainthood, an official called the Promoter of the Faith, popularly the advocatus diaboli or devil's advocate, was charged with arguing the case against: questioning miracles, raising doubts about the candidate's character, and looking for flaws in the evidence. Pope Sixtus V formalized the office in 1587. Pope John Paul II reduced the role sharply in 1983, and the number of canonizations rose markedly afterward.

Outside the Church, the idea became a technique for groups. The psychologist Irving Janis, who studied policy disasters like the 1961 Bay of Pigs invasion, recommended assigning someone to challenge a group's favored plan as a defense against premature agreement; see [[Groupthink]]. Similar roles appear in many forms:

- **Red teams** play an adversary to probe plans and systems for weaknesses; see [[Red teaming]].
- **Pre-mortems** ask a group to imagine a plan has already failed and explain why; see [[Pre-mortem]].
- **The tenth man rule** holds that if nine people agree, the tenth must argue otherwise. It was popularized by a 2013 film and is often attributed to Israeli intelligence after the surprise of the 1973 Yom Kippur War, though the details are hard to verify.

The technique works against real biases. Groups tend to collect evidence that supports what they already want to do and discount the rest; see [[Confirmation Bias]] and [[Motivated Reasoning]]. Members who doubt a plan often stay quiet, assuming they're alone; see [[Spiral of Silence]] and [[Conformity]].

Research complicates the picture. The psychologist Charlan Nemeth and colleagues found that an assigned devil's advocate stimulates less fresh thinking than an authentic dissenter, partly because the group knows the objections are role-play and discounts them. Assigned critics can even make a group more confident, having "considered the alternatives." Genuine disagreement, when people feel safe to voice it, does more; see [[Psychological Safety]].

The phrase also has an everyday misuse. "Just playing devil's advocate" can introduce a view the speaker holds while avoiding responsibility for it, or shift the work of defense onto others; see [[Burden of Proof]].

A more productive form of opposition argues against the strongest version of a position rather than the weakest; see [[Steelmanning]]. Formal processes that require dissent can help as long as the dissent is taken seriously, rather than recorded and set aside. The useful question for any group is whether someone in the room could change the decision by objecting, and whether anyone has tried.
