**The OODA loop** describes decision-making as a cycle of four steps: observe, orient, decide, act. It was developed by John Boyd, a United States Air Force fighter pilot and military strategist, in briefings delivered from the 1970s into the 1990s, and it has since spread from military strategy into business, sports, and computer security.

The steps:

1. **Observe** the situation: what's happening, including the results of one's own last action.
2. **Orient**: interpret those observations through experience, culture, training, and mental models.
3. **Decide** on a course of action.
4. **Act**, which changes the situation and starts the next round of observation.

Boyd's thinking drew on air combat. American pilots in the Korean War achieved favorable kill ratios against MiG-15s that were in some respects better aircraft. Boyd attributed part of the advantage to the F-86's bubble canopy, which gave a better view, and to its hydraulic controls, which let pilots shift from one maneuver to the next more quickly. The faster a pilot could see, decide, and change course, the more the opponent was left reacting to situations that no longer existed.

That became the loop's central claim. An actor who cycles faster than an opponent gets "inside their loop": each of the opponent's decisions is based on a picture already out of date, which produces confusion and paralysis; see [[Fog of War]].

Boyd placed the most weight on orientation, which is often lost in simplified retellings. Everyone sees the world through a mental model, and a stale model makes faster cycling useless, since quick decisions on a wrong picture are just quick mistakes; see [[Map and Territory]] and [[Naive Realism]]. Experienced people often skip explicit deciding altogether: they orient, recognize the situation, and act; see [[Expert Intuition]]. Boyd's own diagram was a web of feedback paths between the steps, not a neat circle; see [[Feedback Loop]].

The idea maps onto several modern practices:

- **Software delivery** shortens the cycle from idea to feedback, so teams learn and correct course quickly; see [[Agile Software Development]].
- **Incident response** follows a loop of noticing, diagnosing, and acting under time pressure; see [[Incident Response]].
- **Operations** depend on seeing what systems are actually doing; see [[Observability]].
- **Strategy** favors cheap, undoable moves that can be tried quickly; see [[Reversible Decision]].

Critics note that speed isn't always an advantage, that the loop describes almost any purposeful activity and so explains little on its own, and that business writers have stretched it into a slogan. Its lasting lesson is about adapting: noticing when the world has changed and updating the picture before acting on it.
