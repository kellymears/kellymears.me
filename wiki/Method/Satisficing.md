**Satisficing** means choosing the first option that meets a threshold of acceptability, instead of searching for the best possible option. The economist and psychologist Herbert Simon coined the word in 1956, a blend of "satisfy" and "suffice," as part of a broader argument he called **bounded rationality**: real decision-makers have limited information, time, and computing power, so the ideal of weighing every option is not available to them.

Classical economics assumed people maximize, finding the choice with the greatest expected value among all possibilities. Simon pointed out that this requires knowing every option and its consequences, which is rarely possible and often costs more than the decision is worth. A person looking for an apartment doesn't view every apartment in the city; they set criteria and take the first that meets them, perhaps lowering the bar if the search drags on. Simon argued this was not a failure of rationality but rationality adapted to its limits, and he received the Nobel Prize in economics in 1978 partly for this work.

Satisficing works because searching has costs. Each additional option considered takes time and attention, and the expected gain from continuing to search falls the longer it goes on; see [[Opportunity Cost]] and [[Decision Fatigue]]. Mathematically, the well-known "secretary problem" shows that when options appear one at a time and can't be revisited, the best strategy is to look at roughly the first 37 percent without choosing, then take the first one better than all of those.

Psychologists later distinguished **maximizers**, people who habitually look for the best option, from satisficers. Studies led by Barry Schwartz found that maximizers often make objectively better choices, such as jobs with higher salaries, but report being less satisfied with them and more prone to regret, partly because they keep comparing against the options they rejected; see [[Hedonic Treadmill]].

The idea appears throughout engineering:

- **Algorithms.** Many problems are too expensive to solve optimally, so programs use heuristics that find good solutions quickly; see [[Greedy Algorithm]] and [[Simulated Annealing]].
- **Design.** "Good enough" often wins over "right"; see [[Worse Is Better]] and [[Minimum Viable Product]].
- **Optimization.** Speeding up code past the point anyone notices wastes effort; see [[Premature Optimization]].
- **Decisions.** Spending an hour debating a minor choice costs more than any difference between the options; see [[Law of Triviality]].

Experts satisfice constantly and well. Experienced firefighters and nurses typically consider one option, check it mentally, and act on it, comparing alternatives only if the first seems flawed; see [[Expert Intuition]].

Satisficing has its own risk: setting the bar too low, or never revisiting it. The skill is matching the threshold to the stakes, searching hard for the decisions that are expensive to reverse and settling quickly for the rest.
