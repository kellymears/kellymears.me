**Inversion** is the habit of attacking a problem from the opposite direction. Instead of asking how to succeed, ask what would guarantee failure and avoid it; instead of reasoning forward from what you have, start from the result you want and work back. It doesn't replace forward thinking, but it reliably finds things forward thinking misses.

The slogan comes from mathematics. The nineteenth-century mathematician Carl Gustav Jacobi is credited with advising "invert, always invert," because many hard problems become easier when restated in reverse. Mathematics is full of the move: proof by contradiction assumes the opposite of what you want and derives an absurdity, and many puzzles are solved by starting at the end. The investor Charlie Munger made it famous as a general tool, joking that all he wanted to know was where he was going to die, so he'd never go there.

Inversion works because failure is often easier to see than success. Ask a team how to make a launch go well and you get vague goals; ask how it could go badly and you get a list of specific, checkable risks. That's the logic of imagining a project has already failed and explaining why; see [[Pre-mortem]]. It also suits a world where many things go wrong in predictable ways; see [[Murphy's Law]].

Common uses:

- **Avoiding stupidity before seeking brilliance.** Removing the few behaviors that cause most failures often beats adding clever ones; see [[Margin of Safety]].
- **Design.** Asking how a user could misuse a form, or lose their work, leads to constraints and safe defaults; see [[Forcing Function]] and [[Defensive Default]].
- **Security and reliability.** Thinking like an attacker, or listing every way a dependency could disappear, is inversion applied to systems; see [[Threat Modeling]].
- **Goals.** Working backward from a deadline to today shows which steps are already late.
- **Arguments.** Constructing the strongest case against your own position tests it better than rehearsing the case for it; see [[Steelmanning]].
- **Debugging.** Asking what would have to be true for the bug to occur, then checking those conditions, narrows the search faster than reading code forward; see [[Root Cause Analysis]].

Inversion has limits. Avoiding every failure isn't the same as succeeding: a product with no flaws can still be one nobody wants, and an organization obsessed with avoiding error can stop taking the risks that progress needs. Failure lists also grow without end, so inversion works best paired with judgment about which risks matter most; see [[Pareto Principle]].

It pairs naturally with other ways of escaping the default line of thought, such as rebuilding a problem from its fundamentals or following consequences a step further than usual; see [[First Principles Thinking]] and [[Second-Order Thinking]].
