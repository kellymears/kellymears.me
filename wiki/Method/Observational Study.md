An **observational study** watches what happens to people, or anything else, without assigning a treatment. The researcher records who smokes and who doesn't, who takes a drug and who doesn't, and compares outcomes. In an experiment, by contrast, the researcher decides who gets what, ideally at random; see [[Randomized Controlled Trial]].

Observation is often the only option. It would be unethical to assign people to smoke for decades, impossible to randomize where people grow up, and too slow to run a trial for every question. Much of what is known about the causes of disease comes from observational work.

The main designs:

- **Cohort studies** follow a group over time, recording exposures and later outcomes. The Framingham Heart Study, started in 1948, followed residents of one Massachusetts town and identified blood pressure and cholesterol as risk factors for heart disease. The British Doctors Study, from 1951, tracked tens of thousands of physicians' smoking and deaths.
- **Case-control studies** start from the outcome. Researchers gather people who have a disease and similar people who don't, then look back at what differed. Richard Doll and Austin Bradford Hill's 1950 study of lung cancer patients was of this kind; see [[Bradford Hill Criteria]].
- **Cross-sectional studies** take a snapshot of a population at one moment, measuring exposure and outcome together, which shows association but not which came first.

The core weakness is that groups chosen by circumstance differ in many ways. People who take vitamins also tend to exercise more, smoke less, and see doctors more often, so they would be healthier anyway; see [[Confounding]]. The textbook warning is hormone replacement therapy: observational studies suggested it protected women's hearts, but a large randomized trial reported in 2002 found it slightly raised the risk of heart disease. The women who chose it had been healthier to begin with.

Other traps include the outcome causing the exposure rather than the reverse, the way participants are selected creating patterns that aren't in the population, and people changing behavior because they are watched; see [[Correlation and Causation]], [[Selection Bias]], [[Collider bias]], and [[Hawthorne Effect]].

Researchers fight back with careful design: measuring and adjusting for known confounders, matching similar people, checking whether the effect grows with the dose, and looking for circumstances that act like a random assignment; see [[Natural Experiment]]. Results reported as a ratio of risks between groups need care in reading; see [[Relative Risk]].

The same issues apply far outside medicine. Product analytics comparing users who adopted a feature with those who didn't is an observational study, with the same confounding, which is why teams run controlled experiments when they can; see [[AB Testing]]. Combining many observational studies can't remove a bias they all share; see [[Meta-Analysis]].
