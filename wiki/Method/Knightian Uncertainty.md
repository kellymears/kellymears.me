**Knightian uncertainty** is uncertainty that can't be expressed as a known probability. The economist Frank Knight drew the distinction in the 1921 book Risk, Uncertainty and Profit. **Risk**, in Knight's sense, applies when the possible outcomes and their odds are known or can be estimated from experience: a roulette wheel, mortality tables, the failure rate of a well-tested component. **Uncertainty** applies when they can't: whether a new invention will catch on, or whether a war will break out. Knight argued that profit, as distinct from wages or interest, is the reward for bearing the second kind, since anything calculable can be insured or priced away.

John Maynard Keynes made a related argument in the same year and returned to it in 1937, writing that on questions such as the price of copper or the rate of interest twenty years hence, "we simply do not know." The idea reached a wide public through a 2002 Pentagon briefing in which the US defense secretary distinguished known knowns, known unknowns and **unknown unknowns**.

Standard decision theory weighs outcomes by their probabilities and picks the option with the best average; see [[Expected Value]] and [[Monte Carlo Method]]. Under Knightian uncertainty those weights are guesses, and every calculation built on them inherits their false precision. The 2008 financial crisis is often cited as a case. Risk models built on historical price data assigned tiny probabilities to a nationwide fall in house prices, an event with little precedent in the data they were fitted to. Such rare, extreme events are the subject of [[Black Swan]].

People do treat the two kinds differently. In 1961 Daniel Ellsberg described a choice in which most people prefer to bet on an urn with a known mix of colored balls rather than one with an unknown mix, even when nothing favors either; see [[Ellsberg paradox]].

Practical responses accept that the odds can't be known and aim for decisions that hold up anyway. Planners sketch several divergent futures instead of forecasting one; see [[Scenario Planning]]. Engineers and investors build in slack for errors they can't foresee; see [[Margin of Safety]]. Some strategies go further and seek positions that gain from volatility; see [[Antifragility]]. Where harms could be severe and irreversible, regulators may act before the evidence is settled; see [[Precautionary Principle]]. Settling for good enough when information is limited fits here too; see [[Satisficing]].

The distinction is a spectrum more than a binary. Forecasting research suggests that careful people can often put useful numbers on questions that seem unknowable, which narrows the zone of true Knightian uncertainty without eliminating it; see [[Superforecasting]]. The [[Cynefin Framework]] builds the difference into the choice of method: analysis where cause and effect can be known in advance, small experiments where they can't.
