A **thought experiment** imagines a scenario and reasons through what would happen, without running it. It can test a theory, expose a contradiction, or show what an idea really implies. Physicists made the method famous, and Ernst Mach and later Einstein used the German word *Gedankenexperiment*, but philosophy, mathematics, and engineering use it constantly.

Some of the best known:

- **Galileo's falling stones.** If heavier objects fall faster, tie a heavy stone to a light one. The light one should slow the heavy one, so the pair falls slower; but the pair is heavier, so it should fall faster. The contradiction suggests that all objects fall at the same rate, without dropping anything.
- **Einstein's light beam.** As a teenager Einstein imagined chasing a beam of light and asked what he would see if he caught up, a puzzle that fed into his later theory of relativity; see [[Time Dilation]].
- **Maxwell's demon.** A tiny being sorting fast and slow molecules seemed to break the laws of heat, a puzzle that led a century later to the physics of information.
- **Schrödinger's cat**, devised in 1935 to show how absurd quantum theory became when applied to everyday objects.
- **The Two Generals.** Two armies can never be certain they have agreed on a time to attack over an unreliable messenger, a result that shapes network protocols; see [[Two Generals Problem]].
- **Ethics and mind.** The trolley problem and the Chinese room probe intuitions about harm and about understanding; see [[Trolley Problem]] and [[Chinese room]].

Thought experiments do several kinds of work. Some are **refutations**: they assume a claim and derive an absurdity. Some **explore**: they trace the consequences of principles already accepted, as in reasoning from fundamentals; see [[First Principles Thinking]]. Some **pump intuitions**: the philosopher Daniel Dennett called these intuition pumps, devices that lead a reader to a conviction by the way the scenario is framed.

That last kind is also the main risk. A scenario's details, wording, and simplifications shape the answer, and changing one detail, which Dennett called turning the knobs, can flip people's intuitions. A thought experiment smuggles in assumptions, such as frictionless planes, perfectly rational agents, or perfect information, and its conclusions are only as good as those; see [[Map and Territory]]. It can show that a theory is inconsistent, but not that a consistent theory is true; that takes evidence; see [[Falsifiability]].

Everyday engineering is full of small thought experiments: what happens if this server disappears, if two requests arrive at once, if the user presses back halfway through; see [[Pre-mortem]] and [[Inversion]]. When imagination runs out, simulation takes over, running the experiment on a computer; see [[Monte Carlo Method]].
