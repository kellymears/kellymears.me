**Sturgeon's law** holds that ninety percent of everything is crud. Theodore Sturgeon, a science fiction writer, offered it as a defense of the genre. Critics dismissed science fiction by pointing to its worst stories; Sturgeon replied that the same share of novels, films, and every other form was poor too, so the genre should be judged by its best work like any other. The idea appeared in print in Sturgeon's column in *Venture Science Fiction* in 1958, though Sturgeon had reportedly been making the point in talks for several years before, and later editors softened "crud" to the blunter word now attached to it.

The number isn't a measurement. It's a statement about base rates: in any field, most output is mediocre, so finding mediocre examples tells you nothing about the field. Holding up the worst of a rival tradition while comparing it to the best of one's own is a standard rhetorical trick; see [[Base Rate Fallacy]] and [[Selection Bias]].

The law also explains why the past seems better. Old music, films, and books arrive already filtered, since only the good ones were remembered and reissued. The other ninety percent of every earlier decade has quietly vanished, which makes current work, encountered unfiltered, look worse by comparison; see [[Survivorship Bias]]. Works that remain in circulation after decades are therefore likely to stay; see [[Lindy Effect]].

Quality tends to be distributed very unevenly, with a small share of works accounting for most of the value and attention; see [[Pareto Principle]]. That makes filtering the real work in any large body of content. Editors, critics, peer reviewers, and recommendation algorithms all exist to find the ten percent; see [[Peer Review]] and [[Recommender System]]. When the cost of producing something falls sharply, as it did with blogs, app stores, and generated text, the volume of crud rises and filtering becomes more valuable, not less; see [[Email Spam]].

Some apply the law to their own work. Most first drafts, sketches, prototypes, and ideas are poor, and producing many of them is how the good ones are found; see [[Minimum Viable Product]]. The point appears in design and writing advice as an argument for volume and revision over polishing the first attempt.

The law is sometimes quoted as a sneer at whatever the speaker dislikes, which turns it upside down. Sturgeon's point was generous: a form deserves to be judged by what it can do at its best. It sits beside other wry laws stated as if they were measurements, which each carry a real observation inside an exaggerated number; see [[Murphy's Law]] and [[Parkinson's Law]].
