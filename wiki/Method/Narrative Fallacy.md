The **narrative fallacy** is the habit of fitting a story onto a sequence of facts, linking them with causes whether or not the causes are real. Nassim Nicholas Taleb named it in *The Black Swan* (2007), arguing that people can barely look at a series of events without weaving an explanation into them, and that the explanation then feels like knowledge; see [[Black Swan]].

Stories compress. A list of unrelated facts is hard to remember; the same facts joined by "because" are easy. That economy is useful, but it pushes toward too much coherence. Details that fit the story are kept, details that don't are forgotten, and chance is recast as cause. Memory itself is rebuilt each time it is recalled, so the story can rewrite the record that would correct it; see [[False Memory]].

The fallacy feeds on hindsight. Once an outcome is known, the path to it looks obvious, and the signals that pointed elsewhere fade; see [[Hindsight Bias]]. Business writing is a common example. Books that study successful companies and extract the habits behind their success tend to find the same companies doing worse a few years later. The psychologist Phil Rosenzweig argued that the reported habits were often inferred from the success, not the other way round; see [[Halo Effect]] and [[Survivorship Bias]].

Adding detail makes a story more convincing and less likely. Every added link is another thing that has to be true, so the probability of the whole can only fall, yet people rate the more detailed version as more probable; see [[Representativeness Heuristic]]. Stories also favor agents over circumstances, so outcomes get credited to the character or skill of the people involved; see [[Fundamental Attribution Error]].

Random data provides plenty of material. A streak of wins, a cluster of cases, or a stock's recent climb invites an explanation even when chance would produce the same pattern; see [[Texas Sharpshooter Fallacy]]. Daniel Kahneman described flight instructors who concluded that praise made pilots worse and criticism made them better, when a very good or very bad landing is usually followed by a more ordinary one regardless; see [[Regression to the Mean]].

The term "just-so story," after Rudyard Kipling's fables about how the leopard got its spots, is used in a similar sense for evolutionary explanations that fit the facts but were never tested; see [[Falsifiability]].

Narrative isn't the enemy. People understand and remember through stories, and a well-known plot shape can carry real meaning; see [[Hero's Journey]]. The remedies are to ask what the story predicts that could be checked, to write down forecasts before the outcome is known, and to ask how often things like this happen without any story at all; see [[Superforecasting]] and [[Base Rate Fallacy]].
