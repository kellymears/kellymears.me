The **burden of proof** is the obligation to support a claim with evidence. In argument, it rests on whoever asserts something, not on whoever doubts it. A person who says a new drug cures a disease must show that it does; skeptics don't have to prove that it doesn't. The term comes from law, where it decides which side loses when the evidence is inconclusive.

The reasoning is practical. There are endlessly many claims that can't easily be disproved, and if each had to be refuted before it could be dismissed, anyone could tie up an argument indefinitely; see [[Brandolini's Law]]. Bertrand Russell made the point in 1952 with a thought experiment. If he claimed a china teapot was orbiting the Sun between Earth and Mars, too small to see with any telescope, nobody could disprove it. But it would be absurd to treat that as reason to believe it, or to call doubters unreasonable. This became known as **Russell's teapot**. Christopher Hitchens later compressed it: "What can be asserted without evidence can also be dismissed without evidence."

The burden also scales with the claim. Carl Sagan popularized the line "extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence," now called the **Sagan standard**. A claim that fits what is already known needs little support; one that would overturn well-established findings needs a great deal, because it starts with a low prior probability; see [[Bayes' Theorem]] and [[Base Rate Fallacy]]. A study reporting faster-than-light particles in 2011 was rightly met with doubt, and the result traced back to a loose cable.

Science formalizes the burden through the **null hypothesis**: by default, an intervention is assumed to have no effect, and a study must produce data that would be unlikely if that were true; see [[Statistical Significance]]. Absence of evidence isn't proof of absence, but a claim with no evidence earns no belief. Claims built to be untestable can't carry any burden at all; see [[Falsifiability]].

Common moves to dodge the burden:

- **Shifting it.** "Prove it's not true" or "you can't explain it otherwise," which demand that doubters disprove the claim.
- **Retreating.** Defending a modest version of a claim when challenged, then returning to the bold one; see [[Motte and Bailey]].
- **Repetition.** Repeating a claim until it feels familiar, which makes it seem true without adding evidence; see [[Illusory Truth Effect]].
- **Appeals to ignorance.** Treating a gap in current knowledge as support for a particular explanation.

In engineering, the burden falls on the proposal for change: the person who wants a new dependency, rewrite, or optimization should show it helps, ideally with measurements; see [[Premature Optimization]] and [[Plausible Mechanism]]. The reverse also applies to long-standing safeguards, where the burden falls on whoever wants to remove them to understand why they exist; see [[Chesterton's Fence]].
