A **logical fallacy** is an error in reasoning that makes an argument fail to support its conclusion, however persuasive it sounds. Aristotle catalogued thirteen in his *Sophistical Refutations*, and the list has grown ever since.

Fallacies divide into two kinds.

**Formal fallacies** are errors in an argument's structure, wrong whatever the subject. The classic is affirming the consequent: if it rained, the street is wet; the street is wet; so it rained. A street cleaner could have wet it. The reverse form, denying the consequent, is valid; see [[Boolean Algebra]] and [[Vacuous Truth]].

**Informal fallacies** depend on content and context. Common ones:

- **Ad hominem** attacks the person making an argument instead of the argument.
- **Straw man** refutes a weaker version of the opposing view; its remedy is engaging the strongest version; see [[Steelmanning]].
- **False dilemma** presents two options as the only ones when others exist.
- **Slippery slope** claims one step will inevitably lead to an extreme, without showing why each step follows.
- **Post hoc ergo propter hoc**, "after this, therefore because of this," treats sequence as cause; see [[Correlation and Causation]].
- **Begging the question** assumes the conclusion in the premises.
- **No true Scotsman**, named by the philosopher Antony Flew in 1971, redefines a group to exclude a counterexample: no true Scotsman puts sugar on his porridge.
- **Whataboutism** answers a criticism by pointing to someone else's fault, which may be true but leaves the criticism standing.
- **Retreating** from a bold claim to a modest one when challenged; see [[Motte and Bailey]].
- **Drawing the target after shooting**, finding a pattern in data and then treating it as predicted; see [[Texas Sharpshooter Fallacy]].

Probabilistic reasoning has its own fallacies: [[Gambler's Fallacy]], [[Base Rate Fallacy]], and inferring individual traits from group averages; see [[Ecological Fallacy]]. Decisions add the [[Sunk Cost Fallacy]].

Three cautions keep the idea useful.

First, a fallacious argument doesn't make its conclusion false. Concluding otherwise is itself called the fallacy fallacy.

Second, many informal "fallacies" are reasonable shortcuts in context. Deferring to experts is often sensible; it fails only when the authority is outside their field or the evidence is available directly. Some slopes really are slippery, when there is evidence each step makes the next likelier. The question is who must show what; see [[Burden of Proof]].

Third, naming fallacies has become a debating tactic of its own: shouting "ad hominem" at any criticism, or listing fallacies faster than anyone can answer them. Refuting nonsense takes far more effort than producing it; see [[Brandolini's Law]].
