**Survey design** is the craft of asking questions so the answers mean something. A survey turns opinions, habits, and experiences into numbers, and every step of that conversion, from who gets asked to how the question is phrased, can bend the result.

Wording has large effects. In a classic 1940 experiment, Americans were asked whether the country should allow public speeches against democracy, or whether it should forbid them. Far more people were willing to "not allow" such speeches than to "forbid" them, though the policies are identical. The words chosen set the frame; see [[Framing Effect]].

Common problems in the questions themselves:

- **Leading questions.** Phrasing that signals the expected answer, such as "how much did you enjoy," pushes responses toward it.
- **Double-barreled questions.** "Is the app fast and easy to use?" asks two things and gets one answer.
- **Order effects.** Earlier questions put ideas in mind that color later ones; see [[Priming]]. Options at the start or end of a list get picked more often; see [[Serial Position Effect]].
- **Anchors.** A number in the question, or in the answer brackets, pulls estimates toward it; see [[Anchoring Effect]].
- **Defaults.** A preselected option or a "no change" choice attracts answers on its own; see [[Status quo bias]].
- **Social desirability.** People overreport voting and exercise and underreport drinking, especially when talking to an interviewer.

Answer formats carry choices too. Rensis Likert introduced the agree-disagree scale in 1932, and the **Likert scale**, typically five or seven points from strongly disagree to strongly agree, became the default for measuring attitudes. Whether to offer a neutral midpoint, and whether the answers can be averaged as numbers, are both contested. First, someone has to decide which questions actually capture a concept like satisfaction or trust; see [[Operationalization]].

Then there's who answers. The 1936 Literary Digest poll gathered over two million responses and confidently predicted that Franklin Roosevelt would lose the presidential election, which Roosevelt won in a landslide. Its ballots went to readers, car owners, and telephone subscribers, who skewed wealthier, and those who replied skewed further. A far smaller but well-chosen sample would have done better; see [[Sampling bias]]. Random sampling is the cure in principle, but falling response rates mean most surveys now rely on weighting to correct for who didn't reply.

A survey's margin of error covers only random sampling noise, not the biases above; see [[Confidence Interval]]. Counting everyone is a different and much more expensive exercise; see [[Census]].

Pretesting questions on real people who think aloud while answering catches confusions the author can't see; see [[Usability Testing]]. Where possible, check stated answers against behavior, and remember that being studied can itself change it; see [[Hawthorne Effect]].
