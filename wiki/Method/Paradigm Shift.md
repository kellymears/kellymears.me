A **paradigm shift** is a fundamental change in the framework a scientific field works within. The idea comes from Thomas Kuhn's *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962), one of the most cited books of the twentieth century. Kuhn argued that science doesn't advance by steady accumulation. It alternates between long, quiet periods and occasional upheavals.

In the quiet periods, which Kuhn called **normal science**, a field shares a paradigm: accepted theories, methods, standard examples, and a sense of which problems matter. Scientists solve puzzles the paradigm defines, and they don't question its foundations. Results that don't fit, called anomalies, are usually set aside as errors or problems for later; see [[Confirmation Bias]].

Anomalies can accumulate until they can't be ignored. The field enters a crisis, rival frameworks appear, and eventually one replaces the old. Kuhn's examples included the move from an Earth-centered to a Sun-centered astronomy, Antoine Lavoisier's oxygen chemistry displacing the theory of phlogiston, and Einstein's physics replacing Newton's at high speeds. In geology, continental drift was proposed by Alfred Wegener in 1912 and largely rejected until evidence from the seafloor in the 1960s made plate tectonics the new consensus; see [[Plate Tectonics]].

Kuhn's most contested claim was **incommensurability**: rival paradigms can't be fully compared on neutral ground, because they define terms, problems, and acceptable evidence differently. "Mass" doesn't mean quite the same thing for Newton as for Einstein. That made theory choice look partly like conversion, not purely logic, and critics accused Kuhn of making science irrational. Kuhn rejected that reading, saying that values like accuracy, simplicity, and fruitfulness still guided choice, though they didn't dictate it; see [[Occam's Razor]].

The book was written partly against Karl Popper's view that science advances by bold conjectures and attempts at refutation; see [[Falsifiability]]. Kuhn argued that scientists rarely abandon a theory when a test fails; they adjust it, as normal science requires. Imre Lakatos later tried to reconcile the two, describing research programs that survive anomalies as long as they keep producing new predictions.

The social side drew attention as well. Max Planck had remarked that new truths win not by convincing opponents but because opponents eventually die, and Kuhn's account gave the remark a framework. Training, reputation, and journals all reward work within the reigning paradigm; see [[Peer Review]] and [[Path Dependence]].

The term itself shifted. The philosopher Margaret Masterman counted more than twenty senses of "paradigm" in the book, and Kuhn later proposed narrower terms. Meanwhile "paradigm shift" became business jargon for any significant change, stretched so far it now often signals hype more than meaning; see [[Map and Territory]].
