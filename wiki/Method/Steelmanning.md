**Steelmanning** is engaging with the strongest version of a view you disagree with, ideally one its holders would recognize and endorse, before arguing against it. It's the deliberate opposite of the **straw man**, which replaces someone's actual position with a weaker, sillier one that's easy to knock down. Defeating a straw man proves nothing about the real argument. Defeating a steel man actually tests your own view.

The underlying idea is old. Philosophers call it the *principle of charity*: when someone's words can be read several ways, assume the most reasonable reading. Rapoport's rules, popularized by the philosopher Daniel Dennett, give a practical recipe for criticizing someone's view:

- **Restate their position** so clearly and fairly that they say "thanks, I wish I'd put it that way."
- **List the points of agreement**, especially uncommon ones.
- **Mention anything you learned** from them.
- **Only then** offer your rebuttal or criticism.

There are several reasons to bother. Arguing against the best version of a view is the only way to learn whether you're right, since nearly any position looks wrong when only its weakest form is examined. It protects against [[Confirmation Bias]], which steers us toward arguments that confirm what we already believe. It makes the other person far more likely to listen, since people disengage when they feel misrepresented. And sometimes the steel man is simply correct, and you change your mind, which is the point of reasoning at all.

Straw men take many forms. Exaggerating a claim into an absolute, the move described in [[Exhaustive Claim]], picking the most extreme member of a group as its representative, and answering a question nobody asked are common ones. Its mirror image is the [[Motte and Bailey]], where an arguer retreats to an easy-to-defend version of their own claim under pressure. A related habit, [[Hanlon's Razor]], applies charity to motives instead of arguments: assume a mistake before malice.

Steelmanning has its own pitfalls. It can drift into arguing for a version of the view nobody holds, which is useful for thinking but unfair if presented as the opponent's real position. It takes effort and some knowledge of the other side, so it's easiest where someone has made their case in writing. Online, [[Poe's Law]] makes it hard to tell sincere positions from parody, and the charitable reading isn't always the right one.

In technical work, the same habit improves [[Code Review]] and design discussions. Before rejecting a proposed approach, explain why its author chose it and what it gets right. Often that reveals a constraint the reviewer missed, the lesson of [[Chesterton's Fence]], and an [[Adversarial Review]] is most useful when the critic understands what the design is trying to do.
