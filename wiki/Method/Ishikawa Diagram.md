An **Ishikawa diagram**, also called a **fishbone diagram** or **cause-and-effect diagram**, is a chart for organizing the possible causes of a problem. The problem goes in a box at the right, the fish's head. A horizontal spine runs left from it, and slanted bones branch off the spine, one for each broad category of cause. Specific causes hang off those bones as smaller branches, and causes of causes branch off those in turn.

The diagram is named for Kaoru Ishikawa, a Japanese engineer and professor who popularized it in the 1960s as part of the quality movement in Japanese industry. An early use is usually dated to work at the Kawasaki shipyards. It later became one of the "seven basic tools of quality," alongside the check sheet, histogram, scatter diagram, stratification, Pareto chart and [[Control Chart]], a set meant to be simple enough for factory workers to use rather than only statisticians; see [[Toyota Production System]].

Manufacturing teams often seed the bones with a standard list of categories, the "six Ms": manpower, machines, methods, materials, measurement and mother nature, meaning the environment. Service businesses use variants such as policies, procedures, people and place. The categories are prompts, not a theory. Their job is to stop a group from fixating on the first cause someone names. A team trying to explain why a batch of welds failed might list operator training under people, a worn electrode under machines, an outdated specification under methods, a new steel supplier under materials and a humid week under environment.

The diagram is usually built in a group session. People call out possible causes, which are placed on the bone where they fit, a step that resembles clustering ideas in an [[Affinity Diagram]]. Each candidate can then be pushed deeper by asking why it would happen; see [[Five Whys]].

What the diagram produces is a map of hypotheses, not a verdict. Every branch is a suspicion until data supports it, and the usual next step is to check which causes actually occur and how often, which often shows that a few account for most of the defects; see [[Pareto Principle]]. Its tree shape also understates how causes interact. A failure often needs several contributing conditions at once, which a fishbone lists side by side without saying that they combine; see [[Swiss cheese model]]. Tools that model combinations explicitly include [[Fault Tree Analysis]], which works backward from a failure through AND and OR gates, and [[Failure Mode and Effects Analysis]], which works forward from components to what could go wrong.

The diagram fits naturally into the review after an incident, where its breadth helps keep discussion on systems and conditions rather than on whoever was nearest the failure; see [[Blameless Postmortem]] and [[Root Cause Analysis]].
