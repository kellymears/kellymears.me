**A just culture** is an approach to mistakes in organizations, especially in aviation and health care, that aims to be fair to individuals while learning from what went wrong. It rests on a simple observation: if people are punished for every error, they stop reporting errors, and the organization loses the information it needs to prevent the next one.

The psychologist James Reason, known for describing accidents as holes lining up in layers of defense, set out the idea in a 1997 book on organizational accidents. The engineer David Marx developed a widely used version for health care around 2001. It distinguishes three kinds of behavior:

- **Human error**: an inadvertent slip or mistake, such as picking up the wrong vial. The response is to console the person and fix the system that made the error easy.
- **At-risk behavior**: a choice whose risk the person didn't recognize or thought justified, often a shortcut that has become normal. The response is coaching and removing the incentives for the shortcut; see [[Normalization of Deviance]].
- **Reckless behavior**: consciously disregarding a substantial and unjustifiable risk. The response can include discipline.

Outcomes aren't supposed to determine the response. Two nurses who make the same slip deserve the same treatment, whether or not a patient happened to be harmed. Judging by outcome is a natural bias; see [[Hindsight Bias]] and [[Fundamental Attribution Error]].

Aviation provides a model. NASA's confidential Aviation Safety Reporting System, set up in 1976, lets pilots and controllers report errors and close calls, and offers limited protection from penalties to those who file. Thousands of reports a year feed into safety improvements. Commercial aviation's safety record is often credited partly to this habit of reporting.

Health care has been slower. Clinicians involved in errors that harm patients often suffer guilt, anxiety, and burnout, and are sometimes called second victims. A Tennessee nurse's criminal conviction in 2022 over a fatal medication error alarmed many nurses, who warned it would discourage reporting.

Critics, including the safety researcher Sidney Dekker, point out that deciding what counts as reckless is itself a judgment made by people with power, after the fact. A just culture, in this view, is less a formula for sorting people than a commitment to ask what in the system set them up to fail.

Software engineering borrowed the idea for incident reviews that focus on how a failure happened, not who caused it; see [[Blameless Postmortem]] and [[Incident Response]]. It depends on people feeling safe to speak; see [[Psychological Safety]]. Production lines that let any worker stop the line apply the same trust; see [[Andon Cord]].
