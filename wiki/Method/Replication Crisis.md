The **replication crisis** is the realization that a large share of published scientific findings fail when independent researchers repeat the studies. It hit psychology hardest and most publicly, but medicine, economics, and other fields found similar problems. A finding that can't be reproduced may never have been real, and much of what textbooks taught rested on single, small studies.

The landmark was the **Reproducibility Project** in 2015, in which the Open Science Collaboration repeated 100 studies from leading psychology journals. About 97% of the originals had reported significant results. Only about 36% of the replications did, and the effects that did replicate were on average about half their original size. Other large replication efforts found similar rates. Some famous effects shrank to nothing, among them *ego depletion*, the idea that willpower is a resource used up by exertion. A 2016 replication across 23 labs found an effect close to zero, which is why [[Decision Fatigue]] is now discussed with caution. Power posing and several social priming effects suffered similar fates.

The causes were mostly not fraud but ordinary incentives and methods:

- **Small samples.** Underpowered studies detect true effects unreliably, and the significant results they do produce overstate effect sizes.
- **Flexible analysis.** Trying many analyses and reporting the one that worked, the practice of [[P-Hacking]], produces false positives at high rates.
- **Publication bias.** Journals preferred novel, positive results, so null results disappeared from the record, as in [[Publication Bias]], and the literature became a [[Survivorship Bias]] sample of lucky studies.
- **Hypothesizing after results are known,** which turns exploration into apparent confirmation.
- **Career incentives** that rewarded publication count over reliability, [[Goodhart's Law]] applied to science.

The reforms are mostly about committing in advance. **Preregistration** records hypotheses and analysis plans before data collection. **Registered reports** have journals accept a study on its design before results exist, removing the reward for positive findings. Larger samples, multi-lab collaborations, open data, and routine replication are now far more common. The underlying lesson is about [[Falsifiability]]: a claim earns trust by surviving attempts to break it, not by appearing once in print. [[AB Testing]] in industry faces the same pitfalls on a smaller scale, and [[Regression to the Mean]] explains why surprising first results usually come back smaller.
