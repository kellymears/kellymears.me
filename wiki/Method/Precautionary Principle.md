The **precautionary principle** says that when an activity threatens serious or irreversible harm, the absence of full scientific certainty is not a reason to wait before acting. It reverses a common default. Instead of requiring proof of harm before restricting something, it allows restriction while the evidence is still incomplete, and in stronger versions it asks the people proposing the activity to show that it's safe; see [[Burden of Proof]].

The idea took shape in West German environmental policy in the 1970s, under the name Vorsorgeprinzip, roughly "foresight principle." Its best-known statement is Principle 15 of the 1992 Rio Declaration on Environment and Development, which says that where there are threats of serious or irreversible damage, lack of full scientific certainty shall not be used as a reason for postponing cost-effective measures to prevent environmental degradation. The European Union wrote the principle into its treaties in the 1990s, and it shapes European rules on chemicals, food, and genetically modified crops.

Its defenders point to cases where waiting for certainty was costly. Leaded gasoline, asbestos, and the chemicals that thinned the ozone layer all caused damage that was suspected long before it was proven. The argument is strongest under three conditions:

- **Irreversibility.** Harm that can't be undone deserves more caution than harm that can; see [[Reversible Decision]].
- **Ruin.** When the downside is catastrophic, an expected-value calculation that averages it away is misleading; see [[Expected Value]] and [[Margin of Safety]].
- **Unknown distributions.** When the worst outcomes are poorly understood, past experience understates them; see [[Black Swan]].

The main criticism is that the strong version can't be followed consistently. Inaction carries risks too. Banning a pesticide may raise food prices or encourage a worse substitute, and delaying a drug costs the patients who would have been helped. Cass Sunstein argued in 2005 that because risks sit on every side of a decision, a principle that forbids any risky action forbids everything, including itself. In practice, critics say, it gets applied selectively to new and unfamiliar risks while old, familiar ones pass unexamined; see [[Status quo bias]].

Related objections concern side effects and incentives. Restrictions shift activity elsewhere, sometimes somewhere less careful; see [[Unintended consequences]]. Safety measures can also change behavior in ways that eat into their benefit; see [[Risk Compensation]].

Weaker versions survive these criticisms better. Read as "don't use uncertainty as an excuse for doing nothing" and "weigh irreversible harms heavily," the principle is close to ordinary prudence: imagine how a decision could fail before committing to it, and keep the option of changing course; see [[Pre-mortem]] and [[Opportunity Cost]].
