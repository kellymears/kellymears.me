**Motte and bailey** is a rhetorical pattern named by the philosopher Nicholas Shackel, in "The Vacuity of Postmodernist Methodology" (*Metaphilosophy*, 2005), for a medieval castle design: a lightly defended bailey — the useful, livable ground — sits beside a motte, a steep mound easy to hold against attack. The argument version advances a bold, contentious claim (the bailey) but, when challenged, retreats to a modest, easily defended version of it (the motte); once the challenge passes, the bold claim quietly resumes operating. The two claims share a name, so the retreat reads as clarification rather than as abandoning the position under pressure.

Identifying it fairly requires separating the equivocation from the underlying claims themselves: the pattern is not that a modest claim and a bold one exist, it is that a single label is used for both and the speaker moves between them depending on which is under attack. A useful test is whether the modest version, if it were offered on its own from the start, would have supported the same conclusions the bold version was used to argue for. If not, something changed at the retreat, not just the wording.

The caution runs the other way too: the accusation is frequently levelled at people who hold one carefully qualified position, not two, and get accused of switching every time they add a caveat a critic finds inconvenient. Distinguishing genuine equivocation from ordinary nuance is the harder and more important half of the skill — see [[Exhaustive Claim]] for the neighbouring failure of stating more than the evidence supports, and [[Falsifiability]] for what a claim has to risk to count as a real one. The pattern also shows up at the group level whenever a movement's stated, defensible aims and its practical demands drift apart, which is part of what makes the edges of an [[Overton Window]] hard to pin down.
