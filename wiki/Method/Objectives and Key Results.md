**Objectives and Key Results**, or **OKRs**, is a goal-setting framework that pairs a qualitative objective with a few measurable results that would show it has been achieved. An objective states a direction in plain language, such as "make onboarding painless for new customers." Its key results are numbers with deadlines, such as "cut median setup time from two days to two hours this quarter" and "raise the share of trials that reach first use from 40 to 60 percent." The objective says why; the key results say how anyone will know.

The idea descends from **management by objectives**, which Peter Drucker described in 1954: managers and the people they supervise agree on goals, and performance is judged against them rather than against activity. Andy Grove adapted it at Intel in the 1970s, adding the emphasis on a few measurable key results and frequent review. John Doerr, who had worked at Intel under Grove, introduced the system to Google in 1999, and its spread through technology companies followed.

Several conventions distinguish OKRs from other target systems. Teams are encouraged to set few objectives, often three to five a quarter, so that choosing them forces trade-offs; see [[Opportunity Cost]]. Goals are meant to connect, with a team's objectives supporting the organization's, though many practitioners now prefer alignment negotiated across teams to strict top-down cascading. Google popularized **stretch goals** scored from zero to one, where reaching about 0.7 counts as success, on the theory that ambitious targets draw out effort a safe target wouldn't. Scores are meant to be visible across the company and kept separate from pay.

That last rule addresses the most common failure. Once key results set compensation, people negotiate easy targets and steer toward the number rather than the purpose; see [[Goodhart's Law]] and [[Cobra Effect]]. Even without money attached, a key result that captures only what is easy to count crowds out what matters; see [[McNamara Fallacy]] and [[Operationalization]]. Research on motivation also suggests that external rewards can erode interest in work people already find meaningful; see [[Overjustification effect]].

Good key results describe outcomes rather than outputs. "Ship the new onboarding flow" is a task; "halve setup time" is a result, and it leaves room to discover that the flow wasn't the problem. A useful set often mixes a lagging measure of the outcome with a [[Leading Indicator]] expected to move first, and engineering teams frequently anchor reliability objectives in a [[Service Level Objective]].

Critics note that quarterly cycles suit some work poorly, that the surrounding rituals can harden into bureaucracy, and that the framework assumes goals can be stated well in advance. In fast-changing or exploratory work, teams often hold their OKRs loosely and revise them mid-cycle; see [[Feedback Loop]] and [[Cynefin Framework]].
