**Relative risk** compares how likely an outcome is in two groups as a ratio. If 2 of every 1,000 smokers in a study develop a disease and 1 of every 1,000 nonsmokers do, the relative risk is 2: smokers are twice as likely. **Absolute risk** is the plain probability in each group, here 0.2 and 0.1 percent, and the **absolute risk difference** is the gap between them, one in a thousand.

Both are correct, and they tell very different stories. A treatment that cuts a risk from 2 percent to 1 percent produces a 50 percent relative reduction and a 1 percentage point absolute reduction; see [[Percentage Point]]. "Halves your risk" and "helps one person in a hundred" describe the same result. Press releases, advertisements, and headlines tend to choose the relative number because it sounds larger.

A useful translation is the **number needed to treat**: one divided by the absolute risk difference, so here 100. It answers a practical question: how many people must take the treatment for one to benefit? Its counterpart, the number needed to harm, does the same for side effects, and putting the two side by side often clarifies a decision more than any ratio.

The classic cautionary case is a 1995 warning in the UK that newer contraceptive pills doubled the risk of dangerous blood clots compared with older ones. The relative risk was 2, but the absolute risk rose from about 1 in 7,000 women to about 2 in 7,000. Many women stopped taking the pill, and the following year saw an estimated 13,000 additional abortions in England and Wales, a harm far larger than the one the warning addressed.

Some subtleties:

- **The base matters.** Doubling a rare risk changes little; doubling a common one changes a lot; see [[Base Rate Fallacy]].
- **Odds ratios** are reported by case-control studies, which can't measure risk directly. For rare outcomes they approximate the relative risk, but for common ones they exaggerate it; see [[Observational Study]].
- **Uncertainty.** A relative risk of 1.3 with a confidence interval running from 0.9 to 1.8 is compatible with no effect at all; see [[Confidence Interval]].
- **Small ratios from observational data** are fragile, since a modest confounder could produce them; see [[Confounding]] and [[Bradford Hill Criteria]].

Good practice is to report absolute risks in each group first, and the relative change second. Expressing risks as natural frequencies, such as "2 in 7,000," makes them easier for most people to reason about than percentages or ratios; see [[Bayes' Theorem]] and [[Sensitivity and Specificity]].

The general principle is that a ratio needs its denominator. A size of effect is only meaningful against what it is a change from; see [[Effect Size]] and [[Expected Value]].
