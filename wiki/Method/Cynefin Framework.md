**The Cynefin framework** sorts situations by the relationship between cause and effect, and suggests a different way of acting in each. It was developed by the management consultant Dave Snowden, beginning at IBM around 1999, and became widely known through a 2007 Harvard Business Review article written with Mary Boone. The name is a Welsh word, usually translated as habitat or the place of one's many belongings.

The framework has five domains:

- **Clear** (earlier called simple or obvious). Cause and effect are plain to anyone. The approach is to sense, categorize, and respond: recognize the situation and apply the known procedure; see [[Checklist]].
- **Complicated.** Cause and effect exist but take expertise to work out, as in engineering or diagnosing a car. The approach is to sense, analyze, and respond, and there may be several good answers; see [[Expert Intuition]].
- **Complex.** Cause and effect are visible only in hindsight, because many agents interact and the system changes in response to interventions, as in markets, cultures, and many software organizations. The approach is to probe, sense, and respond: run safe-to-fail experiments, amplify what works, and dampen what doesn't; see [[Wicked Problem]] and [[Fail Fast]].
- **Chaotic.** No discernible relationship between cause and effect, as in a crisis. The approach is to act first to establish stability, then sense and respond; see [[Incident Response]].
- **Confusion** (earlier called disorder), at the center, where it's unclear which domain applies and people default to their habitual approach.

The framework's main warning is against treating complex situations as complicated ones. Expert analysis and detailed planning assume a knowable answer; in a complex system, interventions have consequences no analysis would have predicted; see [[Second-Order Thinking]]. Copying another organization's best practice assumes its situation was clear; see [[Cargo Cult]]. Working systems in complex domains tend to grow from simpler working systems rather than being designed whole; see [[Gall's Law]].

Snowden also describes a cliff between clear and chaotic. Organizations that grow complacent, assuming everything is routine, can fall suddenly into crisis when conditions shift; see [[Normalization of Deviance]] and [[Normal Accidents]].

The framework is meant for sense-making, deciding together what kind of situation a group is in, rather than for filing problems into boxes. Different parts of one problem can sit in different domains, and situations move between them. People also tend to see problems through the methods they already know; see [[Law of the Instrument]].

Critics say the domains are hard to identify in practice and that the framework mostly renames older ideas from complexity science. Supporters answer that its value lies in prompting the question of which kind of problem a group faces before choosing how to solve it; see [[Map and Territory]].
