**Scenario planning** is a way of preparing for an uncertain future by describing several different ways it could unfold and testing plans against each. It doesn't try to predict which future will arrive. It tries to make an organization ready for more than one.

The method has military and corporate roots. Herman Kahn, a strategist at the RAND Corporation in the 1950s, wrote detailed stories about how nuclear war might begin and proceed, arguing that thinking through unthinkable outcomes was better than refusing to. In the early 1970s Pierre Wack and colleagues at Royal Dutch Shell adapted the approach for business. Shell's planners wrote scenarios in which oil supply was disrupted and prices rose sharply, and the company's own account credits that work with helping it respond faster than its rivals to the 1973 oil crisis.

A common recipe starts by listing the forces that will shape the question, such as technology, regulation, demographics, and prices. The forces that are both important and highly uncertain are separated from those that are predictable. Two of the most uncertain become the axes of a grid, giving four distinct futures, each written up as a story with a name and a plausible sequence of events. Plans are then checked against each one, sometimes called wind-tunneling, to find strategies that hold up in all four and early signs that would show which world is arriving; see [[Sensitivity Analysis]].

Scenarios are not forecasts with error bars. They are meant to be plausible and different from one another, not ranked by likelihood. That's their strength, since they make people take seriously possibilities that a single best guess would dismiss, and their weakness, since they say little about which future to weight most heavily; see [[Superforecasting]] and [[Cone of Uncertainty]].

The value is often in the conversation. Writing scenarios forces planners to state assumptions that otherwise stay hidden, and a story is easier to argue with than a spreadsheet. A team that has imagined a disruption in detail is less likely to dismiss its first signs; see [[Second-Order Thinking]] and [[Groupthink]]. The pre-mortem uses a similar trick on a smaller scale, assuming a plan has failed and asking why; see [[Pre-mortem]].

Scenario planning fits problems that are complex and slow-moving, where expert forecasting has a poor record; see [[Wicked Problem]] and [[Cynefin Framework]]. It is weak against shocks no one thought to imagine, and scenarios chosen by insiders tend to stay within what the organization finds comfortable; see [[Black Swan]]. Structured expert panels and quantitative simulations are often used alongside it; see [[Delphi Method]] and [[Monte Carlo Method]]. Military planners use a related technique, playing out conflicts with opposing teams; see [[Wargame]].
