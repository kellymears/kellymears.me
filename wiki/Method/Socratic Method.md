The **Socratic method** is a way of examining ideas through structured questioning rather than lecturing. Its model is the conduct of Socrates, the Athenian philosopher of the fifth century BCE, as portrayed in the dialogues of Plato. Socrates left no writings, so what is known of the method comes mostly from Plato's portrayal, which mixes history with literary invention.

The core move is the **elenchus**, Greek for refutation or cross-examination. Someone confidently states a definition, such as what courage or justice is. Socrates asks a series of questions the speaker readily agrees to, then shows that those agreed answers contradict the original claim. In one dialogue, a general defines courage as standing firm in battle. Questioning brings out that a tactical retreat can also be courageous, and the definition collapses. A revised definition follows, and the cycle repeats. Many early dialogues end in **aporia**, a state of puzzlement in which every proposed answer has failed and the participants admit they don't know.

That ending was the point. The Socrates of the dialogues claims to be wise only in recognizing the limits of what anyone knows, and the method targets the gap between feeling that one understands something and being able to explain it; see [[Illusion of explanatory depth]] and [[Metacognition]]. It had costs in its own time. Publicly embarrassing prominent Athenians likely contributed to the trial in 399 BCE that ended in a death sentence.

Modern uses soften the humiliation. American law schools adopted a version in the late nineteenth century, with professors cold-calling students to defend their reading of a case against changing facts. In teaching, guiding a learner to work out an answer, rather than stating it, tends to produce more durable learning, partly because the effort of producing an answer strengthens memory; see [[Desirable Difficulty]] and [[Retrieval practice]]. Questions pitched just beyond what a student can manage alone fit the [[Zone of Proximal Development]]. Explaining also helps experts get past the [[Curse of Knowledge]]. Therapists use **Socratic questioning** to help clients test the evidence for anxious or self-critical beliefs; see [[Cognitive Behavioral Therapy]].

Programmers rediscover a solitary version when explaining code line by line to an inanimate listener exposes the bug; see [[Rubber Duck Debugging]].

The method has limits. Questions can be leading, steering someone toward a conclusion the questioner already holds while appearing to let them discover it, and critics say some of Plato's dialogues do exactly that. Used combatively, it becomes a way to win arguments rather than understand them, which is why practices that strengthen the opposing view before challenging it are a useful counterweight; see [[Steelmanning]] and [[Devil's Advocate]]. AI tutors face the reverse problem, tending to agree with a learner when they should be questioning the learner; see [[Sycophancy]].
