**Brandolini's law**, also called the *bullshit asymmetry principle*, states that the energy needed to refute nonsense is an order of magnitude larger than the energy needed to produce it. The Italian programmer Alberto Brandolini posted it in 2013, after reading a book by Daniel Kahneman and watching a political talk show at the same time.

The asymmetry has several sources:

- **Claims are cheap; evidence is expensive.** A false statistic takes seconds to invent. Showing it's false means finding the real data, explaining methods, and citing sources.
- **Proving a negative is hard.** "There's no evidence for X" is weaker-sounding than "X is true," even when it's the stronger claim.
- **Refutations need context.** A false claim fits in one sentence; the correction often needs paragraphs, and most people stop reading first.
- **Repetition builds belief.** Hearing a claim again makes it feel truer, even when it's being repeated in order to debunk it; see [[Illusory Truth Effect]].

A well-documented example: a 1998 paper by Andrew Wakefield claiming a link between the MMR vaccine and autism. It was retracted in 2010, and Wakefield lost his medical license, but rebutting it took dozens of large studies involving millions of children, and the claim still circulates.

A debating tactic exploits the asymmetry. The *Gish gallop*, named after creationist Duane Gish, buries an opponent in many weak claims at once. Each takes moments to say and minutes to answer, so the opponent can't address them all, and any left unanswered look conceded.

What helps:

- **Pick battles.** Not every falsehood deserves a rebuttal. Correcting fringe claims can spread them; see [[Streisand Effect]].
- **Lead with the truth.** Researchers recommend stating the fact first, mentioning the myth once, explaining why it's wrong, then restating the fact.
- **Prebunking.** Teaching people common manipulation techniques before they meet them works better than correcting specific claims after.
- **Make claims cost something.** Requiring sources, evidence, or a [[Reproducible Case]] shifts effort back to whoever makes the claim. Scientific peer review and code review both do this in part.

The same asymmetry appears in technical work. A vague bug report, a flaky test, or an unverified claim about performance takes moments to file and hours to investigate; see [[Flaky Test]] and [[Benchmarking]]. Language models can produce confident, plausible text far faster than anyone can check it, which sharpens the problem; see [[Hallucination]].

Brandolini's law sits alongside other observations about online discourse, such as [[Poe's Law]] and [[Cunningham's Law]], and alongside the discipline of engaging with the strongest version of an argument instead of the weakest; see [[Steelmanning]].
