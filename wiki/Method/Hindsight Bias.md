**Hindsight bias** is the tendency to see events as more predictable after they've happened than they were beforehand. Once we know the outcome, we unconsciously rewrite our memory of what we expected, and the path to the result looks obvious. Baruch Fischhoff demonstrated it in the 1970s. People told the outcome of a historical event rated it far more likely than people who weren't told, and they believed they would have predicted it, even when they had recorded different predictions before.

It's sometimes called *creeping determinism*. Knowing the ending, we select the clues that pointed toward it and forget the ones that pointed elsewhere. The warning signs before an outage, a market crash, or a failed launch seem glaring afterward, because we no longer see the hundreds of similar signals that came to nothing. That selective memory is a cousin of [[Confirmation Bias]], and the vivid ending makes it easy to recall, which the [[Availability Heuristic]] then treats as likely.

A close relative is **outcome bias**: judging a decision by its result instead of by its quality given what was known at the time. A reckless bet that paid off looks wise, and a careful decision that hit bad luck looks foolish. Over time, organizations that reward outcomes teach people to take risks that happened to work, and punish good judgment that happened to fail.

The costs show up in how we learn:

- **Unfair blame.** After an incident, reviewers ask why someone didn't see what is now obvious. This is the core reason the [[Blameless Postmortem]] exists: it asks what the people involved knew and saw at each moment, not what the reviewer knows now.
- **False lessons.** If the outcome seemed predictable, the story becomes "they should have known," and the real systemic causes go unexamined, cutting short [[Root Cause Analysis]].
- **Overconfidence.** Believing past events were predictable makes us think future ones are too, feeding the [[Planning Fallacy]].
- **Survivor stories.** Success narratives read as inevitable, a pattern that combines with [[Survivorship Bias]].

Simply knowing about the bias barely reduces it. Better defenses are procedural:

- **Write predictions down** before outcomes are known, so they can't be quietly revised.
- **Reconstruct the timeline** from the actors' point of view, using only information available at each step.
- **Consider alternatives.** Asking how the outcome could have gone differently, and what would have explained that, restores the uncertainty that hindsight erased.
- **Judge process separately from outcome**, especially for decisions made under real uncertainty.

A [[Pre-mortem]] turns the effect into a tool, borrowing the clarity of hindsight before the outcome instead of after. The bias also illustrates why [[Falsifiability]] matters: an explanation built after the fact can always be made to fit.
