A **wicked problem** is one that can't be cleanly defined, has no test for when it is solved, and has no right answer, only better or worse ones. The term comes from a 1973 paper by the design theorist Horst Rittel and the urban planner Melvin Webber, who argued that the methods of science and engineering, so successful on what they called **tame problems**, fail on the problems of social policy. "Wicked" meant resistant, not evil.

A tame problem, such as a chess puzzle, solving an equation, or building a bridge to a given load, can be stated completely, and anyone can check whether a proposed solution works. Rittel and Webber listed ten properties of wicked ones, including:

- **No definitive formulation.** Understanding the problem and solving it are the same activity, since how you describe it determines what solutions you consider.
- **No stopping rule.** You stop when time, money, or patience runs out, not when it is done.
- **Better or worse, not right or wrong.** Different people, with different interests, judge solutions differently.
- **No immediate or final test.** Consequences spread over years and in unexpected directions; see [[Second-Order Thinking]].
- **Every attempt counts.** You can't experiment without affecting people, so there is little room for trial and error.
- **Every problem is a symptom of another.** Fix crime and you face poverty, schools, and housing.
- **Each is essentially unique**, so lessons transfer poorly.

Their examples were from planning: freeway routes, school curricula, crime and poverty. Later writers applied the idea to climate policy, public health, and shared resources; see [[Tragedy of the Commons]]. Interventions in such systems often backfire through feedback no one anticipated; see [[Feedback Loop]] and [[Cobra Effect]].

Product and software design have wicked features too. What users need changes as they see what's built, requirements can't be fully fixed up front, and the problem statement keeps shifting as understanding grows; see [[Jobs to Be Done]] and [[Cone of Uncertainty]]. Rittel's work influenced design methods that treat problem framing as part of the work rather than its input; see [[Design Thinking]].

Since wicked problems can't be solved by analysis alone, the approaches that help are about process:

1. **Iterate.** Make small changes and watch the results; see [[Minimum Viable Product]] and [[Gall's Law]].
2. **Involve stakeholders**, since judgment of what counts as better is part of the problem.
3. **Aim for good enough** rather than optimal; see [[Satisficing]].
4. **Prefer reversible moves** where possible; see [[Reversible Decision]].
5. **Frame and reframe.** Ask whether the problem stated is the problem that matters; see [[XY Problem]].

The concept also warns against a common error: treating a wicked problem as tame, fixing a measurable proxy, and declaring victory; see [[Goodhart's Law]].
