**The PDCA cycle** is a four-step loop for improving a process by experiment:

1. **Plan** a change and predict what it will do.
2. **Do** it, preferably on a small scale.
3. **Check** the results against the prediction.
4. **Act** on what was learned: adopt the change, adjust it, or abandon it, and begin again.

The loop applies the scientific method to everyday work. Each change is a hypothesis, and each cycle a test that could prove it wrong; see [[Falsifiability]].

Its roots lie with Walter Shewhart, a physicist and statistician at Bell Telephone Laboratories, who described manufacturing in 1939 as a cycle of specification, production, and inspection rather than a straight line. W. Edwards Deming, an American statistician, presented a version of the cycle in lectures to Japanese engineers and executives in 1950. Japanese managers developed it into plan-do-check-act, and it became part of the quality movement that made Japanese manufacturing famous; see [[Toyota Production System]].

Deming later preferred "study" to "check," naming the loop PDSA. "Check" suggested inspecting whether the plan was followed; "study" stressed learning why the result came out as it did, especially when it differed from the prediction.

The cycle is a vehicle for continuous improvement, called kaizen in Japanese: many small changes made by the people closest to the work, rather than rare large changes imposed from above. Several practices fit inside it:

- **Measuring the process over time** separates real effects of a change from routine variation; see [[Control Chart]].
- **Finding causes** of problems before planning fixes; see [[Root Cause Analysis]] and [[Five Whys]].
- **Stopping work** to fix a problem the moment it appears; see [[Andon Cord]].
- **Making work visible** so bottlenecks show up; see [[Kanban]].

The loop's lessons are about pace and scale. Small changes are cheap to undo and easy to attribute; a large change makes it hard to know which part worked. Prediction matters too: without one, every result looks like success after the fact; see [[Hindsight Bias]].

The same shape recurs widely. Agile software teams plan an iteration, build, review, and adjust in a retrospective; see [[Agile Software Development]]. Product teams ship a small version and learn from users; see [[Minimum Viable Product]]. Controlled comparisons of two versions are a sharper form of the check step; see [[AB Testing]]. Military strategists describe a similar cycle with an emphasis on speed against an opponent; see [[OODA Loop]].

The common failure is skipping steps: planning and doing without checking, or checking without acting on what was found, so the loop never closes and the same problems return; see [[Feedback Loop]].
