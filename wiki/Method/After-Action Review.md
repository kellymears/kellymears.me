An **after-action review**, or AAR, is a short, structured discussion held right after an event to work out what happened and what to do differently. The US Army developed the practice in the 1970s and made it central to training at its National Training Center in the Mojave Desert, which opened in 1981. Units fought realistic mock battles against a dedicated opposing force, with laser engagement systems recording hits, and then sat down to review the result. Short reviews held immediately after an exercise are sometimes called a **hot wash**.

The review is built around four questions. What was supposed to happen? What actually happened? Why was there a difference? What should be kept, and what should change? Starting with the plan matters, because many failures turn out to be disagreements about what the plan was. The gap between intent and outcome is the subject, not the performance of any one person; see [[Map and Territory]].

The Army's version had rules that made it unusual in a hierarchy. Rank was set aside during the discussion. Junior soldiers were expected to describe what they saw, including mistakes by their officers, and officers were expected to admit their own. Facilitators asked questions instead of delivering verdicts. Without that, people protect themselves and the review turns into a performance; see [[Psychological Safety]] and [[Just Culture]].

Timing is part of the method. Memory reshapes events quickly, and once an outcome is known, the earlier choices start to look obviously right or wrong; see [[Hindsight Bias]]. Holding the review within hours, not weeks, with the people who were there and with whatever record exists, keeps the account closer to what was known at each moment.

An AAR is lighter than a full investigation. It doesn't try to establish a single root cause, and it is usually run by the team itself rather than by outsiders; see [[Root Cause Analysis]]. Its output is a few lessons and changes, fed into the next rehearsal, in the same plan-do-check-act rhythm that quality programs use; see [[PDCA Cycle]] and [[Feedback Loop]].

The practice spread to firefighting, medicine, and business. Software teams adopted closely related forms: the blameless postmortem after an incident, and the regular retrospective at the end of a work cycle; see [[Blameless Postmortem]] and [[Retrospective]]. Its prospective twin imagines the failure before the event; see [[Pre-mortem]].

Reviews fail in familiar ways. Lessons get written down and never acted on, collected in a database nobody reads. Reviews held only after failures teach people that a review means trouble, while near misses and successes go unexamined; see [[Normalization of Deviance]].
