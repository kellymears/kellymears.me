The **Bradford Hill criteria** are nine considerations for judging whether an observed association, such as between an exposure and a disease, is likely to be causal. The British epidemiologist and statistician Austin Bradford Hill set them out in a 1965 lecture titled "The Environment and Disease: Association or Causation?" They remain a standard starting point wherever causes must be inferred without a controlled experiment; see [[Correlation and Causation]].

Hill knew the problem firsthand. With Richard Doll he had produced some of the key evidence that smoking causes lung cancer, first comparing patients with and without the disease and then following tens of thousands of doctors; see [[Observational Study]]. Smoking couldn't be randomly assigned, and prominent critics, including the statistician Ronald Fisher, argued that a hidden genetic factor might cause both the urge to smoke and the cancer; see [[Confounding]]. Hill's list summarizes how such doubts are weighed.

His nine viewpoints:

1. **Strength.** Large associations are harder to explain away; heavy smokers had many times the lung cancer rate of nonsmokers; see [[Relative Risk]].
2. **Consistency.** The association appears across different places, people, and study designs; see [[Meta-Analysis]].
3. **Specificity.** The exposure is tied to a particular disease, the weakest consideration, since smoking causes many diseases.
4. **Temporality.** The cause comes before the effect, the only one Hill treated as essential.
5. **Biological gradient.** More exposure brings more effect, a **dose-response** relationship.
6. **Plausibility.** A believable mechanism exists, judged against current knowledge; see [[Plausible Mechanism]].
7. **Coherence.** The causal reading doesn't conflict with what is known of the disease.
8. **Experiment.** Removing the exposure reduces the effect, as when quitting smoking lowers risk; see [[Randomized Controlled Trial]].
9. **Analogy.** Similar causes are known to produce similar effects.

Hill insisted they weren't a checklist. None except temporality is required, and meeting them all doesn't prove causation. He also warned against leaning on tests of statistical significance in place of judgment about the size and consistency of an effect; see [[Statistical Significance]] and [[Effect Size]].

Several points have aged. Dose-response relations aren't always steady increases; some substances help at low doses and harm at high ones; see [[Hormesis]]. Plausibility is limited by current biology, which once lacked a mechanism for many real causes. And a strong association can still be confounded.

Modern methods add tools Hill lacked, including causal diagrams that make assumptions explicit and designs that exploit natural randomness, such as genetic variation; see [[Natural Experiment]]. The criteria still frame the argument in courts, regulation, and public health debates, from asbestos to air pollution.

The broader lesson is that causal claims from observation rest on a convergence of evidence, weighed together, rather than a single decisive test; see [[Abductive Reasoning]].
