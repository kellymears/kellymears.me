**Antifragility** is the property of gaining from stress, volatility, and disorder. Nassim Nicholas Taleb coined the term in *Antifragile* (2012) because no existing word named the opposite of fragile. A fragile thing, like a wine glass, is harmed by shocks. A robust thing, like a rock, is indifferent to them. An antifragile thing improves because of them, at least up to a point.

Biology supplies most of the examples. Muscles and bones grow stronger under load they can recover from; see [[Supercompensation]] and [[Stress Fracture]]. Small doses of some toxins and stresses trigger repair responses that leave an organism better off; see [[Hormesis]]. The immune system learns from exposure, which is the logic behind vaccination; see [[Immune System]]. In each case the benefit depends on the dose. The same stressor that strengthens in moderation destroys in excess.

Taleb extended the idea to systems. Restaurants fail constantly, and the restaurant trade improves because each failure teaches the rest. Individual fragility can make the larger system antifragile, provided failures stay small and separate. Removing small shocks can make a system more fragile, as when suppressing every minor forest fire lets fuel build up toward a catastrophic one. Software engineers apply a version of this by injecting failures on purpose; see [[Chaos Engineering]] and [[Fail Fast]].

The mathematical core is about the shape of outcomes. Something is antifragile when its gains from variation exceed its losses, so that more volatility raises its average result. Options work this way: a small fixed cost buys exposure to a large potential gain; see [[Expected Value]]. The **barbell strategy** applies the same logic to a portfolio, putting most resources somewhere extremely safe and a small share into highly speculative bets, avoiding the middle where losses can be large and gains modest; see [[Black Swan]].

Two further principles follow. The first is caution about intervention. Treatments with small visible benefits and hidden or delayed harms, especially when applied to people who are mostly well, can do net damage; see [[Second-Order Thinking]] and [[Chesterton's Fence]]. The second, **via negativa**, says that removing harmful things is often more reliable than adding helpful ones; see [[Inversion]]. Taleb also argued that things which have lasted a long time are likely to be robust, since time has already tested them; see [[Lindy Effect]].

The idea has critics. Antifragility is hard to measure, and nearly anything can be described as gaining from some stress in hindsight. Taleb's presentation is polemical, and the line between strengthening stress and harmful stress is rarely known in advance. Building in slack and redundancy is the more conservative version of the same advice; see [[Margin of Safety]].
