**Poe's law** holds that, absent a clear marker of intent, an extreme or absurd statement made in earnest cannot reliably be told apart from a parody of the same view. The name comes from Nathan Poe, who stated it in 2005 on a Christian forum discussing creationism: without a winking emoticon or other explicit tell, sufficiently sincere fundamentalism and sufficiently committed mockery of fundamentalism produce the same sentence.

The claim is not that people are gullible; it is that the text itself, stripped of context, underdetermines the answer. Two readers with different priors about the author will resolve the same passage in opposite directions, and both readings are defensible from the words alone. In that sense the law names a genuine limit rather than a failure of attention — there is no reading strategy that recovers intent the text does not encode, which is a close cousin of [[Falsifiability]]: a claim about authorial sincerity that no feature of the artifact can settle is not one the artifact can be used to test.

The law grew more relevant as online audiences lost shared context — the same post reaches strangers, bots, and search engines with no access to the author's history or tone of voice, a collapse that also drives the spread of coordinated, source-obscured messaging such as [[Astroturfing]]. It has taken on a further life as a way of talking about synthetic content: text or images generated to imitate sincere human expression face exactly the same test, and the same absence of a reliable internal marker, that made the original joke work. A statement extreme enough to read as parody to one reader is, to another, simply a statement, and no amount of rereading settles which — the same underdetermination that lets a [[Confidence Trick]] wear the clothes of a genuine offer. Where a marker does survive, it is worth reading: [[Betteridge's Law of Headlines]] turns on one, a question mark in a headline signaling that the publication is declining to assert what the story implies.
