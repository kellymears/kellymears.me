**The Delphi method** gathers judgments from a panel of experts through several rounds of anonymous questionnaires. After each round, a facilitator summarizes the answers, often as the median and the range, and sends the summary back. Experts revise their answers in light of the group's, and explain outlying views. After two to four rounds, answers usually converge, and the final summary reports both the consensus and any lasting disagreement.

It was developed at the RAND Corporation, an American research institute, in the 1950s, by researchers including Olaf Helmer and Norman Dalkey. Early studies asked experts to forecast military and technological questions during the Cold War, where data was scarce and expert opinion was all there was. The name refers to the oracle at Delphi in ancient Greece, a joke the developers reportedly weren't fond of.

The method's design targets the ways committees go wrong:

- **Anonymity** keeps rank and reputation from swaying others; see [[Halo Effect]].
- **Written, separate answers** prevent the loudest or first speaker from setting the frame; see [[Anchoring Effect]].
- **Controlled feedback** shares the group's views without the pressure of a room; see [[Conformity]] and [[Spiral of Silence]].
- **Iteration** gives people a chance to change their minds without losing face.

Together these reduce the drift toward premature agreement that afflicts meetings; see [[Groupthink]].

Delphi studies became common in health care, where panels use them to agree on clinical guidelines and definitions, and in policy and technology forecasting. Software estimation borrowed the idea. Barry Boehm described a "wideband" variant in the 1970s, with discussion between anonymous rounds, and planning poker, in which a team reveals estimates simultaneously, descends from it; see [[Story Points]].

The method has critics:

- **Consensus isn't accuracy.** A panel can converge on the wrong answer, and convergence may reflect people yielding to the group median rather than learning from it; see [[Information Cascade]].
- **Experts forecast poorly.** Research on prediction has found that many experts do little better than simple rules, and that how they think matters more than what they know; see [[Superforecasting]].
- **Design choices matter.** Who is on the panel, how questions are worded, and when to stop all shape the outcome, and are often poorly reported.
- **Dropouts.** Panelists who disagree may stop responding, producing agreement by attrition.

Alternatives aggregate judgment differently. Averaging many independent guesses works when errors are uncorrelated, and markets let people bet on outcomes; see [[Wisdom of the Crowd]]. Delphi remains useful where questions have no market, the experts are few, and the goal is a considered collective judgment rather than a single number.
