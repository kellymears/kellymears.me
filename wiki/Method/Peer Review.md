**Peer review** is the practice of having other experts evaluate research before it's published. A journal editor sends a submitted paper to two or three specialists, usually anonymous to the authors, who judge whether its methods are sound and its conclusions supported. The editor then accepts it, rejects it, or asks for revisions.

Its roots go back to the Royal Society of London, whose journal began in 1665 and whose council sometimes consulted members about papers. But systematic external review became standard surprisingly late. Many journals relied on editors' judgment well into the twentieth century. Albert Einstein reportedly withdrew a 1936 paper from the journal Physical Review on learning it had been sent to an anonymous referee, a practice then unfamiliar. Nature made outside review routine only in the 1970s. Postwar growth in research funding and submissions pushed journals to share the work of judging.

Peer review does some things well. Reviewers catch errors in reasoning, demand missing controls, and push authors to temper claims. It's also part of how a field decides what counts as established; see [[Burden of Proof]].

It also has well-documented limits:

- **Reviewers miss errors.** In studies where editors planted mistakes in manuscripts, reviewers typically caught only a minority.
- **It rarely catches fraud.** Reviewers see the paper, not the raw data or the lab notebooks.
- **It favors the familiar.** Prestige and reputation can sway judgments, and novel work that challenges a field may struggle; see [[Halo Effect]] and [[Matthew Effect]].
- **It shapes what's published.** Journals prefer positive, striking results, which distorts the literature; see [[Publication Bias]] and [[P-Hacking]].
- **It's slow and unpaid.** Reviews can take months, and the work is done as unpaid service.

The failures of peer-reviewed findings to hold up in repeat studies showed that passing review doesn't establish that a result is true; see [[Replication Crisis]]. Some journals now review a study's design before results exist, so the decision can't depend on what was found.

**Preprints** offer another route. Physicists began sharing manuscripts on the arXiv server in 1991, and biology and medicine followed with their own servers in the 2010s. During the COVID-19 pandemic, preprints spread findings in days rather than months, along with some that didn't survive scrutiny. Open review, where reports are published alongside the paper, and comments after publication extend review beyond a few anonymous gatekeepers.

Software teams use a similar check, with the same tension between catching problems and slowing work; see [[Code Review]]. A reviewer who engages with the strongest form of an argument, rather than its weakest, gives the most useful feedback; see [[Steelmanning]].
