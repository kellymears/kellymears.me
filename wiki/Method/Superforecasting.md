**Superforecasting** refers to the unusually accurate predictions made by a small group of forecasters identified in research by the psychologist Philip Tetlock, and to the habits that make them good. Its central claim is that forecasting is a skill that can be measured and improved, not a gift of expertise or a matter of luck.

The work began with a discouraging result. In a project spanning two decades and published in 2005, Tetlock collected tens of thousands of predictions from hundreds of experts on politics and economics. On average they did little better than chance or simple rules such as "nothing changes." He found one pattern, borrowing a distinction from Isaiah Berlin: **hedgehogs**, who organized everything around one big idea, did worse than **foxes**, who drew on many ideas and were comfortable with uncertainty. The most famous experts tended to be hedgehogs.

In 2011 a US intelligence research agency ran a forecasting tournament on real geopolitical questions, such as whether a given leader would leave office within a year. Tetlock and Barbara Mellers's team, the **Good Judgment Project**, recruited thousands of volunteers and beat the competing research teams by wide margins over four years. The top 2 percent, the superforecasters, stayed at the top year after year, which showed skill rather than luck.

Accuracy was scored with the Brier score, which penalizes the squared gap between stated probabilities and outcomes, so it rewards being both right and appropriately confident; see [[Calibration]].

The habits that distinguished them:

1. **Start with the base rate.** Before considering details of a case, ask how often things like it happen; see [[Reference class forecasting]] and [[Base Rate Fallacy]].
2. **Break the question down** into parts that can be estimated separately; see [[Fermi Estimation]].
3. **Use precise probabilities.** Distinguishing 60 from 65 percent turned out to carry real information.
4. **Update often, in small steps**, as news arrives, rather than holding firm or lurching; see [[Bayes' Theorem]].
5. **Seek out contrary evidence** and treat beliefs as hypotheses to test; see [[Confirmation Bias]].
6. **Review results honestly** without rewriting what you predicted; see [[Hindsight Bias]].

Teams outperformed individuals, and combining many forecasts, then pushing the average toward the extremes, did better still; see [[Wisdom of crowds]]. Prediction markets apply the same idea with money at stake. A short training module on these habits improved accuracy measurably.

There are limits. The tournaments asked well-defined questions resolving within months to a year, while the events that matter most may be rare, unprecedented, and impossible to assign a probability; see [[Black Swan]]. Forecasts also require a question precise enough to be judged; vague predictions can never be wrong, which is exactly why they're popular; see [[Falsifiability]].

Uncertainty also widens with distance; see [[Cone of Uncertainty]].
