**Cost-benefit analysis** is the practice of listing the expected costs and benefits of a decision, converting them to a common unit, usually money, and comparing the totals. A project whose benefits exceed its costs passes; among alternatives, the one with the largest net benefit wins. It's the standard way governments evaluate roads, dams, and regulations.

The idea goes back to the French engineer Jules Dupuit, who in 1844 asked how to measure the public value of a bridge when users would pay different amounts to cross it. In the United States, the Flood Control Act of 1936 allowed federal flood projects only where benefits exceeded costs. Since 1981, executive orders have required federal agencies to analyze the costs and benefits of major regulations.

The hardest step is putting prices on things that aren't sold. Agencies value reductions in risk of death using the **value of a statistical life**, derived from how much people accept in wages for riskier jobs or pay for safety. US agencies have used figures in the region of ten million dollars. It's not a price for a person; it's a way of saying how much society is willing to spend to reduce a small risk spread across many people.

Time is the second difficulty. Money available now can be invested, so future costs and benefits are discounted, shrunk by a set percentage per year; see [[Opportunity Cost]]. The choice of rate dominates long-lived decisions. At five percent, a harm a century away counts for less than a hundredth of the same harm today. Debates over climate policy turn substantially on that number, and ordinary intuitions about delay are inconsistent in ways that complicate any choice; see [[Hyperbolic Discounting]].

Supporters argue that the method makes trade-offs explicit. Every regulation spends resources that could save lives elsewhere, and without a common measure, decisions follow whichever harm is most vivid; see [[Expected Value]] and [[Availability Heuristic]]. It also exposes costs pushed onto others; see [[Externality]].

Critics raise several objections. Anything hard to quantify, such as beauty, dignity, or the existence of a species, tends to be valued at zero or left out; see [[McNamara Fallacy]]. Willingness to pay depends on ability to pay, so harms to poorer people can count for less. Results depend on contested inputs, and an analysis can be steered toward a desired answer; see [[Regulatory Capture]] and [[Goodhart's Law]]. Where harms are catastrophic and irreversible, some argue that expected-value arithmetic should give way to caution; see [[Precautionary Principle]].

Careful analyses report how the conclusion changes as uncertain inputs vary, instead of a single figure; see [[Sensitivity Analysis]].
