**Opportunity cost** is the value of the best alternative given up when choosing one option. If an afternoon goes to fixing a minor bug, its cost is not only the hours but whatever else those hours could have produced. The idea was formalized by Austrian economists in the late nineteenth century, building on Frédéric Bastiat's 1850 essay "What Is Seen and What Is Not Seen," which argued that bad decisions come from counting visible effects and ignoring the unseen alternatives.

The concept matters because it is easy to miss. Money spent shows up on a receipt, but the option not taken leaves no record. People routinely judge an option on its own merits, asking whether it is good, rather than against the alternatives, asking whether it is better than the next best use of the same time or money; see [[Base Rate Fallacy]].

Some classic consequences:

- **Free is not free.** A free event that takes a whole day costs that day. An open-source library that is free to download still costs the time to integrate, learn, and maintain it; see [[Not Invented Here]].
- **Time is the binding constraint.** Every feature built is another not built, and every meeting attended is focused work not done; see [[Feature Creep]] and [[Attention Residue]].
- **Sunk costs are irrelevant.** Money already spent can't be recovered whatever is chosen next, so only future costs and benefits, including what else the resources could do, should matter; see [[Sunk Cost Fallacy]].
- **Specialization.** Countries and people benefit from doing what they give up least to do, which is the basis of comparative advantage in trade.

In engineering, opportunity cost reframes several common debates. Polishing code that rarely runs has a cost in work elsewhere; see [[Premature Optimization]]. Paying down [[Technical Debt]] competes with features, and the right answer depends on which yields more over the relevant time span. Arguing at length over a trivial decision costs the time the argument takes; see [[Law of Triviality]]. Choosing one architecture closes off others; see [[Path Dependence]].

It also applies to attention and choice. Every option considered and rejected has a cost in effort, which is part of why a long menu tires people; see [[Decision Fatigue]]. Sometimes the best use of time is accepting a good-enough choice rather than searching for the best one; see [[Satisficing]].

Opportunity cost is hard to estimate precisely, since the alternatives are hypothetical and the value of each is uncertain. But the habit of asking "compared to what?" catches many poor decisions, and it is the core of prioritization frameworks that rank work by value against cost; see [[Minimum Viable Product]] and [[Yak Shaving]].
