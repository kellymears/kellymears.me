A **saddle sore** is skin damage in the area where a cyclist contacts the saddle. The term covers a range of problems: chafed, raw skin; infected hair follicles that become tender red bumps; deeper boils and abscesses; and, in long events, open sores. They're among the most common complaints in cycling, and professional riders have abandoned major stage races because of them.

Four factors combine. **Pressure** from sitting compresses skin and soft tissue, reducing blood flow; the weight is meant to rest on the sit bones of the pelvis, but a poorly matched saddle shifts it onto softer tissue. **Friction** from thousands of pedal strokes rubs the skin; see [[Friction]]. **Moisture** from sweat softens the skin and makes it easier to damage; see [[Sweat]]. And **bacteria** that normally live on the skin get into hair follicles and small breaks, causing infection.

Pressure in the same area can also compress nerves and blood vessels, causing numbness in the groin. That's a separate problem from sores but shares causes and fixes; see [[Paresthesia]]. Hands are the other main pressure point on a bike, with their own nerve problems; see [[Ulnar Neuropathy]].

Most prevention comes down to fit and kit. A saddle that is too high makes the hips rock side to side, increasing rubbing; one that is too low or tilted wrongly shifts weight forward. Saddle width should match the distance between the sit bones; see [[Bike Fit]]. Cycling shorts have a padded insert, historically called a chamois after the leather once used, and are designed to be worn without underwear, which adds seams that rub. Riders apply **chamois cream**, a lubricating and sometimes antibacterial cream, to reduce friction. Standing on the pedals periodically restores blood flow.

Hygiene matters as much as equipment. Getting out of damp shorts promptly after a ride, washing them after every use, and keeping the area clean and dry reduce infection. Shaving can irritate follicles and may make problems worse for some riders.

Small sores usually heal with rest from the bike; continuing to ride on them keeps reopening the damage. Keeping a broken area clean and covered helps it close; see [[Moist Wound Healing]]. Larger abscesses can need drainage by a doctor and sometimes antibiotics, and recurrent sores in the same spot can leave firm scar tissue that becomes a new pressure point; see [[Scar Tissue]] and [[Inflammation]].

Saddle sores follow the same pattern as other overuse injuries: tissue fails when load exceeds what it can tolerate and recover from. Sudden increases in riding time, such as a first long tour or multi-day event, are a common trigger; see [[Training Load]] and [[Repetitive Strain Injury]]. Changing position on the bike, including riding posture over long distances, spreads the load; see [[Posture]].
