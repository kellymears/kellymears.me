**Rhabdomyolysis** is the rapid breakdown of skeletal muscle, which spills the contents of muscle cells into the bloodstream. The most important of these are myoglobin, the oxygen-binding protein that gives muscle its red color, which can clog and damage the kidneys, and potassium, which in high amounts disrupts the heart's rhythm. The enzyme creatine kinase, released at the same time, is the standard blood marker.

Causes:

- **Crush injuries.** The condition was recognized in its modern form in 1941, when the physician Eric Bywaters described people pulled from bombed buildings in the London Blitz who seemed fine at first, then died of kidney failure days later. Crush syndrome remains a major cause of death after earthquakes. Prolonged pressure on muscles can also cause it, as when someone lies immobile after a fall; see [[Compartment Syndrome]].
- **Extreme exercise.** Exertional rhabdomyolysis follows workouts far beyond what a person is used to, especially with many eccentric contractions, where muscles lengthen under load, such as downhill running or lowering weights. First-time spin classes and high-intensity group workouts have produced well-documented cases; see [[Delayed Onset Muscle Soreness]].
- **Heat.** Hard exercise in heat raises the risk, and heat stroke often causes it; see [[Heat Stroke]].
- **Drugs and toxins.** Alcohol, some stimulants, and cholesterol-lowering statins (rarely, especially combined with interacting drugs) can contribute; see [[Xenobiotic Metabolism]].
- **Genetic conditions** affecting muscle metabolism, which may show up as recurrent episodes.

Symptoms are muscle pain far worse than ordinary soreness, swelling, weakness, and urine the color of cola or tea. The classic triad appears in only some patients, and creatine kinase can rise to hundreds of times normal. Ordinary hard training also raises creatine kinase somewhat, which makes the line between normal adaptation and injury blurry; see [[Inflammation]].

Treatment centers on large amounts of intravenous fluid to flush myoglobin through the kidneys before it causes damage, along with monitoring potassium; see [[Fluid Balance]]. Most people with the exertional form recover fully, but severe cases need dialysis.

Prevention for athletes:

- **Progress gradually,** especially when starting a new type of workout or returning after a break, since fitness for one activity doesn't protect muscles unused to another; see [[Training Load]] and [[Detraining]].
- **Be cautious with eccentric-heavy work**, which damages muscle more than other contractions.
- **Avoid peer pressure** to finish a punishing session. Military and team sports have seen clusters of cases from conditioning sessions.
- **Respect heat and hydration**; see [[Thermoregulation]] and [[Heat Acclimation]].

Repeated muscle damage at manageable doses is how muscles adapt, through repair that makes them more resistant to the same stress; see [[Muscle Hypertrophy]] and [[Strength Training]].
