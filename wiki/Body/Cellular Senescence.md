**Cellular senescence** is a state in which a cell permanently stops dividing but doesn't die. A senescent cell stays metabolically active, often for a long time, and changes what it secretes into its surroundings. Senescence is a safeguard against cancer and a tool in healing, but senescent cells accumulate with age and contribute to many of the body's age-related problems.

The phenomenon was discovered by accident. For decades, biologists believed that cells grown in culture could divide forever, a belief resting partly on famous experiments claiming to have kept chicken heart cells alive for many years. In 1961, Leonard Hayflick and Paul Moorhead reported that normal human cells in culture divide a limited number of times, roughly forty to sixty, and then stop. This ceiling became known as the **Hayflick limit**.

Part of the explanation lies at the ends of chromosomes. **Telomeres** are repetitive sequences that cap each chromosome, like the plastic tips on shoelaces. Because of how DNA is copied, telomeres shorten slightly with each cell division, and when they become too short, the cell reads this as damage and stops dividing. An enzyme called telomerase can rebuild telomeres; its discovery in the 1980s led to a Nobel Prize in 2009. Most adult cells make little of it, while many cancer cells reactivate it to keep dividing.

Short telomeres aren't the only trigger. DNA damage, oxidative stress, ultraviolet light, and activated cancer-causing genes can all push cells into senescence; see [[Mitochondria]] and [[Sunburn]]. This is the protective role: a cell with damaged DNA that stops dividing can't become a tumor. Senescent cells also help coordinate wound healing, signaling for repair before being cleared away; see [[Scar Tissue]].

The trouble comes from what they secrete. Senescent cells release inflammatory signals and enzymes that degrade surrounding tissue, and the immune system clears them less efficiently with age; see [[Immune System]]. The resulting low-grade, chronic inflammation has been linked to joint degeneration, bone loss, and muscle loss; see [[Inflammation]], [[Osteoarthritis]], [[Osteoporosis]], and [[Sarcopenia]].

In 2011, researchers showed that genetically removing senescent cells from aging mice delayed several age-related disorders. That result launched the search for **senolytic** drugs that selectively kill senescent cells. Early human trials are under way, but whether the mouse results translate into longer or healthier human lives remains unproven.

The idea of accumulated damage that degrades a system over time has close parallels in engineering. Long-running software can slow down from leaked resources, and restarting it is a known remedy; see [[Software Aging]]. Failure rates rising late in a component's life follow a familiar curve; see [[Bathtub Curve]] and [[Material Fatigue]].
