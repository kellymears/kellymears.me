An **allergy** is an immune response to a substance that is harmless in itself, such as pollen, dust mites, pet dander, or a food protein. The [[Immune System]] mistakes it for a threat and mounts a defense whose symptoms, from sneezing to a dangerous drop in blood pressure, are the damage.

Most common allergies follow the same sequence. On a first exposure, the body produces a class of antibody called IgE against the substance, which coats mast cells in the skin, airways, and gut. This sensitization causes no symptoms. On later exposures, the substance binds the IgE and the mast cells burst open, releasing **histamine** and other chemicals that dilate blood vessels, make them leak, tighten airways, and stimulate nerves; see [[Inflammation]] and [[Itch]].

The range of reactions:

- **Hay fever.** Pollen in the nose and eyes, causing sneezing, congestion, and itching in season.
- **Asthma.** Allergic inflammation narrows the airways.
- **Eczema.** Itchy, inflamed skin, often the first allergic condition in infancy.
- **Food allergy.** Hives, vomiting, or worse within minutes of eating.
- **Anaphylaxis.** A body-wide reaction with swelling, airway closure, and collapsing blood pressure, treated with an injection of epinephrine.

Allergies have become far more common in wealthy countries over the past century. The **hygiene hypothesis**, proposed by the epidemiologist David Strachan in 1989, noted that children with more older siblings had less hay fever, and suggested that fewer early infections leave the immune system poorly trained. Later versions emphasize contact with diverse microbes from soil, animals, and food, which shape the [[Gut Microbiome]] and teach tolerance, rather than infections themselves.

Early exposure turned out to matter for food too. For years parents were told to keep peanuts away from infants, and peanut allergy kept rising. The LEAP trial, published in 2015, gave high-risk infants peanut regularly from early in their first year, which cut peanut allergy by over 80 percent, and guidelines reversed; see [[Cobra Effect]].

Treatments target different points. Antihistamines block histamine's receptors; older ones cross into the brain and cause drowsiness, while newer ones mostly don't. Steroid sprays reduce inflammation. **Immunotherapy** gives gradually increasing doses of the allergen, by injection or under the tongue, until the immune system becomes tolerant, a process that takes years; see [[Hormesis]].

Allergy tests produce many false positives: a positive skin or blood test shows sensitization, not necessarily a reaction, so a test without a history can mislead; see [[Base Rate Fallacy]]. Around one in ten people carry a penicillin allergy label, yet most of them can take it safely, and the label pushes doctors toward broader drugs; see [[Antibiotic Resistance]].

Climate change is lengthening pollen seasons in many regions, with earlier springs and higher pollen counts.
