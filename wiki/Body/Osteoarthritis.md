**Osteoarthritis** is the most common form of arthritis, a condition in which the tissues of a joint gradually break down and remodel. It most often affects knees, hips, hands, and the spine, and causes pain, stiffness, and loss of movement. Worldwide it affects hundreds of millions of people and is a leading cause of disability in older adults.

The disease was long described as wear and tear, as if joints simply wore out like car tires. Researchers now see it as a disease of the whole joint:

- **Cartilage.** The smooth, slippery layer covering bone ends thins and roughens. Cartilage has no blood supply, so it repairs itself poorly; see [[Watershed Blood Supply]].
- **Bone.** The bone beneath thickens, and bony outgrowths called osteophytes form at the joint edges; see [[Bone Remodeling]].
- **Lining.** The membrane around the joint becomes inflamed and produces fluid; see [[Inflammation]].
- **Ligaments and muscle.** Supporting structures weaken and loosen; see [[Tendon and Ligament]].

Risk rises with age, and with obesity, which increases load on knees but also seems to affect joints through metabolic effects, since it raises the risk of hand osteoarthritis too; see [[Body Composition]]. Genetics play a part. So does past injury: a torn knee ligament or meniscus, or a badly healed fracture, raises the risk of osteoarthritis in that joint years later, sometimes called post-traumatic arthritis; see [[Ankle Sprain]].

A common worry is that running destroys knees. Evidence suggests that recreational running doesn't raise, and may even lower, the risk of knee osteoarthritis, though very high volumes and prior injury may change the picture. Cartilage depends on loading and unloading to stay healthy, and inactivity is also bad for joints.

Pain in osteoarthritis tracks imaging poorly. Some people with severe changes on an X-ray have little pain, while others with mild changes hurt a lot, partly because the nervous system can amplify pain signals over time; see [[Central Sensitization]].

Treatment starts with movement. Exercise, especially strengthening the muscles around a joint, and weight loss where relevant, are recommended first; see [[Strength Training]]. Pain relievers, from acetaminophen to anti-inflammatory drugs and injections, follow; see [[Analgesic Ladder]]. Arthroscopic surgery to clean out knee joints was common until trials, including one comparing it with sham surgery published in 2002, found little benefit beyond placebo; see [[Placebo Effect]]. For severe disease, joint replacement is among the most successful operations in medicine.

Osteoarthritis differs from osteoporosis, which thins bone itself, and from rheumatoid arthritis, an autoimmune disease; see [[Osteoporosis]]. Engineers studying how materials and machines fail see a similar pattern of gradual wear interacting with damage; see [[Bathtub Curve]].
