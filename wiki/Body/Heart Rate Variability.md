**Heart rate variability (HRV)** is the variation in time between successive heartbeats. A resting heart at 60 beats per minute doesn't beat exactly once a second. The gaps might run 0.95, 1.08, 1.01 seconds. That variation is healthy. It reflects the *autonomic nervous system* continually adjusting the heart, balancing the sympathetic branch (fight or flight, which speeds the heart and steadies its rhythm) against the parasympathetic branch (rest and digest, which slows it and adds variability through the [[Vagus Nerve|vagus nerve]]).

The clearest example is *respiratory sinus arrhythmia*: heart rate rises slightly on each inhale and falls on each exhale. Slow breathing at around six breaths a minute amplifies the swing, which is the basis of HRV biofeedback.

Higher resting HRV generally indicates stronger parasympathetic activity and better recovery. Lower HRV, compared with a person's own baseline, is associated with stress, illness, poor sleep, alcohol, heat, and accumulated training fatigue. Across populations, low HRV predicts worse cardiovascular outcomes. HRV is measured by several statistics. Wearables mostly report *RMSSD*, a measure of how much consecutive intervals differ, usually taken during sleep or right after waking.

Athletes use morning HRV to guide training: a reading well below one's rolling baseline suggests backing off, and a normal one suggests the body has absorbed recent [[Training Load]]. Studies of HRV-guided training show modest benefits over fixed plans, mostly from avoiding hard sessions on bad days. It complements, rather than replaces, other signals in the fitness-fatigue picture of [[Supercompensation]], and it sits alongside resting heart rate and the [[Heart Rate Zones]] used during exercise.

The caveats are substantial:

- **Individual.** Normal values vary several-fold between healthy people and fall with age, so comparing your number with someone else's is meaningless. Only trends against your own baseline carry information.
- **Noisy.** A single reading moves with posture, breathing, hydration, and timing, so rolling averages are more useful than daily numbers.
- **Device-dependent.** Chest-strap ECG measures beat timing accurately. Wrist optical sensors are reasonable at night and poor during movement.
- **Gameable.** Optimizing the score directly, rather than what it reflects, runs into [[Goodhart's Law]].

HRV is also used as a broad index of stress physiology, linking it to [[Allostatic Load]], [[Emotional Regulation]], and [[Interoception]]. Some popular theories built on vagal tone, notably polyvagal theory, go well beyond the evidence. HRV rises during deep [[Sleep Stages]], follows a [[Circadian Rhythm]], and drops in heat (see [[Thermoregulation]]).
