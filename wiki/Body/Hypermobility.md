**Hypermobility** means joints that move further than usual. Someone who can bend their thumb back to touch their forearm, or bend their elbows and knees backward past straight, has hypermobile joints. It's sometimes called being double-jointed, though the joints are normal in number; the difference lies in the connective tissue around them; see [[Tendon and Ligament]].

It's common. Estimates vary widely with age, sex and ancestry, but a substantial minority of people have some generalized hypermobility. It's more frequent in children, in women, and in people of Asian and African ancestry, and joints tend to stiffen with age. The standard screening tool is the **Beighton score**, published in 1973, a nine-point scale checking the little fingers, thumbs, elbows, knees and the ability to put the palms flat on the floor with straight knees.

For many people it causes no trouble, and it can be an advantage. Dancers, gymnasts, swimmers and musicians are often hypermobile, partly because flexibility helps those pursuits and partly because people who find them easy are drawn to them. Stretching can increase range of motion in anyone, but hypermobility differs in being a property of the tissue rather than of training; see [[Stretching]].

For others, it comes with problems. Joints that move too freely are more prone to partial dislocations, sprains and early wear; see [[Ankle Sprain]] and [[Osteoarthritis]]. Many hypermobile people have long-term pain, sometimes widespread and hard to explain from the joints alone; see [[Central Sensitization]]. Fatigue and dizziness on standing, from blood pooling in the legs, are commonly reported; see [[Syncope]]. Hypermobility also appears to be more common among autistic people and people with ADHD, though the link isn't well understood; see [[Neurodiversity]].

At the far end are inherited connective tissue disorders. The **Ehlers-Danlos syndromes** are a group of conditions affecting collagen, the main structural protein in skin, ligaments and blood vessels. A 2017 classification described thirteen types. Most have identified genetic causes, but the most common, hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, has none yet and is diagnosed by clinical criteria. People who have symptomatic hypermobility but don't meet those criteria are described as having hypermobility spectrum disorder.

Hypermobile people often have reduced proprioception, a weaker sense of where their joints are in space, which may contribute to injury and clumsiness; see [[Proprioception]]. The usual management therefore emphasizes building strength and control rather than more flexibility. Gradual strength training gives muscles the job that loose ligaments can't do, and balance and coordination work sharpens joint sense; see [[Strength Training]]. Pacing activity to avoid boom-and-bust cycles helps with pain and fatigue; see [[Training Load]]. Posture habits such as locking the knees backward when standing can be retrained; see [[Posture]].
