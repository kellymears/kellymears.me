The **gut microbiome** is the community of microorganisms living in the digestive tract, mostly bacteria in the colon, along with archaea, fungi, and viruses. A 2016 estimate put the bacteria at around 38 trillion, roughly the same as the number of human cells, revising the old claim that microbes outnumber us ten to one. Hundreds of species live in a typical gut, and the mix differs a great deal between people.

Some of what they do is well established:

- **Fermenting fiber.** Human enzymes cannot digest many plant fibers. Gut bacteria break them down by [[Fermentation]], producing short-chain fatty acids such as butyrate, which is the main fuel of the cells lining the colon.
- **Making vitamins.** Some bacteria produce vitamin K and several B vitamins.
- **Training the immune system.** Contact with microbes early in life shapes how the immune system tells harmless from harmful, and an imbalanced microbiome is associated with chronic [[Inflammation]].
- **Metabolizing drugs.** Gut bacteria can activate or deactivate medications. One species inactivates the heart drug digoxin, a striking extension of [[Xenobiotic Metabolism]] beyond the liver.
- **Resisting invaders.** An established community crowds out pathogens.

That last point has the strongest clinical evidence. Antibiotics can wipe out much of the community, letting *Clostridioides difficile* take over and cause severe, recurring diarrhea. Transferring stool from a healthy donor, a **fecal microbiota transplant**, cures most recurrent cases, far better than more antibiotics.

The microbiome forms early, seeded by birth route, feeding, and environment, and stays fairly stable in adults, though diet shifts it within days. A 2021 Stanford study found that ten weeks of fermented foods such as yogurt, kimchi, and kefir raised microbial diversity and lowered inflammatory markers, while a high-fiber diet did not change diversity over that period.

Athletes have a curious example. A 2019 study found *Veillonella* bacteria enriched in marathon runners after races. These bacteria consume lactate, and mice given them ran longer, linking the gut to the muscle chemistry in [[Lactate Threshold]].

The **gut-brain axis** is the most hyped area. The gut and brain communicate through the [[Vagus Nerve]], hormones, and immune signals, and mice raised without microbes behave differently. In humans, microbiome differences are associated with depression, autism, obesity, and [[Insulin Sensitivity]], but associations run both ways: illness changes diet, which changes the microbiome. Few human trials show that changing the microbes changes the condition, so much of the field is still sorting out [[Correlation and Causation]]. Over-the-counter probiotics are mostly unproven for healthy people, and their strains rarely persist.

Gut signals also contribute to the body sense of [[Interoception]], which may explain why digestion and mood so often travel together.
