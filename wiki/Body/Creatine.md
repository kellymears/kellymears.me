**Creatine** is a compound made by the body from amino acids and found in meat and fish. Most of it is stored in muscle, where it holds a reserve of quickly available energy. As a supplement, usually in the form of creatine monohydrate, it is one of the most researched and effective products in sports nutrition.

Muscles run on ATP, the molecule that powers contraction, but they store only enough for a few seconds of hard work. Creatine, bound to phosphate as phosphocreatine, rapidly hands its phosphate back to regenerate ATP, sustaining all-out efforts like sprints and heavy lifts for roughly ten seconds before slower energy pathways take over; see [[Energy Systems]]. More stored creatine extends that window slightly and speeds recovery between repeated efforts.

The body makes about a gram a day and a typical diet with meat supplies about another gram. Supplements raise muscle stores by roughly 20 to 40 percent, with the largest increase in people who start low, such as vegetarians. A common approach is about 3 to 5 grams a day; a higher-dose "loading" phase saturates muscle within a week but isn't necessary.

The effects are well documented:

- **Strength and power.** Combined with resistance training, creatine increases gains in strength and lean mass more than training alone; see [[Strength Training]] and [[Muscle Hypertrophy]].
- **Repeated sprints.** It helps most in sports with repeated short bursts, and in high-intensity intervals; see [[Interval Training]].
- **Endurance.** Benefits for long steady efforts are small, and the added water weight can be a slight disadvantage.

Creatine draws water into muscle cells, so people typically gain a kilogram or two in the first weeks; see [[Fluid Balance]]. Concerns that it causes cramps, dehydration, or kidney damage in healthy people have not been supported by long-term studies; see [[Muscle Cramp]].

Research has expanded to aging and the brain. Combined with resistance training, creatine may help older adults preserve muscle and strength; see [[Sarcopenia]]. The brain also uses creatine for energy, and studies have looked at effects on memory under sleep deprivation, depression, and recovery from brain injury, with mixed but promising results; see [[Sleep Deprivation]] and [[Concussion]].

Creatine is a useful example in how supplement claims should be judged. Most products marketed to athletes have little evidence behind them, while creatine has hundreds of controlled trials; see [[Randomized Controlled Trial]]. Still, some people respond little. Supplements are loosely regulated in many countries, so contamination with banned substances is a real risk for tested athletes.

Creatine doesn't replace training. It modestly increases how much work a muscle can do, which only matters when that work is part of a well-designed program; see [[Training Load]] and [[Supercompensation]].
