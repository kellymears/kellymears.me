The **kidneys** are two fist-sized organs tucked against the back wall of the abdomen. Each holds on the order of a million filtering units called **nephrons**. Together they receive about a fifth of the heart's output and filter roughly 180 liters of plasma a day, then reclaim all but one or two liters of it. What remains leaves as urine; see [[Cardiac Output]].

Each nephron works in two steps. A knot of capillaries called the glomerulus pushes water and small molecules out of the blood under pressure, holding back cells and large proteins. A long tubule then reabsorbs what the body wants to keep, such as glucose, sodium, bicarbonate and most of the water, and secretes extra waste into the stream. Hormones adjust the tubules minute to minute, which is how the kidneys keep blood volume and salt concentration within narrow limits; see [[Fluid Balance]] and [[Osmosis]].

That control reaches well beyond waste disposal:

- **Blood pressure.** When the kidneys sense low flow they release renin, starting a hormone chain that constricts vessels and retains salt and water. Many blood pressure drugs act on this system; see [[Blood Pressure]].
- **Acid-base balance.** The kidneys excrete acid and regenerate bicarbonate, the slow partner to the lungs in keeping blood near pH 7.4; see [[pH]].
- **Red blood cells.** Low oxygen prompts the kidneys to release erythropoietin, the hormone athletes chase with altitude camps; see [[Altitude Training]].
- **Bone.** The kidneys perform the last step in activating vitamin D; see [[Vitamin D]].

Doctors track kidney function with the **glomerular filtration rate**, usually estimated from blood creatinine, a breakdown product of muscle creatine. Very muscular people and those taking supplements can show high creatinine with healthy kidneys, a common source of false alarms; see [[Creatine]].

The kidneys are vulnerable to what they filter. Myoglobin released from damaged muscle can block the tubules after extreme exertion; see [[Rhabdomyolysis]]. Heavy use of anti-inflammatory painkillers reduces blood flow to them, especially in dehydrated athletes; see [[Analgesic Ladder]]. The leading causes of chronic kidney disease are [[Diabetes]] and high blood pressure, which damage the glomeruli slowly and often silently.

**Kidney stones** form when minerals, most often calcium oxalate, crystallize out of concentrated urine. Passing one is famously painful, and drinking enough water to keep urine dilute is the simplest prevention.

When the kidneys fail, **dialysis** filters the blood artificially. Willem Kolff built an early working machine in the occupied Netherlands in the 1940s, and the first successful kidney transplant, between identical twins in Boston in 1954, opened the era of organ transplantation. A person can live normally with one healthy kidney, which is what makes living donation possible.
