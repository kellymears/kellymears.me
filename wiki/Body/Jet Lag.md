**Jet lag** is the mismatch between the body's internal clock and the local time after rapid travel across time zones. The [[Circadian Rhythm]] that times sleep, alertness, body temperature, digestion, and hormone release keeps running on home time for days after arrival. The result is sleepiness during the day, wakefulness at night, poor concentration, digestive upset, and a general sense of being unwell.

The clock adjusts slowly, typically about one hour per day, so a six-hour shift can take most of a week to fully resolve.

**Direction matters.** Most people find flying east harder than flying west. The human internal clock runs slightly longer than 24 hours on average, so it naturally drifts later. Flying west requires delaying the clock, which goes with its natural tendency. Flying east requires advancing it, waking and sleeping earlier, which works against it.

**Light is the main lever.** The clock is reset mainly by light reaching special light-sensitive cells in the eye, the same system described under [[Circadian Lighting]]. The effect depends on timing, summarized in the *phase response curve*:

- **Light in the evening and early night**, before the body's temperature low point in the early morning, *delays* the clock, shifting sleep later.
- **Light in the early morning**, after that low point, *advances* the clock, shifting sleep earlier.

Getting light at the wrong time can push the clock the wrong way. After a long eastward flight, morning light at the destination may land before the traveler's temperature low point, delaying them further, so some guidance recommends avoiding early morning light for the first day or two and seeking it later in the morning.

Other tools:

- **Melatonin.** The hormone the body releases in the evening to signal darkness. Taken in small doses, about 0.5 milligrams, at the right local time, it helps shift the clock and is one of the better-supported remedies.
- **Shifting in advance.** Moving sleep times an hour a day toward the destination's schedule before departure.
- **Timed meals, exercise, and caffeine.** All provide weaker clock signals, and caffeine counters daytime sleepiness, though it can disrupt night sleep by blocking adenosine; see [[Sleep Pressure]].

Jet lag affects performance in measurable ways. Studies of professional sports have found that teams traveling east perform worse, and errors and accidents rise among shift workers and travelers. Disrupted timing also lowers [[Insulin Sensitivity]] and disturbs the architecture of [[Sleep Stages]].

A milder version happens without flying. *Social jet lag* is the gap between sleep timing on workdays and free days; sleeping in on weekends shifts the clock later, so Monday morning feels like flying east. Daylight saving changes, discussed alongside [[Time Zone]] quirks, impose a small jet lag on whole populations twice a year.
