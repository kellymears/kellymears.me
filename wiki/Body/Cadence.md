**Cadence** is the rate at which a cyclist turns the pedals, in revolutions per minute (rpm), or a runner takes steps, in steps per minute. For the same speed, a cyclist can push hard on the pedals in a big gear at low cadence or spin a small gear quickly. The choice changes how the effort is shared between muscles and the heart and lungs.

The physics is simple. Power is [[Torque]] multiplied by rotational speed. To produce 200 watts at 60 rpm, each pedal stroke must push considerably harder than at 90 rpm. Lower cadence means fewer but more forceful contractions; higher cadence means more contractions, each lighter.

That trade-off matters because of how muscles work:

- **Force per stroke.** Heavy pushes recruit more fast-twitch fibers, which burn [[Glycogen]] quickly and tire. Lighter strokes lean on fatigue-resistant slow-twitch fibers. See [[Muscle Fiber Types]].
- **Cardiovascular cost.** Moving the legs faster has its own cost. At the same power, higher cadence raises heart rate and oxygen use slightly, so the heart and lungs carry more of the load.
- **Joints.** High forces at low cadence put more strain on the knees, a common source of overuse pain. See [[Tendon and Ligament]].

Lab studies have long found a puzzle. For submaximal efforts, the metabolically cheapest cadence is fairly low, around 50 to 70 rpm, yet trained cyclists freely choose higher cadences, often 85 to 100 rpm. One explanation is that the cheapest cadence for the heart isn't the one that best preserves the legs: spinning faster reduces muscle force and glycogen use, delaying fatigue over a long ride.

Recreational riders often pedal too slowly, around 60 to 70 rpm, grinding big gears. Gradually raising cadence usually improves comfort and endurance. Power meters and cadence sensors make it easy to track alongside [[Functional Threshold Power]] and [[Heart Rate Zones]].

Running has a parallel debate. The coach Jack Daniels counted the steps of elite runners at the 1984 Olympics and found nearly all took about 180 steps per minute or more, while many recreational runners take 150 to 160. A modest increase in step rate, around 5 to 10 percent, shortens the stride, reduces overstriding, and lowers impact loads on the knees and hips. The number 180 is not a universal target, though; cadence naturally rises with speed and varies with height.

On a bike, cadence also interacts with position and [[Aerodynamic Drag]], since a high cadence lets riders hold an aero tuck without grinding, and with group riding, where frequent changes in speed in a [[Drafting]] line reward riders who can spin up quickly. How cadence affects fuel use ties it to the body's [[Energy Systems]].
