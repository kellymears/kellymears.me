**VO2 max** is the maximum rate at which a person can take in, transport, and use oxygen during exercise, usually expressed in milliliters of oxygen per kilogram of body weight per minute. It sets the ceiling on aerobic energy production: above it, any extra work must come from anaerobic sources that can only be sustained for a few minutes.

The Fick equation breaks it into two parts: oxygen uptake equals **cardiac output** (how much blood the heart pumps) times the **arteriovenous oxygen difference** (how much oxygen the muscles pull out of that blood). In trained people the heart is usually the limit. Endurance training enlarges the heart's stroke volume and expands blood volume, while muscles grow more capillaries and mitochondria to extract oxygen. Untrained adults typically measure in the 30s to 40s. Elite cross-country skiers and cyclists reach the 80s and occasionally the 90s.

It is partly fixed. Large training studies such as HERITAGE found that people given identical programs improved by wildly different amounts, with some barely responding, and that the response is substantially heritable. VO2 max also declines with age, roughly 10% per decade after early adulthood, though training slows that decline considerably.

VO2 max is a ceiling, not a performance. Among athletes with similar values, the winner is usually the one who can sustain a higher fraction of it, which is their [[Lactate Threshold]], and who wastes less energy moving (economy). That is why a laboratory number predicts race results only loosely within a trained group, and why most training aims at the threshold and at [[Functional Threshold Power]] more than at the ceiling itself.

Its strongest claim may be outside sport. Cardiorespiratory fitness is one of the most powerful predictors of all-cause mortality in large cohort studies, comparable to or stronger than smoking or blood pressure. The gap between the least fit and the merely below-average is the largest. Wearables now estimate VO2 max from heart rate and pace. Those figures are model outputs, useful for trends rather than as measurements, and prone to [[Regression to the Mean]] when a single good or bad day moves them.
