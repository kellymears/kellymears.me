**Heat acclimation** is the set of adaptations the body makes to repeated exercise in hot conditions. Strictly, *acclimation* refers to artificial exposure such as sessions in a heat chamber, and *acclimatization* to natural exposure from living or training in a hot place, though the terms are often used interchangeably. Most of the adaptation happens within 10 to 14 days of daily heat sessions of an hour or more, and the biggest changes come in the first week.

Exercise in heat strains [[Thermoregulation]] in two ways at once. Muscles produce heat, and the body must shed it by sending blood to the skin and by sweating, which competes with blood flow to working muscles and draws down blood volume. Heart rate rises, a process called cardiac drift (see [[Heart Rate Zones]]), and performance falls. Acclimation eases both:

- **Plasma volume expands** by up to about 10 percent within days, supporting both cooling and cardiac output.
- **Sweating starts earlier and increases**, giving more evaporative cooling.
- **Sweat becomes more dilute.** The sweat glands reabsorb more sodium, conserving electrolytes.
- **Core temperature and heart rate run lower** at the same workload, and the sensation of heat feels less oppressive.

The adaptations decay over a few weeks without heat exposure, so athletes heading to a hot race schedule a block shortly beforehand. Endurance athletes also use heat training in cool seasons for the plasma-volume boost, a kind of [[Hormesis]] that some argue mimics altitude training. Whether it improves performance in cool conditions is debated.

Sweat rates vary from about half a liter to over two liters an hour between individuals and conditions, so fluid needs vary just as widely. Drinking far more than sweat losses can cause *exertional hyponatremia*, dangerously diluted blood sodium, as water moves into cells by [[Osmosis]] and the brain swells. It has caused deaths at marathons. Thirst, combined with knowing one's approximate sweat rate, is a reasonable guide.

Heat also changes fuel use, increasing reliance on [[Glycogen]], and dampens [[Heart Rate Variability]]. Evaporation works only when the air can absorb moisture, so humid heat is much more dangerous than dry heat at the same temperature. Airflow matters too, which is why a cyclist moving at speed feels cooler than a runner. At the slow speeds of a long climb, [[Aerodynamic Drag]] and its cooling breeze disappear and overheating risk rises. Heat stroke, with confusion and a core temperature above about 40 °C, is a medical emergency, and acclimation reduces but doesn't remove the risk.
