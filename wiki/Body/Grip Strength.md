**Grip strength** is the maximum force a person can generate by squeezing with the hand. It's measured with a handheld dynamometer, a device that registers how hard someone can squeeze its handle, usually taking the best of a few attempts per hand. The test takes a minute, needs no special training, and costs little, which has made it one of the most widely collected physical measurements in medical research.

It's also surprisingly informative. In a study published in 2015 that followed nearly 140,000 adults in seventeen countries, each five-kilogram drop in grip strength was associated with about a 16 percent higher risk of death from any cause over the following years. Grip strength predicted death from cardiovascular disease better than systolic blood pressure did; see [[Blood Pressure]]. Other studies have linked weak grip to disability, falls, hospital stays, and cognitive decline in older adults.

Why should a squeeze predict so much? Mostly because grip strength is a window onto overall muscle mass and condition. Strong hands tend to belong to people with more muscle elsewhere, more physical activity, and fewer chronic illnesses; see [[Body Composition]]. Illness, poor nutrition, and inactivity all weaken grip, so a low reading captures declining health from many causes at once.

That makes it a valuable marker but a misleading target. The associations come from observational studies, and a hand exerciser that raises grip strength without changing the rest of the body probably wouldn't buy the same benefit; see [[Observational Study]] and [[Confounding]]. Turning a useful indicator into a goal can strip it of what made it informative; see [[Goodhart's Law]].

Grip strength is central to diagnosing **sarcopenia**, the loss of muscle with age. Clinical guidelines use grip thresholds as a first screen, followed by measurements of muscle mass and walking speed; see [[Sarcopenia]]. Grip typically peaks in the thirties and declines gradually afterward, faster in later decades. Muscle lost during inactivity or illness takes effort to regain; see [[Detraining]].

Several kinds of grip are distinguished in training:

- **Crush** grip is squeezing between fingers and palm, the movement a dynamometer measures.
- **Support** grip is holding on for time, as when carrying heavy bags or hanging from a bar, limited by endurance; see [[Muscle Fatigue]].
- **Pinch** grip is holding between thumb and fingers.

Resistance training improves grip along with everything else, particularly exercises that require holding heavy loads; see [[Strength Training]]. Climbers develop exceptional finger strength, at the cost of a high rate of finger tendon injuries; see [[Tendon and Ligament]].

Nerve problems can weaken grip without any loss of muscle. Compression of the nerves at the elbow or wrist causes numbness and clumsiness in the hand; see [[Ulnar Neuropathy]] and [[Repetitive Strain Injury]].
