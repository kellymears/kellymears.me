**Myopia**, or nearsightedness, is blurry distance vision with clear near vision. In most cases the eyeball has grown slightly too long from front to back, so light from distant objects comes to a focus in front of the retina instead of on it. Glasses or contact lenses correct the blur by spreading the light out before it enters the eye, using [[Refraction]] to shift the focal point back.

During childhood, the eye grows in a guided way called *emmetropization*: visual feedback from the retina regulates growth so that focus ends up right on target. Myopia develops when this feedback drives the eye to keep elongating past that point.

Myopia has become far more common in a short time. A 2016 analysis led by Brien Holden estimated that about a third of the world's population was myopic in 2020 and projected about half by 2050. In parts of East Asia, 80 to 90 percent of young adults finishing school are now myopic, up from a small minority a few generations ago. A change that fast is environmental, not genetic, although genes affect individual risk.

The leading environmental factor is **time outdoors**. Studies in the 2000s, including a large Sydney study led by Kathryn Rose in 2008, found children who spent more time outside were less likely to become myopic, regardless of how much reading they did. Trials in China and Taiwan that added outdoor time to the school day reduced new cases. The likely reason is light intensity. Outdoor daylight is commonly 10,000 lux or more, compared with a few hundred indoors, and bright light is thought to release dopamine in the retina, which slows the eye's growth. The same light also affects the [[Circadian Rhythm]], as discussed under [[Circadian Lighting]].

Near work, including reading and screens, was long assumed to be the main cause. It does contribute, but its effect is weaker and less consistent than that of outdoor time, and the two are tangled, since children who read a lot tend to spend less time outside. Common advice is to aim for around two hours outdoors per day in childhood and to take breaks from close work, such as looking at something far away every 20 minutes.

Myopia matters beyond inconvenience. High myopia, a very long eye, stretches the retina and raises lifelong risks of retinal detachment, glaucoma, cataract, and myopic macular degeneration, a leading cause of irreversible vision loss. Treatments that slow it include low-dose atropine eye drops, special contact lenses worn overnight to reshape the cornea, and spectacle lenses designed to put the edges of the image in front of the retina, signaling the eye to stop growing.

Time in natural settings has other benefits for attention and mood, described in [[Attention Restoration Theory]].
