**Shin splints**, known medically as medial tibial stress syndrome, is pain along the inner edge of the shinbone, the tibia, usually in its lower third. It comes on during or after running and jumping and is among the most common injuries in runners, military recruits, and dancers. The pain is typically spread over several centimeters of bone, tender to the touch, and worst at the start of a run.

The cause is load outpacing adaptation. Each footstrike bends the tibia slightly. Bone responds to that strain by remodeling: cells remove old bone and lay down new, stronger bone along the lines of stress; see [[Bone Remodeling]]. Removal comes before rebuilding, so for a period the bone is weaker. If mileage, speed, or hills increase faster than this cycle can keep up, small amounts of damage accumulate along the bone and its covering, and the area becomes painful; see [[Training Load]] and [[Supercompensation]].

Imaging shows the condition sits on a spectrum. Mild cases show stress reaction in the bone surface; continuing to train through it can progress to a [[Stress Fracture]], a true crack, which takes much longer to heal. Pain that becomes sharp and localized to one fingertip-sized spot, or that persists at rest or at night, suggests the more serious end and needs assessment.

Risk factors include:

- **A sudden jump in training,** especially for beginners and recruits starting basic training.
- **Previous shin splints,** the strongest predictor of the next episode.
- **Low energy availability and low bone density,** more common in women and in athletes who under-eat; see [[Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport]], [[Vitamin D]], and [[Osteoporosis]].
- **Running mechanics,** such as overstriding with a heavy heel strike. Raising [[Cadence]] slightly, taking shorter, quicker steps, reduces the load per stride.
- **Foot structure,** with a flatter or collapsing arch linked to higher risk in some studies; see [[Plantar Fasciitis]].

Treatment is mostly reducing load. Running is cut back or replaced with cycling or swimming to keep fitness without the impact, then reintroduced gradually, over several weeks, once pain has gone. Ice and pain relievers can ease symptoms but do not speed healing of the bone. Evidence for special shoes, insoles, and compression sleeves is weak, and none reliably prevents it.

Prevention is the same idea in reverse: increase running volume gradually, mix hard days with easy ones, and add strength work for the calves and hips, which absorb some of the impact; see [[Periodization]] and [[Strength Training]]. Shin splints are one of a family of overuse injuries where tissue fails from repetition, not a single event; see [[Repetitive Strain Injury]] and [[Tendinopathy]].
