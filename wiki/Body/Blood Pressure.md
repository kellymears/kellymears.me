**Blood pressure** is the pressure blood exerts on the walls of the arteries as the heart pumps. It is reported as two numbers in millimeters of mercury: *systolic*, the peak during a heartbeat, over *diastolic*, the low point between beats, as in 118/76. Scipione Riva-Rocci introduced the inflatable cuff in 1896, and Nikolai Korotkov added the method of listening for sounds in the artery as the cuff deflates in 1905, which is still the basis of manual measurement.

Pressure is regulated continuously, an everyday example of [[Homeostasis]]:

- **The baroreflex.** Stretch sensors in the major arteries detect pressure changes within seconds. A rise triggers slowing of the heart through the [[Vagus Nerve]] and widening of blood vessels; a fall does the opposite through the sympathetic nervous system. This beat-to-beat adjustment is part of what [[Heart Rate Variability]] reflects.
- **The kidneys.** Over hours and days, the kidneys set blood volume by retaining or excreting salt and water, the long-term controller, closely tied to [[Fluid Balance]].
- **Hormones.** The renin-angiotensin system constricts vessels and retains sodium when pressure or volume drops.

Standing up quickly briefly pools blood in the legs. If the baroreflex lags, pressure drops, causing the head rush or fainting of *orthostatic hypotension*, more common when dehydrated, in heat, after long exercise, and in fit people with low resting heart rates.

**Hypertension**, chronically high pressure, affects about 1.3 billion adults worldwide, and most do not know they have it, since it usually causes no symptoms. Over years it damages arteries, the heart, kidneys, eyes, and brain, and it is the leading modifiable risk factor for stroke and heart attack. US guidelines since 2017 define it as 130/80 or higher; many other countries use 140/90.

Measuring accurately is surprisingly hard. Readings rise in a doctor's office, the *white coat effect*, and fall with relaxation. Talking, a full bladder, crossed legs, an unsupported arm, or a cuff that is too small all push readings up. A single high reading often comes down on repetition, partly through [[Regression to the Mean]], which is why diagnosis relies on several readings, ideally home or 24-hour monitoring. Pressure normally dips during sleep; a lack of nighttime dipping is itself a risk marker, and sleep apnea is a common hidden cause.

Lifestyle changes have real effects. Regular aerobic exercise lowers systolic pressure by several points, comparable to a single medication, and both aerobic and [[Strength Training]] help. Cutting sodium, losing excess weight, limiting alcohol, and managing chronic stress, the strain described under [[Allostatic Load]], each contribute. During exercise, systolic pressure rises substantially while diastolic stays similar; in heat, widened skin vessels lower pressure, part of the cardiovascular strain covered under [[Thermoregulation]].
