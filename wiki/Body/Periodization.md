**Periodization** is the practice of planning training as a sequence of phases, each with its own emphasis, rather than repeating the same training week after week. The goal is to develop different qualities in an order that builds on itself, manage fatigue, and reach peak fitness at the right time.

The idea is usually traced to Soviet sports scientists of the 1950s and 1960s, especially Lev Matveyev, whose models shaped Eastern Bloc athletics. It draws on the observation that the body adapts to a stress during recovery, and that the same stimulus stops working once the body has adapted to it; see [[Supercompensation]].

Plans are usually nested:

- **Macrocycle**: a season or year, built around main competitions.
- **Mesocycle**: a block of several weeks with a particular focus.
- **Microcycle**: usually a week, arranging hard and easy days.

A classic endurance season moves through phases:

- **Base.** Long periods of mostly easy aerobic work, building the foundation of [[Mitochondria]], capillaries, and fat use, along with general strength; see [[Heart Rate Zones]] and [[Metabolic Flexibility]].
- **Build.** More race-specific intensity, such as threshold efforts and longer intervals; see [[Lactate Threshold]] and [[Interval Training]].
- **Peak and taper.** Volume drops while some intensity remains, letting fatigue clear while fitness holds, so the athlete arrives fresh.
- **Transition.** A period of rest or unstructured activity to recover physically and mentally before the next cycle.

Within these phases, most plans build load for two or three weeks, then take an easier week to absorb it, sometimes called a deload or recovery week. This rhythm reflects how fatigue accumulates and how adaptation shows up only after rest; see [[Training Load]].

Several models exist. **Linear** periodization moves gradually from high volume and low intensity toward lower volume and higher intensity. **Block** periodization concentrates on one or two qualities at a time in short, focused blocks, useful for advanced athletes whose adaptations need a strong, specific stimulus. **Undulating** periodization varies emphasis within each week, popular in [[Strength Training]].

Evidence comparing the models is limited, mostly from short, small studies, and the main ingredients of success, consistency, sufficient volume, progressive overload, and adequate recovery, matter more than which formal model is used. The distribution of intensity across training also matters a great deal; see [[Polarized Training]].

Periodization has costs if applied rigidly. Life stress, illness, and poor sleep change how much training a person can absorb, so modern coaching adjusts plans based on how the athlete is responding, using measures like [[Heart Rate Variability]] and perceived effort. Fitness gained in one phase also fades if not maintained in the next; see [[Detraining]].

The broader idea, alternating periods of focused stress with periods of recovery, applies well beyond sport; see [[Spaced Repetition]] for a learning counterpart.
