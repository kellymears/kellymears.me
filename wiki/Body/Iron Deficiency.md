**Iron deficiency** is a shortage of the iron the body needs to make hemoglobin, the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen, and to run the enzymes that produce energy inside [[Mitochondria]]. It is the most common nutritional deficiency in the world, especially among menstruating women, children, pregnant people, vegetarians, and endurance athletes.

It develops in stages:

- **Depleted stores.** Iron reserves in the liver and elsewhere run down, shown by low *ferritin*, the protein that stores iron, while hemoglobin is still normal.
- **Iron-deficient red cell production.** The bone marrow lacks iron to build red cells properly.
- **Anemia.** Hemoglobin falls below the normal range, reducing the blood's capacity to carry oxygen.

The first stages can cause symptoms even before anemia appears: fatigue, poor exercise tolerance, breathlessness, restless legs, feeling cold, and difficulty concentrating. In anemia the effect on endurance is direct. Less hemoglobin means less oxygen delivered to working muscle, lowering [[VO2 Max]], and the heart beats faster to compensate, so familiar efforts sit higher in the [[Heart Rate Zones]].

Athletes lose iron in several ways: through sweat, through blood in the gut during long hard efforts, through menstruation, and in runners from red cells broken by repeated foot strikes. Exercise also affects absorption. The liver hormone **hepcidin**, which blocks iron absorption from the gut, rises for several hours after hard exercise as part of the [[Inflammation]] response, so iron taken at the wrong time is poorly absorbed. Underfueling makes everything worse, a common thread with [[Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport]].

Diagnosis relies on blood tests. Ferritin is the main marker, but it rises with inflammation and recent hard training, so a normal value can hide a real deficiency, and tests are best taken rested. Many sports physicians treat ferritin below about 30 to 35 micrograms per liter as deficient in athletes, higher than the general population threshold.

Treatment and prevention:

- **Food.** Heme iron, from meat and fish, is absorbed far better than the non-heme iron in plants. Vitamin C taken with a meal improves non-heme absorption, while tea, coffee, and calcium reduce it.
- **Timing.** Supplements are better absorbed in the morning, before hepcidin rises. Taking them on alternate days, rather than daily, can improve absorption, since each dose itself raises hepcidin for about a day.
- **Testing first.** Iron should not be supplemented blindly. Too much is toxic, and the inherited condition hemochromatosis causes iron overload.

Iron matters especially before [[Altitude Training]], since the body needs ample iron to build the extra red cells that altitude stimulates; without it, the camp produces fatigue rather than adaptation. Unexplained fatigue in an athlete who is otherwise training sensibly, with no sign of overload in [[Training Load]], is worth a blood test.
