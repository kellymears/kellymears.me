**Segmented sleep** is a pattern in which night sleep divides into two bouts — a *first sleep* and a *second sleep* — separated by an hour or more of quiet wakefulness. The historian A. Roger Ekirch assembled the case in *At Day's Close* (2005), citing several hundred references across court depositions, diaries, medical texts and literature in which the interval is treated as unremarkable: people prayed, talked, tended fires, or lay still until the second sleep took them.

Laboratory work gave the idea a physiological anchor. Thomas Wehr's early-1990s experiments placed participants in fourteen hours of darkness nightly; after some weeks their sleep tended to split into two bouts around a wakeful interval marked by elevated prolactin. That is a real result about long nights, and it makes the historical pattern credible.

The dispute is over universality. Ekirch's evidence is documentary and drawn largely from temperate pre-industrial Europe, where winter nights are long; the sources are also indirect, which raises the usual questions of [[Provenance]]. Field studies of societies without electric light — including Tanzanian, Namibian and Bolivian groups — found consolidated single sleep bouts, arguing that segmentation answers long dark nights rather than expressing a human default. The strong reading, that modern consolidated sleep is unnatural, outruns its evidence and is better held as [[Plausible Mechanism]] than fact; the narrow reading survives contact with [[Falsifiability]].

What electric light changed is not in doubt. Evening illumination suppresses melatonin and delays circadian phase, compressing the biological night, an effect compounded by clock conventions covered in [[Time Zone]]. Nocturnal waking is common and usually benign; sedative-hypnotics used against it carry the issues in [[Physical Dependence]].
