**Mitochondria** are small structures inside nearly every human cell that produce most of the body's usable energy. They take fragments of carbohydrate and fat, combine them with oxygen, and use the energy released to make ATP, the molecule that powers muscle contraction, nerve signaling, and almost everything else. The process is called *oxidative phosphorylation*, and it is the oxidative part of the body's [[Energy Systems]].

Their origin is one of biology's great stories. Mitochondria have their own small circle of DNA and divide on their own, much like bacteria. In 1967 Lynn Margulis argued that they descend from free-living bacteria engulfed by an ancestral cell more than a billion years ago, which then lived on as permanent partners. The *endosymbiotic theory* was met with skepticism at first and is now accepted. Because sperm contribute almost no mitochondria to the embryo, mitochondrial DNA is inherited from the mother, which makes it useful for tracing ancestry.

Mitochondria are adaptable, which makes them central to endurance fitness. In 1967 John Holloszy showed that running training roughly doubled the mitochondrial enzymes in rat muscle. Sustained aerobic work triggers *mitochondrial biogenesis*: muscle cells sense energy stress, through signals such as calcium, falling energy levels, and a master regulator protein called PGC-1α, and respond by building more and larger mitochondria. Trained muscle can then:

- **Burn more fat** at a given pace, sparing limited [[Glycogen]].
- **Produce less lactate** at the same effort, pushing up the [[Lactate Threshold]].
- **Extract more oxygen** from the blood, one component of [[VO2 Max]].

This adaptation is a main reason for long, easy sessions in [[Polarized Training]] and the attention given to zone 2 in [[Heart Rate Zones]]. Slow-twitch fibers carry the most mitochondria, as described under [[Muscle Fiber Types]]. The gains also reverse: mitochondrial content falls within weeks of stopping training.

Mitochondria also produce reactive oxygen species as a byproduct, molecules that can damage cells but also act as signals that trigger adaptation. Small doses of this stress make cells more resilient, an example of [[Hormesis]] sometimes called mitohormesis. Large doses of antioxidant supplements can blunt training adaptations, likely by muting the signal.

In brown fat, a special protein lets mitochondria burn fuel without making ATP, releasing the energy as heat instead, a contribution to [[Thermoregulation]] that is especially important in infants. Mitochondrial function also bears on metabolic health: active, plentiful mitochondria in muscle support good [[Insulin Sensitivity]], and declining mitochondrial function is associated with aging and type 2 diabetes. Inherited mitochondrial diseases, caused by faulty mitochondrial DNA, often affect muscle and nerves first, the tissues with the highest energy needs. Training at altitude adds further oxygen-related adaptations, described under [[Altitude Training]].
