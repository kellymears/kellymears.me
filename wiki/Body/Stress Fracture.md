A **stress fracture** is a small crack in a bone caused not by a single impact but by repeated loading. Each footstrike, jump, or march produces tiny amounts of damage in bone. Normally the body repairs that damage continuously. A stress fracture happens when damage builds up faster than repair can keep up. It's a fatigue failure, like a paperclip bent back and forth until it breaks.

Bone responds to load. The nineteenth-century surgeon Julius Wolff observed that bone adapts its structure to the forces placed on it, a principle known as **Wolff's law**. Regular loading makes bones denser and stronger; disuse, as in bed rest or spaceflight, weakens them. But adaptation takes weeks to months. In the short term, a sudden increase in loading first triggers the removal of old bone, temporarily weakening it before new bone is laid down; see [[Bone Remodeling]]. That window is when stress fractures happen.

Stress fractures are common in runners, dancers, and military recruits. The most frequent sites are the shin bone, the long bones of the foot, and the heel, with the hip and pelvis less common but more serious. Risk factors include:

- **Training errors.** Sudden increases in mileage, intensity, or hills, or a change in surface; see [[Training Load]].
- **Low energy availability.** Not eating enough to support training disrupts hormones and bone formation. Stress fractures are a hallmark of [[Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport]].
- **Low vitamin D and calcium**; see [[Vitamin D]].
- **Biomechanics and footwear.** Foot structure, running form, and worn shoes change how forces are distributed; see [[Cadence]].
- **Previous stress fracture**, a strong predictor of another.

Symptoms start gradually: pain that comes on during activity and fades with rest, becoming more persistent and localized. A precise spot of tenderness on the bone is a classic sign. X-rays often look normal for weeks, so MRI is used for early diagnosis. Pushing through the pain can turn a stress reaction into a complete fracture.

Treatment usually means reducing load for six to eight weeks or more, often with a protective boot, and a gradual return. Some sites heal poorly because of their blood supply or the forces on them, such as the front of the shin, the hip, or certain foot bones, and may need longer immobilization or surgery; see [[Watershed Blood Supply]], [[Fracture Nonunion]], and [[Fracture Fixation]].

Prevention follows from the mechanism: increase load gradually, eat enough, include strength training to absorb forces, and allow recovery; see [[Strength Training]] and [[Supercompensation]]. Varying surfaces and activities spreads loading across different parts of the skeleton.

Cycling and swimming, being low-impact, do little to load bone, and endurance cyclists can have surprisingly low bone density. Adding impact or resistance training helps maintain it; see [[Sarcopenia]] for the parallel story in muscle.
