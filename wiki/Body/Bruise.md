**A bruise**, or contusion, forms when a blunt impact damages small blood vessels under the skin without breaking it. Blood leaks into the surrounding tissue, producing the familiar discolored, tender patch. Most bruises are harmless and fade within two weeks, though large ones, or those on the legs, where circulation is slower, can take longer. The tenderness of a bruise comes from pressure and inflammation in the damaged tissue, and it usually eases well before the color fades.

The color changes as the body clears the blood. Fresh bruises look red or purple. Bruises under the skin often look blue partly because skin scatters and absorbs light differently at different wavelengths. Over days, immune cells break down the hemoglobin from red blood cells into other pigments; see [[Hemoglobin]] and [[Immune System]]:

- **Green**, from biliverdin.
- **Yellow**, from bilirubin.
- **Brown**, from iron deposits.

Fluid and debris are cleared through the lymphatic system; see [[Lymphatic System]].

Doctors once tried to date bruises by color, especially when investigating abuse. Research has found color a poor guide to age, since it varies with depth, location, skin tone, and lighting, and experts are now cautious about such estimates.

Gravity moves blood. A blow to the forehead can produce a black eye a day later, as blood drains downward through the loose tissue around the eye. A sprained ankle often bruises along the foot well below the injury; see [[Ankle Sprain]].

A **hematoma** is a larger, localized pool of blood that can form a firm lump. Bleeding inside the skull is a medical emergency; see [[Concussion]]. Deep bruising of a large muscle, as from a knee to the thigh in contact sports, can be disabling. In rare cases, swelling within a closed muscle compartment cuts off blood supply; see [[Compartment Syndrome]]. Occasionally, bone forms within a badly bruised muscle as it heals. A bone bruise, where impact damages the bone beneath cartilage, can hurt for months; see [[Bone Remodeling]].

Some people bruise more easily:

- **Older adults**, whose skin and blood vessel walls thin with age.
- **People taking blood thinners** such as aspirin or anticoagulant medications.
- **People with bleeding disorders**, such as hemophilia, or low platelet counts.
- **Vitamin C deficiency**, which weakens collagen in vessel walls, as in scurvy.

Some medicines, including long-term steroids, also thin the skin and make it fragile. Unexplained, frequent, or very large bruises are worth investigating, as are bruises in places that rarely take knocks.

For ordinary bruises, rest, cold packs early on, and elevation can limit swelling, and time does the rest; see [[Inflammation]]. Intense exercise can also damage muscle fibers enough to release their contents into the blood, a different and sometimes dangerous process; see [[Rhabdomyolysis]].
