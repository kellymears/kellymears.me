**Sarcopenia** is the progressive loss of skeletal muscle mass, strength, and function that comes with aging. Muscle mass starts declining slowly from around the fourth decade, often estimated at 3 to 8 percent per decade, and the loss speeds up after 60. Strength falls faster than size, a gap sometimes called *dynapenia*, because the nervous system also loses motor neurons and the muscle that remains is less well recruited. The term, from Greek for "poverty of flesh," was proposed by Irwin Rosenberg in 1989.

The losses are uneven. Fast-twitch type II fibers, the ones that produce power, shrink and disappear first, as [[Muscle Fiber Types]] describes. That is why older adults often keep endurance for walking long after they lose the ability to rise quickly from a chair or catch themselves when tripping.

The consequences go beyond strength:

- **Falls and fractures.** Weak legs and slower reactions cause falls, and weaker bones, under the same disuse signals that drive [[Bone Remodeling]], break more easily. A hip fracture in old age often ends independent living, despite good [[Fracture Fixation]].
- **Metabolism.** Muscle is the largest sink for blood glucose, so losing it worsens [[Insulin Sensitivity]] and lowers [[Basal Metabolic Rate]].
- **Balance.** Loss of muscle combines with decline in [[Proprioception]] and the [[Vestibular System]] to make walking less stable.

Diagnosis now centers on function rather than mass alone. European guidelines from 2019 start with grip strength and chair-rise tests, then confirm with a measure of muscle quantity and, for severity, walking speed.

Several mechanisms combine: fewer motor neurons, declining hormone levels, chronic low-grade [[Inflammation]], fewer working [[Mitochondria]], and *anabolic resistance*, a blunted muscle-building response to protein and exercise. Older adults appear to need roughly twice as much protein per meal as young adults to get the same rise in muscle protein synthesis.

Inactivity speeds all of it. A week or two of bed rest in an older adult can cost as much muscle as years of aging, and the person may never fully regain it, which makes hospital stays a hidden driver of sarcopenia. [[Detraining]] covers how fast fitness fades.

The encouraging finding is that muscle stays trainable. In a 1990 study by Maria Fiatarone, nursing-home residents aged 86 to 96 lifted weights for eight weeks and nearly tripled their leg strength, with some walking unaided again. Resistance training, progressing load gradually as in [[Muscle Hypertrophy]], plus adequate protein, is the most effective treatment known. Drug treatments remain unproven. The useful frame is a reserve built earlier in life and defended later, much like bone density.
