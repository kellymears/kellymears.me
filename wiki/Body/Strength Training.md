**Strength training** is exercise that makes muscles work against substantial resistance, from barbells, machines, bands, or body weight, to increase strength and muscle size. It has moved from a niche for bodybuilders to a mainstay of health guidance. Most public health guidelines now recommend muscle-strengthening activity at least twice a week alongside aerobic exercise.

The basic principle is progressive overload: muscles adapt to demands slightly beyond what they are used to, so the load, repetitions, or sets must gradually rise for progress to continue, the cycle described under [[Supercompensation]]. Early gains are mostly neural. In the first weeks, the nervous system learns to recruit more muscle fibers and coordinate them better, a form of [[Motor Learning]]; real growth of muscle tissue, described under [[Muscle Hypertrophy]], follows over months.

How heavy and how many matter less than once believed. Research since the 2010s shows that sets of anywhere from about six to thirty repetitions build similar muscle, as long as they are taken close to failure. Maximum strength, by contrast, is specific: lifting heavy loads, near one's *one-rep max*, trains the skill of producing maximal force. Many lifters gauge effort by *reps in reserve*, how many more repetitions they could have managed.

The benefits extend well beyond muscle:

- **Bone.** Heavy loading is one of the strongest signals for [[Bone Remodeling]] to build density, important for non-weight-bearing athletes such as cyclists and swimmers.
- **Tendons.** Slow, heavy loading stiffens and strengthens [[Tendon and Ligament]] tissue and is a front-line treatment for tendon pain.
- **Aging.** Muscle mass and power decline from midlife, a process called [[Sarcopenia]]; strength training is the most effective countermeasure, preserving independence and reducing falls.
- **Metabolism.** More muscle and regular contraction improve [[Insulin Sensitivity]].

For endurance athletes, the long-held fear was that lifting would add bulk and blunt aerobic fitness. Studies, many on cyclists and runners, found that heavy strength training improves efficiency, sprint power, and late-race performance with little weight gain, since concurrent endurance training limits growth. The aerobic and strength signals do partly compete within muscle, so separating sessions by several hours, and keeping volume moderate, helps.

Starting out, unfamiliar movements, especially lowering phases, cause [[Delayed Onset Muscle Soreness]] that fades as the body adapts. Technique and gradual progression matter more than load in the first months. Stopping causes [[Detraining]], though strength fades more slowly than aerobic fitness, and muscle regains faster the second time around.

The adaptations differ by fiber: heavy training enlarges fast-twitch fibers most, as described under [[Muscle Fiber Types]]. Strength training is one of the few interventions that improves almost every tissue it touches, which is why it shows up in advice for everything from back pain to [[Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport]].
