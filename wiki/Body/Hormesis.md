**Hormesis** is a dose-response pattern in which a low dose of something harmful produces a beneficial effect, while a high dose does damage. Graphed, the response is biphasic: improvement at low doses, crossing to harm as the dose rises. The term was coined in 1943 by Chester Southam and John Ehrlich, who found that low concentrations of an extract from red cedar stimulated the growth of fungi that higher concentrations killed. The toxicologist Edward Calabrese spent decades documenting the pattern across thousands of studies and argued it is common rather than exceptional.

The mechanism is adaptive overcompensation. A mild stressor triggers repair and defense systems, such as antioxidant enzymes, heat shock proteins that refold damaged proteins, and [[Mitochondria|mitochondrial growth]], and the response overshoots, leaving the organism more resilient than before. That is the same logic as the old saying "what doesn't kill you makes you stronger," with the crucial qualifier that dose determines which outcome you get.

Familiar examples:

- **Exercise.** A workout produces oxidative stress and muscle damage, and the recovery builds fitness, as with [[Supercompensation]]. The [[Delayed Onset Muscle Soreness]] repeated bout effect is hormesis on a small scale.
- **Heat and cold.** Repeated heat exposure produces [[Heat Acclimation]], and cold exposure similarly trains [[Thermoregulation]].
- **Bone and tissue.** Loading strengthens bone, as described under [[Bone Remodeling]], and [[Tendon and Ligament]] tissue adapts to graded strain.
- **Food chemistry.** Some plant compounds may benefit health partly by mildly stressing cells and activating detoxification pathways, the same pathways described under [[Xenobiotic Metabolism]].

One striking result shows the stress itself is the signal. In a 2009 study, taking high-dose vitamin C and E supplements blunted the improvements in insulin sensitivity that exercise produced, apparently by mopping up the oxidative signals that trigger adaptation. Similar findings suggest routine anti-inflammatory use around training may reduce gains.

Hormesis is also contested. Radiation hormesis, the claim that low radiation doses are beneficial, remains controversial, and regulators assume no safe threshold. "Hormetic" has become a wellness marketing term attached to cold plunges and supplements with thin evidence, so a [[Plausible Mechanism]] is often doing more work than data. The dose for benefit is narrow and individual, and chronic stress without recovery is harmful rather than strengthening, as with [[Allostatic Load]].

Engineers apply the same logic to systems. [[Chaos Engineering]] injects small, controlled failures so systems and teams develop resilience before a large one arrives, and [[Fuzzing]] uses a stream of malformed input to harden software.
