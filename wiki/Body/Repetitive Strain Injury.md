**Repetitive strain injury (RSI)** is an umbrella term for pain and dysfunction in muscles, tendons, and nerves caused by repeated movements or sustained postures. It most often affects the hands, wrists, forearms, elbows, neck, and shoulders, and is common among people who type, use a mouse, play instruments, work assembly lines, or play sports with repeated motions.

The term covers several specific conditions and some vaguer ones:

- **Carpal tunnel syndrome.** Compression of the median nerve where it passes through a narrow channel in the wrist, causing numbness and tingling in the thumb and first fingers, often worse at night.
- **Tendinopathy.** Painful, degenerated tendons, such as tennis elbow on the outside of the elbow or de Quervain's tenosynovitis at the base of the thumb. See [[Tendon and Ligament]].
- **Trigger finger**, where a finger catches or locks when bent.
- **Non-specific arm pain**, aching and fatigue without a clear structural cause, which is common and sometimes the most persistent.

The underlying mechanism is load exceeding the tissue's capacity to recover. Tissues adapt to load, as in [[Supercompensation]], but repetitive work with too little recovery accumulates small damage faster than repair can keep up. The same mismatch between acute and habitual load appears in sports injuries, tracked through [[Training Load]] ratios. Risk factors include high force, high repetition, awkward or static postures, vibration, cold, and lack of breaks.

Psychology matters too. Job stress, high workload, and low control are consistently linked to RSI. In the 1980s, Australia experienced an "RSI epidemic" among keyboard workers, with reports surging and then declining, prompting debate about how much was physical injury, how much heightened awareness, and how much workplace conditions. Today, many chronic cases are understood to involve [[Central Sensitization]], in which the nervous system amplifies pain signals beyond what the tissue state alone explains.

Prevention and management:

- **Breaks and variety.** Short, frequent pauses beat occasional long ones. Timers like the [[Pomodoro Technique]] double as reminders to move.
- **Neutral posture.** Wrists straight rather than bent up or sideways, screens at eye height, and elbows near the body. Split and tented keyboards, vertical mice, and alternating input devices help some people.
- **Reducing force and repetition.** Keyboard shortcuts in place of mouse travel, as in [[Keyboard Navigation]], and voice dictation built on [[Automatic Speech Recognition]] can offload strained hands.
- **Strength and conditioning.** Gradual loading builds tissue capacity better than rest alone.
- **Early attention.** Symptoms caught in the first weeks usually respond to changes in work habits; ignored for months, they become harder to resolve.

Wrist splints at night help carpal tunnel symptoms, and surgery to release the carpal ligament is effective for severe cases. Most other forms improve with load management and time.
