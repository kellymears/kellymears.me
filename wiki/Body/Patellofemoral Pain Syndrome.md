**Patellofemoral pain syndrome**, often called **runner's knee**, is pain around or behind the kneecap. It comes from the joint where the kneecap, or patella, glides in a groove at the end of the thighbone. It's one of the most common causes of knee pain in runners, cyclists, and active teenagers.

The pain is typically a dull ache at the front of the knee, hard to pin to one spot. It worsens with activities that load the joint while the knee is bent: climbing or especially descending stairs, squatting, kneeling, running downhill, and jumping. Sitting for a long time with the knees bent, as in a cinema or on a flight, often makes it ache.

The underlying cause is load that exceeds what the joint's tissues can tolerate. Forces on the kneecap rise steeply as the knee bends, reaching several times body weight in a deep squat. A sudden increase in running mileage or hills, a new training block, or a change in surface often precedes symptoms; see [[Training Load]]. Weakness in the hip muscles that control the thigh's rotation and in the quadriceps is commonly found in people with the condition, though whether it causes the pain or results from it isn't settled; see [[Strength Training]].

For decades, the leading explanation was that the kneecap tracks off-center in its groove, and treatments aimed to correct that. Imaging studies have found maltracking in some patients but not most, and many people with unusual tracking have no pain. The current view treats it as a problem of tissue tolerance, with pain sensitivity playing a role in persistent cases; see [[Central Sensitization]] and [[Nociception]].

Exercise therapy has the strongest evidence. Programs that strengthen the hips and knees, starting with positions that don't provoke pain and progressing gradually, reduce pain for most people within a few months. Temporarily reducing running volume, avoiding deep loaded knee bends, and taping the kneecap can ease symptoms while strength builds. Recovery is often slower than expected, and a sizable share of people still have some symptoms years later.

For cyclists, a saddle set too low increases knee bend at the top of the pedal stroke and with it the load on the kneecap; see [[Bike Fit]]. Pushing big gears at a low cadence adds force per stroke; see [[Cadence]].

The condition overlaps with other overuse knee problems. Pain on the outside of the knee in runners usually points to a different structure; see [[Iliotibial Band Syndrome]]. Pain below the kneecap, at the tendon, suggests a tendon problem; see [[Tendinopathy]]. In children, knee pain is sometimes referred from the hip; see [[Referred Pain]]. Long-term, there's some evidence linking persistent kneecap pain to later arthritis in the same joint; see [[Osteoarthritis]].
