**Central sensitization** is a state in which the central nervous system amplifies pain signals, so that pain becomes larger, longer-lasting, and more widespread than any injury in the tissues would explain. The pain is real, but its source has shifted from the body part to the nervous system processing it. Clifford Woolf described the mechanism in 1983, showing in rats that repeated painful input made spinal cord neurons progressively more responsive.

Normally, [[Nociception]] works like an alarm: tissue damage sends signals up the spinal cord, and the brain interprets them as pain proportional to the threat. After intense or prolonged input, the system can recalibrate. Spinal neurons fire more readily and for longer (*wind-up*), signals from nearby areas spread, and the brain's descending pathways that normally dampen pain weaken. Two hallmark symptoms follow:

- **Hyperalgesia.** Painful stimuli hurt more than they should.
- **Allodynia.** Things that shouldn't hurt at all, such as clothing, light touch, or a cool breeze, become painful.

Some sensitization after injury is protective, encouraging rest while tissue heals. The problem is when it persists after healing. The classification now calls this *nociplastic pain*, distinct from pain caused by tissue damage or nerve damage. Central sensitization is thought to contribute to fibromyalgia, many cases of chronic low back pain, migraine, irritable bowel syndrome, and pain that continues long after surgery or a fracture has healed, including complex regional pain syndrome.

It changes treatment. If the volume is turned up in the nervous system, treating the tissue alone helps little. Helpful approaches include graded activity that gradually re-exposes the body to movement, pain education that explains the mechanism, which can itself reduce pain, sleep improvement, and drugs that act on pain processing, such as some antidepressants and gabapentinoids. Opioids, by contrast, can worsen the picture over time through opioid-induced hyperalgesia and [[Physical Dependence]], which is one reason the [[Analgesic Ladder]] fits acute and cancer pain better than chronic pain. Preventing sensitization is one argument for controlling acute pain well, for instance with a [[Regional Nerve Block]] during surgery.

Expectation and attention also modulate pain, as [[Predictive Processing]] and the [[Nocebo Effect]] describe. Fear of movement can sustain pain, and reassurance can reduce it. [[Referred Pain]] shows a related principle: the brain can misattribute where a signal comes from. Many people with chronic pain also report heightened sensitivity to light and sound, overlapping with [[Sensory Overload]].
