**Circadian lighting** is lighting designed around its effect on the body clock as well as on vision. It rests on a discovery from around 2000: besides rods and cones, the retina contains a third kind of photoreceptor, a small population of **intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells** (ipRGCs) containing the pigment **melanopsin**. These cells contribute little to image-forming sight. Their main job is to report ambient light levels to the brain's master clock, the suprachiasmatic nucleus, which uses them to set the [[Circadian Rhythm]], suppress melatonin, and regulate alertness and pupil size.

Melanopsin is most sensitive to short-wavelength blue-cyan light, peaking around 480 nm. Three practical consequences follow:

- **Brightness dominates.** Daylight delivers tens of thousands of lux outdoors, while a typical home is in the low hundreds. The clock reads that gap as day versus night. Too little bright light in the morning weakens the daily signal as much as too much light at night disrupts it.
- **Color matters at the margin.** For the same brightness, cool, blue-rich light (high [[Color Temperature]]) stimulates the clock more than warm amber light. Warm, dim evening light is the gentlest.
- **Timing determines direction.** Light in the biological morning advances the clock, making people sleepier earlier. Light in the late evening delays it.

Lighting standards now measure this directly. The CIE's 2018 standard defines *melanopic equivalent daylight illuminance*, a lux figure weighted by melanopsin's sensitivity rather than the cones', and recommendations suggest high melanopic light during the day and very low levels in the hours before sleep.

Popular advice compresses this into "blue light is bad", which overstates the case. Screen night modes shift color but leave brightness, which matters more, mostly unchanged, and their measured effects on sleep are small. Engagement with what is on the screen is probably a larger factor, the problem in [[Doomscrolling]]. The larger gains are bright days and dim, warm evenings. Smart lighting can automate a warm-at-night schedule, a [[Defensive Default]] for a clock that [[Segmented Sleep]] history shows was set by firelight for most of human existence.
