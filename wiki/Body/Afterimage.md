An **afterimage** is an image that persists in vision after the original stimulus has disappeared. Stare at a bright window and then look at a blank wall, and a dark window-shaped patch appears. Stare at a red dot and then at white paper, and a greenish dot floats there. The afterimage follows the eyes wherever they move, because it lives in the visual system, not in the world.

Afterimages come in two kinds. A **positive afterimage** keeps the original's brightness and color for a moment, as after a camera flash. A **negative afterimage** reverses them, showing dark for light and complementary colors for the original hues. Negative afterimages are the more striking and the more informative.

They arise from adaptation. Photoreceptors in the retina that are stimulated continuously become less responsive, partly because their light-sensitive pigments are used up faster than they're replenished. When the gaze shifts to a neutral surface, the tired receptors respond weakly while the fresh ones respond normally, so the balance tips toward the unstimulated colors; see [[Dark Adaptation]] and [[Habituation]].

The particular colors that appear reveal how the brain encodes color. After red comes green, after blue comes yellow, and after black comes white. In the 1870s, the physiologist Ewald Hering proposed that color is processed in opposing pairs, red against green, blue against yellow, light against dark, and afterimages were key evidence; see [[Opponent Process Theory]]. Writers had long been fascinated by colored afterimages; the German poet Goethe described them at length in an 1810 book on color.

Afterimages behave in revealing ways:

- **Size depends on distance.** Projected onto a nearby surface, an afterimage looks small; onto a distant wall, large. The retinal image stays the same, but the brain scales it by where it seems to be, a relationship known since the 1880s.
- **Blinking refreshes them.** Afterimages can fade and return as the eyes close and open.
- **Illusions use them.** A flag printed in complementary colors, stared at for half a minute, produces an afterimage in its correct colors on a white page.

Related aftereffects exist for motion. After watching a waterfall, stationary rocks seem to drift upward, as motion-sensing neurons adapt; see [[Apparent Motion]]. One unusual aftereffect, linking color to line orientation, can last for hours or even months after a few minutes of exposure.

Afterimages are a reminder that perception is relative. Colors are judged against what surrounds them and what came just before, and the same light can look different in different contexts; see [[Predictive Processing]] and [[Metamerism]]. Prolonged screen work produces its own visual fatigue, though of a different kind; see [[Digital Eye Strain]]. People with altered color vision see different afterimages; see [[Color Vision Deficiency]].
