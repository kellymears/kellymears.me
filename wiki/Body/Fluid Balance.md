**Fluid balance** is the regulation of the body's water and the salts dissolved in it. Water makes up roughly 60 percent of an adult's body mass. The body tracks not the volume of water directly but its concentration: when the blood grows too salty, the brain triggers thirst and releases antidiuretic hormone, which tells the kidneys to hold on to water. When the blood grows too dilute, thirst fades and the kidneys release more. It is one of the clearest examples of [[Homeostasis]].

Sodium is the main electrolyte in the fluid outside cells, and water follows it by [[Osmosis]]. Shifts in sodium concentration move water into or out of cells, which matters most in the brain, enclosed by the skull with little room to swell.

Exercise strains the system. Sweat cools the body by evaporation, part of [[Thermoregulation]], and rates range from about half a liter to more than two liters an hour, depending on heat, intensity, and the individual. Sweat sodium varies severalfold between people, which is why some athletes finish rides crusted in white salt and others do not. [[Heat Acclimation]] raises sweat rate and makes sweat less salty.

**Dehydration** costs performance, though less than marketing implies. Losing around 2 percent of body mass raises heart rate and perceived effort, adds to the upward creep in heart rate during long efforts, and hurts performance in the heat. Thirst usually appears around that level. Plasma volume losses also explain part of the early fitness drop in [[Detraining]]. Elite marathoners often finish several percent lighter, yet win.

The more dangerous error is the reverse. **Hyponatremia**, low blood sodium, happens when someone drinks much more than they lose, diluting the blood. Water moves into brain cells, causing confusion, seizures, and occasionally death. A study of the 2002 Boston Marathon found 13 percent of finishers hyponatremic, with slower runners who gained weight during the race at highest risk. The guidance has since shifted from "drink as much as possible" to *drink to thirst*, with sodium for very long events.

Several popular beliefs do not hold up:

- **Eight glasses a day.** It traces to a 1945 recommendation that also noted most of the water comes from food. Coffee and tea count too.
- **Cramps are dehydration.** The evidence is weak, as [[Muscle Cramp]] discusses.
- **Urine must be clear.** Pale straw color is enough, and clear can mean overdrinking.

Water also travels with fuel. Each gram of stored [[Glycogen]] binds about three grams of water, which is why carb loading adds weight and why early weight loss on a low-carbohydrate diet is mostly water. [[Altitude Training]] adds losses through dry air and faster breathing, and thirst itself is a form of [[Interoception]].
