**Collagen** is the most abundant protein in the human body, making up roughly a quarter to a third of all protein by weight. It is the structural cable of connective tissue, the reason skin resists tearing, tendons can transmit the pull of muscles, and bones bend slightly instead of shattering.

Its structure is unusually regular. Three protein chains wind around one another into a stiff **triple helix**. Every third amino acid in each chain is glycine, the smallest one, which is the only amino acid that fits in the crowded center of the helix. Many of the others are proline and hydroxyproline. The helices bundle into fibrils, the fibrils into fibers, and cross-links between them add strength.

There are more than two dozen types. **Type I** dominates skin, tendon, ligament and bone; **type II** forms cartilage; **type III** supports blood vessels and fresh scar tissue. Bone is essentially a collagen scaffold stiffened by calcium phosphate mineral; see [[Bone Remodeling]] and [[Osteoporosis]].

Building collagen requires **vitamin C**. An enzyme needs it to convert proline into hydroxyproline, without which the helix is unstable. That chemistry explains **scurvy**, the disease of sailors on long voyages. As collagen wears out and cannot be properly replaced, gums bleed, teeth loosen, bruises spread and even old wounds reopen. In 1747 the naval surgeon James Lind gave pairs of scurvy-stricken sailors different remedies and found that those eating citrus recovered fastest, one of the earliest controlled trials, though its lesson took decades to be adopted; see [[Randomized Controlled Trial]].

Collagen turns over slowly in dense tissues, which is why tendons and ligaments adapt and heal over months rather than weeks; see [[Tendon and Ligament]] and [[Tendinopathy]]. When skin or other tissue heals, new collagen is laid down in a disorganized pattern that never fully regains the original strength; see [[Scar Tissue]].

Aging stiffens and thins collagen. Production declines, and sugar molecules form extra cross-links that make tissues less elastic. Ultraviolet light speeds the breakdown in skin, a major cause of wrinkles; see [[Sunburn]]. Topical vitamin A derivatives are among the few treatments shown to stimulate new collagen in sun-damaged skin; see [[Retinoid]]. Inherited defects in collagen or the enzymes that process it cause conditions including the Ehlers-Danlos syndromes; see [[Hypermobility]].

Heated, collagen unwinds into gelatin, which is why slow-cooked tough cuts of meat turn tender and broth sets when cooled; see [[Protein Denaturation]]. Collagen supplements are digested into amino acids and small peptides like any other protein. Some studies report modest benefits for joint pain or skin, while others find none, and the evidence remains limited; see [[Muscle Protein Synthesis]].
