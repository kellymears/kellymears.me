**Physical dependence** is a state in which a body has adapted to the sustained presence of a drug, such that removing it produces a withdrawal syndrome. It travels with **tolerance**, the need for more drug to produce the same effect, and both are ordinary pharmacology. Neither is the same as addiction — compulsive use continuing despite harm — and conflating them is the common error here. Patients on stable long-term therapy are dependent without being addicted; some highly compulsive patterns involve little physical dependence at all.

Severity and danger separate too. Opioid withdrawal is intensely unpleasant and rarely lethal; alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal can kill through seizure and delirium and may need supervised tapering.

Receptor pharmacology explains the rest. A **full agonist** such as morphine gives more effect with more occupancy. A **partial agonist** such as buprenorphine has a ceiling — beyond a point more drug produces no more effect, particularly respiratory depression — while still binding tightly enough to displace other agents. An **antagonist** such as naloxone occupies the receptor and produces nothing. Effects are dose- and route-dependent throughout, and clearance varies with [[Xenobiotic Metabolism]], so half-life shapes withdrawal onset and overdose risk alike.

Folk comparisons — "X is basically the same as Y" — collapse these axes into a single scale of badness, ignoring dose, potency, route, half-life, receptor behaviour and purpose. The trouble is categorical: dependence, addiction, harm and legality get treated as one prototype rather than independent dimensions, which is what [[Prototype Theory]] and any careful [[Taxonomy]] warn against, and what a demand for a [[Plausible Mechanism]] would catch. Analgesia after injury sits squarely in this space, alongside [[Nociception]] and [[Regional Nerve Block]].
