**Intermittent fasting** describes eating patterns that alternate periods of eating with periods of fasting. Rather than changing what people eat, it changes when. The approach became one of the most popular diets of the 2010s, promoted for weight loss, metabolic health, and longevity.

The main forms are:

- **Time-restricted eating.** All food is eaten within a daily window, commonly 8 hours, with 16 hours of fasting.
- **Alternate-day fasting.** Fasting, or eating very little, every other day.
- **The 5:2 pattern.** Eating normally five days a week and eating around 500 to 600 calories on the other two.

Proponents point to several mechanisms. After about 12 hours without food, the body has used much of its stored carbohydrate and shifts toward burning fat, producing ketones; see [[Glycogen]] and [[Metabolic Flexibility]]. Lower insulin levels between meals may improve how cells respond to it; see [[Insulin Sensitivity]]. Fasting triggers cellular cleanup processes, and the stress of fasting may prompt protective responses in cells; see [[Hormesis]]. Animal studies showed that restricting feeding extended lifespan in some species and improved metabolic markers in mice.

Human trials have been less dramatic. In randomized trials comparing intermittent fasting with ordinary calorie restriction that cuts the same total amount, weight loss is generally similar; see [[Randomized Controlled Trial]]. For many people, the main effect of fasting seems to be that it makes eating less easier, by removing late-night snacking or simplifying decisions. A 2020 trial of 16:8 time-restricted eating found little weight loss compared with a control group, and a modest loss of lean mass.

Timing may still matter. The body handles food differently across the day, and glucose tolerance tends to be better in the morning; see [[Circadian Rhythm]]. Some studies suggest that eating earlier in the day improves blood sugar and blood pressure even without weight loss. Late-night eating, common among shift workers, is associated with metabolic problems; see [[Chronotype]].

Concerns include:

- **Muscle loss.** Long fasts and low protein can reduce lean mass, especially in older adults; see [[Muscle Protein Synthesis]] and [[Sarcopenia]].
- **Athletes.** Training while underfueled can impair performance and health; see [[Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport]].
- **Eating disorders.** Strict rules about when to eat may worsen disordered eating in vulnerable people.
- **Hunger.** Some people adapt quickly, while others find fasting days miserable; see [[Appetite Regulation]].

Intermittent fasting suits some people because it gives them a clear rule to follow, and diets seem to succeed mostly when people can stick with them; see [[Habit Formation]]. The body's energy use adapts during weight loss, slowing results; see [[Basal Metabolic Rate]].
