A **hangover** is the cluster of symptoms that follow heavy drinking, including headache, fatigue, nausea, thirst, sensitivity to light and sound, poor concentration, and low mood. It peaks around the time blood alcohol returns to zero, typically the morning after, and fades within about a day. The medical term is veisalgia.

What causes it is less settled than popular advice suggests:

- **Dehydration.** Alcohol suppresses the hormone vasopressin, so the kidneys release more water, and drinkers are often thirsty and dehydrated the next day. But studies find that hangover severity correlates poorly with markers of dehydration and electrolytes, so it is now seen as a contributor rather than the main cause; see [[Fluid Balance]].
- **Acetaldehyde.** The liver breaks alcohol into acetaldehyde, which is toxic, before breaking it down further; see [[Xenobiotic Metabolism]]. People with a common genetic variant, frequent in East Asian populations, clear it slowly and flush and feel ill after small amounts. Its role in ordinary hangovers is debated, since symptoms peak after levels have fallen.
- **Inflammation.** Hangovers involve raised levels of immune signaling molecules, which produce the same malaise, aches, and poor concentration as an infection; see [[Inflammation]] and [[Immune System]].
- **Disrupted sleep.** Alcohol helps people fall asleep but suppresses REM sleep early in the night and fragments sleep later, so drinkers wake unrested; see [[Sleep Stages]] and [[Sleep Deprivation]].
- **Low blood sugar** and stomach irritation, which add to nausea and weakness.
- **Congeners.** Other substances produced in fermentation and aging, more abundant in dark spirits and red wine; see [[Fermentation]]. A 2010 study found bourbon produced worse hangovers than the same amount of vodka, though less difference in performance.

Hangover is distinct from withdrawal, but the two overlap. Drinking more the next morning, "hair of the dog," postpones symptoms by restarting the cycle, and regular use leads toward dependence; see [[Physical Dependence]].

No remedy has been proven. Systematic reviews of remedies such as artichoke, prickly pear, and various supplements have found the evidence weak or of poor quality. What helps is mostly time: water, food, sleep, and simple painkillers. Ibuprofen and aspirin can irritate a stomach already inflamed by alcohol. Acetaminophen is broken down by the same liver pathways and is best avoided while alcohol is still being processed; see [[Analgesic Ladder]].

Hangovers carry real costs. Studies find impaired attention, memory, and reaction time the morning after, even with no alcohol left in the blood, which affects driving and work.

Prevention is drinking less, slowly, with food, and alternating with water. Expecting to feel awful may also make it worse; see [[Nocebo Effect]].

Alcohol also disturbs the body's daily rhythm and appetite signals; see [[Circadian Rhythm]] and [[Appetite Regulation]].
