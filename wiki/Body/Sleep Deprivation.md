**Sleep deprivation** is getting less sleep than the body needs. It comes in two forms: **acute**, as in a night without sleep, and **chronic**, as in weeks of sleeping an hour or two less than needed. The second is far more common and, in daily life, often more harmful, partly because people adapt to feeling worse and stop noticing.

Most adults need about seven to nine hours. The need is driven by **sleep pressure**, a chemical drive that builds during waking hours and is cleared during sleep, interacting with the body's daily clock; see [[Sleep Pressure]] and [[Circadian Rhythm]]. Sleep that's cut short loses disproportionate amounts of some stages: deep sleep tends to come early in the night, while REM sleep is concentrated toward morning, so an early alarm cuts REM most; see [[Sleep Stages]].

The effects are broad:

- **Attention and performance.** Reaction time slows and attention lapses increase. After about 17–19 hours awake, performance on some tasks resembles that at a blood alcohol level of 0.05%; see [[Vigilance Decrement]].
- **Memory and learning.** Sleep consolidates what was learned during the day; without it, retention suffers; see [[Memory Consolidation]].
- **Mood and emotion.** Sleep loss amplifies emotional reactions and makes negative events feel worse; see [[Emotional Regulation]].
- **Metabolism.** Even a few nights of restricted sleep reduce [[Insulin Sensitivity]] and increase hunger hormones, raising appetite especially for energy-dense food; see [[Appetite Regulation]].
- **Immunity.** People who sleep less are more likely to catch a cold after exposure to a virus; see [[Immune System]].
- **Training.** Sleep loss impairs endurance, increases perceived effort, slows recovery, and raises injury risk; see [[Supercompensation]].

A key finding concerns self-assessment. In a 2003 study by Hans Van Dongen and colleagues, people restricted to six hours a night for two weeks declined steadily on attention tests until they performed as badly as people who'd been kept awake for one or two full nights. Yet their ratings of their own sleepiness leveled off after a few days. They felt only slightly tired while performing much worse.

Can sleep debt be repaid? Partly. Recovery sleep restores alertness, but a single long weekend sleep doesn't fully reverse the effects of a week of short nights, and the metabolic costs of irregular schedules seem to accumulate. Consistent timing matters along with total duration; see [[Jet Lag]].

Short naps help with alertness, and caffeine masks sleep pressure by blocking its signal, but neither replaces sleep. Light exposure in the evening can delay sleep onset by shifting the clock later; see [[Circadian Lighting]].

A small number of people carry gene variants that let them function well on about six hours, but they're rare. Most people who believe they need little sleep are simply used to feeling tired.
