**Plyometrics** is training that uses fast, explosive movements in which a muscle is quickly stretched and then immediately contracts. Jumping, bounding, hopping, skipping and medicine-ball throws are the usual tools. The aim is power, the ability to produce force quickly, rather than maximal strength.

The mechanism is the **stretch-shortening cycle**, which happens in almost every natural movement. When a runner's foot lands, the calf muscles and Achilles tendon are stretched under load. Tendons are springy, so they store some of the landing energy and give it back in the push-off, the way a pogo stick does; see [[Elasticity]] and [[Tendon and Ligament]]. The quick stretch also triggers a reflex that adds muscle activation. A countermovement jump, where you dip before jumping, goes noticeably higher than a jump from a held squat for these reasons.

The method's modern form is usually traced to the Soviet sports scientist Yuri Verkhoshansky, who in the 1960s developed depth jumps for track and field athletes: stepping off a box and rebounding upward as fast as possible on landing. The English term was popularized in the 1970s by the American coach Fred Wilt.

Plyometric training improves jump height and sprint speed in many studies, and it can improve running economy, the energy cost of holding a given pace, likely by stiffening the springs of the lower leg; see [[Exercise Economy]]. It trains the nervous system to recruit muscle quickly, and it draws heavily on the fast-twitch fibers that dominate explosive movements; see [[Muscle Fiber Types]]. Jump-landing programs that teach athletes to land softly with knees aligned have been shown to reduce knee ligament injuries in some sports, partly through better body awareness; see [[Proprioception]].

The cost is impact. Landing from a jump can produce forces of several times body weight, and repeated high-volume jumping can irritate tendons and bones; see [[Tendinopathy]] and [[Stress Fracture]]. Coaches count foot contacts per session and progress from low hops and skipping to bounding and depth jumps over weeks, the same gradual loading principle as any training; see [[Training Load]]. Plyometrics usually comes after a thorough warm-up, early in a session before fatigue spoils technique; see [[Warm-Up]].

New plyometric work tends to cause marked soreness in the days after, because so much of it loads muscles as they lengthen; see [[Delayed Onset Muscle Soreness]]. A base of strength helps, and plyometrics pairs naturally with heavy lifting in programs for power sports; see [[Strength Training]].

Jumping also loads bone in a way that encourages it to grow stronger, which is why impact exercise is recommended to protect bone density; see [[Bone Remodeling]] and [[Osteoporosis]].
