**Glycogen** is the body's storage form of glucose: branched chains of glucose molecules packed into muscle and liver cells. It is the fast-access fuel for hard exercise. Fat stores are vast by comparison, even a lean person carries tens of thousands of calories of it, but fat is oxidized slowly. Above moderate intensity, the body relies increasingly on carbohydrate, and that supply is small.

A typical adult stores roughly 400 to 500 grams of glycogen in muscle and about 100 grams in the liver, together around 1,600 to 2,000 kilocalories. That is enough for perhaps ninety minutes of hard effort. The two stores behave differently. Muscle glycogen fuels only the muscle that holds it, since muscle cells lack the enzyme needed to release glucose into the blood. Liver glycogen keeps blood glucose stable for everything else, including the brain.

**Bonking**, also called hitting the wall, is what happens when the stores run out. It arrives suddenly, not gradually. Power collapses, legs feel empty, and because blood glucose falls, the brain is affected too, bringing confusion, irritability, tearfulness, and poor decisions. Marathoners famously meet it around 30 kilometers, and long rides have their own version. It is preventable. Earlier warning signs are easy to miss, especially for people with weak [[Interoception]] or in [[Flow]], and the fix is to eat before feeling hungry.

The modern fueling rules come from that:

- **Eat during long efforts.** Thirty to sixty grams of carbohydrate per hour for moderate sessions, and up to 90 or more for racing.
- **Mix sugars.** Glucose and fructose cross the gut through different transporters, so a mix raises how much can be absorbed per hour beyond the ceiling for glucose alone.
- **Load before big events.** Carbohydrate loading, from Bergström's 1960s work, tops muscle glycogen up above normal before an event, the original case of [[Supercompensation]].
- **Train the gut.** Absorbing high carbohydrate rates while exercising is itself trainable.

Glycogen also binds water, about three grams per gram, which is why weight swings by a kilogram or two with carbohydrate intake and why loading makes people feel heavy. The rise in that weight is fuel, not fat.
