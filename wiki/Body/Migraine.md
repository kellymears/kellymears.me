A **migraine** is an attack of a neurological disorder, most recognizable as a moderate to severe headache, often on one side and throbbing, that worsens with movement and comes with nausea and sensitivity to light and sound. Attacks typically last from four hours to three days. Migraine is far more than a bad headache: it affects roughly one in seven people worldwide, is about three times more common in women than in men, and ranks among the leading causes of disability in people under fifty.

An attack often unfolds in phases. A premonitory phase, hours or a day before, can bring yawning, food cravings, irritability, or a stiff neck; see [[Yawning]]. About a quarter to a third of people with migraine experience an **aura**, most often visual: a flickering, zigzag arc of light that slowly expands across the visual field over twenty minutes or so, leaving a blind patch behind it; see [[Blind Spot]]. Then comes the headache, and finally a drained, hungover period sometimes called the postdrome; see [[Hangover]].

The aura has a known physical correlate. **Cortical spreading depression**, first described by the Brazilian physiologist Aristides Leão in 1944, is a slow wave of electrical activity followed by silence that creeps across the brain's surface at a few millimeters per minute. Its speed across the visual cortex matches how the aura spreads across vision. The pain itself involves the trigeminal nerve, which senses the blood vessels and membranes around the brain, and a signaling molecule called CGRP that it releases; see [[Nociception]].

That discovery changed treatment. Triptans, introduced in the 1990s, act on serotonin receptors to block the pain pathway. Drugs targeting CGRP or its receptor, approved from 2018, were the first designed specifically to prevent migraine. Ordinary painkillers help many attacks, but using them too often can cause a rebound medication-overuse headache; see [[Analgesic Ladder]] and [[Physical Dependence]].

Common triggers include changes in sleep, skipped meals, stress and the letdown after it, hormonal shifts, and alcohol; see [[Sleep Deprivation]]. Caffeine both relieves attacks and, when withdrawn, provokes them; see [[Sleep Pressure]]. Triggers are hard to identify reliably, because early symptoms of an attack can look like causes. A craving for chocolate in the premonitory phase gets blamed for the headache that was already on its way; see [[Correlation and Causation]].

Migraine brains seem generally more sensitive to sensory input, even between attacks; see [[Sensory Overload]]. Some people experience vestibular migraine, with dizziness rather than headache; see [[Vertigo]]. People with migraine are also more prone to motion sickness and to the sharp pain of eating something cold; see [[Motion Sickness]] and [[Brain Freeze]].
