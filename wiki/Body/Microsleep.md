**A microsleep** is a short burst of sleep, lasting from a fraction of a second to about fifteen seconds, that intrudes on wakefulness. The person may seem awake, sometimes with eyes open, but stops responding to what's around them. Afterward they often don't realize anything happened, and may only notice a missed sentence, a jolt of the head, or a gap in a conversation. Brain recordings show sleep-like activity during these episodes.

Microsleeps are a consequence of building sleep pressure. The longer a person stays awake, or the more sleep they've lost over recent nights, the more often these episodes break through; see [[Sleep Pressure]] and [[Sleep Deprivation]]. They're also more likely:

- at the low points of the body clock, especially the early morning hours and the mid-afternoon dip; see [[Circadian Rhythm]];
- after crossing time zones or working night shifts; see [[Jet Lag]];
- with untreated sleep disorders; see [[Sleep Apnea]];
- during monotonous tasks, like highway driving, watching a screen, or monitoring an automated system.

Driving is where they're most dangerous. A few seconds of microsleep at highway speed means traveling the length of a sports field with no one in control. Drowsy driving causes a significant share of crashes, particularly single-vehicle accidents in which the car drifts off the road without any sign of braking. Opening a window or turning up the radio don't reliably help. The effective countermeasures are pulling over to nap or, better, getting enough sleep in the first place; see [[Napping]].

Laboratory studies measure lapses with the psychomotor vigilance task, in which people press a button whenever a light appears at random intervals. Sleep-deprived people show more slow responses and outright misses, along with greater variability, even when their average speed looks fine; see [[Reaction Time]] and [[Vigilance Decrement]].

Research on animals suggests sleep isn't always all or nothing. In a 2011 study, sleep-deprived rats that seemed fully awake had individual groups of neurons briefly go offline in a sleep-like state, and those moments coincided with mistakes on a task. Researchers call this local sleep and suspect it happens in people too.

A particular danger, borne out in repeated laboratory studies, is that people are poor judges of their own sleepiness. Sleep-deprived people tend to underestimate how impaired they are, and they may think they're alert right up to a lapse; see [[Metacognition]].

Safety-critical industries treat fatigue as a hazard, regulating hours for pilots and truck drivers. Automation can make matters worse, since watching a system that rarely needs intervention is exactly the kind of monotony that invites lapses; see [[Automation Bias]] and [[Inattentional Blindness]].
