The **central governor theory** proposes that the brain regulates exercise intensity to keep the body from reaching dangerous failure, and that fatigue is a sensation it generates to enforce that limit rather than a direct readout of exhausted muscle. The South African physiologist Tim Noakes developed it from the late 1990s, building on a 1920s suggestion by Archibald Hill that something must protect the heart during maximal effort.

The older model held that exercise stops when muscles run out of fuel or oxygen, or are flooded with metabolites, a *peripheral* limit. Several observations sit poorly with that:

- **The end spurt.** Runners and cyclists often speed up in the final minutes of a race. If muscles were truly at their limit, there would be nothing left to spend.
- **Reserve muscle.** Even at exhaustion, the brain recruits only part of the available motor units. Muscle stimulated electrically can still produce more force.
- **Anticipatory pacing.** In hot conditions, athletes slow down from the start, before core temperature has risen, as if pacing for a predicted cost. Deceiving people about distance or time changes pacing too.
- **Carbohydrate mouth rinse.** Swishing a sports drink and spitting it out improves performance in hour-long efforts, before any sugar is absorbed. The brain seems to respond to the signal of incoming fuel.

In this picture, pace is set by a prediction of how much can be safely spent, updated by signals from muscles, temperature, fuel stores, and the remaining distance. That makes it a close cousin of [[Predictive Processing]] and depends heavily on [[Interoception]]. [[Glycogen]] depletion, high temperature under [[Thermoregulation]], and low oxygen at altitude all reduce pace before they become dangerous. [[Altitude Training]] shows the same throttling.

The theory has strong critics. Samuele Marcora's **psychobiological model** argues that no subconscious governor is needed. People stop or slow when the perceived effort reaches the most they are willing to bear, or when it feels impossible to continue. In 2009 Marcora showed that 90 minutes of a demanding mental task before a cycling test raised perceived effort and shortened time to exhaustion, with no change in the muscles or heart. That ties endurance to [[Decision Fatigue]] and motivation.

Perceived effort is measured with Gunnar Borg's 6–20 scale, designed in the 1970s so that rating times ten roughly tracks heart rate in young adults. It remains one of the most useful training tools, alongside [[Heart Rate Zones]] and [[Functional Threshold Power]].

Both views agree that the brain shapes endurance and that a felt limit is not the true limit. That explains why music, rivals, crowds, and [[Placebo Effect]] interventions improve performance, and why pacing well is a learned skill. The engineering analogy is a [[Circuit Breaker]], which trips before damage rather than after it.
