**Supercompensation** is the textbook model of how training works. A workout is a stress that temporarily *lowers* capacity: muscles are damaged, [[Glycogen]] is depleted, and the nervous system is fatigued. During recovery the body does not just restore the previous level. It overshoots, rebuilding a little above the old baseline. Apply the next stress near the top of that overshoot and fitness climbs in steps. Apply it too early and fatigue stacks up. Apply it too late and the gain fades back.

The idea traces partly to Bergström and Hultman's 1960s finding that muscle glycogen, after being depleted by exercise, refills to *higher* than before, which was literally a supercompensation. Generalized to all adaptation, it gives the core rules of training:

- **Progressive overload.** The stress has to keep rising, because yesterday's load no longer disturbs today's body.
- **Adaptation happens during rest.** Sleep, food, and easy days are where fitness is built. A hard session only signals for it. [[Circadian Rhythm]] disruption and short sleep blunt recovery measurably.
- **Tissues adapt at different speeds.** The cardiovascular system responds within weeks, but [[Tendon and Ligament|tendons]] and bone, through [[Bone Remodeling]], take months. A fast rise in aerobic fitness can outpace the connective tissue, which is a classic route to overuse injury.

Things go wrong in a predictable sequence. *Functional overreaching* is a deliberate short block of excess load that, followed by rest, produces a rebound. *Non-functional overreaching* is when performance stays down for weeks. *Overtraining syndrome* is a lasting slump in performance, mood, sleep, and immunity that can take months to resolve. It usually comes from training stress combined with life stress, since the body does not keep separate budgets.

The single-session curve is a simplification. Real training overlaps sessions long before any one has fully resolved, and the [[Training Load]] fitness-fatigue model describes that better. The practical structure is the same either way: build for a few weeks, then *deload* with a lighter week, and *taper* before a target event, so that fatigue clears faster than fitness fades.
