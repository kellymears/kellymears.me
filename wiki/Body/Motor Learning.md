**Motor learning** is the process by which practice produces lasting improvement in skilled movement, such as riding a bike, typing, or landing a jump in a game. Despite the common phrase "muscle memory," the learning lives in the nervous system: the motor cortex, the basal ganglia, and especially the cerebellum, which compares intended movements with what actually happened and corrects the error. Muscles adapt to training, but they don't store skills.

In 1967 the psychologists Paul Fitts, also known for [[Fitts's Law]], and Michael Posner described three stages of learning a skill:

- **Cognitive.** The learner thinks through each step, makes many errors, and relies heavily on [[Working Memory]] and verbal instruction.
- **Associative.** Movements become smoother and more consistent as errors are refined away.
- **Autonomous.** The skill runs automatically, freeing attention for strategy and surroundings. This is the fast, effortless mode of [[Dual Process Theory]] and the automaticity of [[Habit Formation]].

Feedback from the senses drives the process. [[Proprioception]] reports limb position and the [[Vestibular System]] reports balance, and the cerebellum uses both to refine the movement plan. Learning continues after practice ends: skills often improve after sleep without further practice, a consolidation linked to [[Sleep Stages]].

Several findings run against intuition:

- **Contextual interference.** Mixing different skills in a practice session (random practice) produces worse performance during practice but better retention and transfer than drilling one skill at a time (blocked practice). John Shea and Robyn Morgan showed this in 1979. Blocked practice feels more productive, an illusion of fluency that [[Metacognition]] easily misreads, like the advantage of [[Spaced Repetition]] over cramming.
- **External focus.** Attending to the effect of a movement, such as the ball's path, usually works better than attending to the body's mechanics. Thinking about the body tends to drag a skill back into slow, deliberate control, a mechanism behind choking under pressure.
- **Variability.** Practicing slight variations of a skill builds a more adaptable movement than repeating one version perfectly.

Games are an intensive motor-learning environment. Tight [[Game Feel]] gives the instant, consistent feedback the cerebellum learns best from. Fighting game players drill execution against [[Frame Data]], speedrunners grind segments of a [[Speedrun]] until routes run automatically, and a [[Rhythm Game]] is motor learning presented as a game. Good tutorials follow the stages, introducing one mechanic at a time and then mixing them along the [[Difficulty Curve]].
