A **concussion** is a mild traumatic brain injury caused by a blow to the head, or a jolt to the body, that makes the brain move rapidly inside the skull. "Mild" refers to its usual lack of life-threatening damage, not to how it feels. Standard scans are typically normal; the injury is functional rather than structural.

The mechanism is mostly acceleration, especially rotational. When the head turns or stops suddenly, the brain lags behind, stretching nerve fibers. Damaged cells release chemicals that disrupt their ion balance, and restoring it demands energy at a moment when blood flow to the brain is reduced, an *energy crisis* that can last days to weeks.

Symptoms vary widely and may appear hours later:

- **Physical.** Headache, nausea, dizziness, and sensitivity to light and noise.
- **Balance and vision.** Unsteadiness and trouble focusing, often from disruption to the [[Vestibular System]].
- **Cognitive.** Fogginess, slowed thinking, poor concentration and memory, strains on [[Working Memory]] and [[Executive Function]].
- **Emotional and sleep.** Irritability, anxiety, low mood, and too much or too little sleep.

Loss of consciousness happens in only a minority of concussions, so its absence rules nothing out. Anyone suspected of a concussion should stop activity immediately. Warning signs such as worsening headache, repeated vomiting, confusion, seizures, or weakness require emergency care, since they may indicate bleeding.

Recovery advice has changed. The old prescription of strict rest in a dark room has given way to a brief period of relative rest, about a day or two, followed by gradual return to light activity that does not markedly worsen symptoms. International consensus statements, most recently in 2022, describe stepwise return-to-learn and return-to-sport protocols. Most people recover within a few weeks, but a significant minority have persistent symptoms, often involving the vestibular system, neck, sleep, or [[Central Sensitization]], which respond to targeted rehabilitation. Easing back into screens, noise, and crowds helps avoid [[Sensory Overload]].

Returning to contact or crash-prone sport too soon is dangerous. A second impact before recovery can cause much worse injury, and in rare cases *second impact syndrome*, rapid brain swelling, mostly reported in adolescents. Repeated head impacts over years are linked to chronic traumatic encephalopathy.

For cyclists, helmets reduce the risk of serious head injury substantially, by roughly half or more in meta-analyses, though they protect less against concussion than against skull fracture. Helmet foam crushes to stretch out the deceleration, applying [[Newton's Laws of Motion]]: a longer stop means less force. Systems that let the shell slip slightly against the head aim to reduce rotational acceleration. A helmet that has taken an impact should be replaced, since crushed foam does not recover.

The brain's ability to recover, described under [[Neuroplasticity]], is substantial, but it needs time and graded load.
