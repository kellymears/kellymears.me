The **Valsalva maneuver** is an attempt to exhale forcefully with the airway closed, either by shutting the throat or by pinching the nose and closing the mouth. No air escapes, so pressure builds inside the chest and abdomen. People do it constantly without naming it: straining on the toilet, lifting something heavy, blowing up a stiff balloon, or popping their ears on a plane.

The maneuver is named for Antonio Maria Valsalva, an Italian anatomist who described it around 1704 in a treatise on the human ear, as a way to force air into the middle ear and push out pus. Its effects on circulation were studied later.

Raising pressure in the chest squeezes the large veins and the heart itself, and the body responds in a characteristic sequence. Blood pressure jumps briefly as the chest pushes blood out. Then, with less blood returning to the heart, output falls, blood pressure drops, and heart rate climbs to compensate; see [[Cardiac Output]] and [[Blood Pressure]]. When the breath is released, blood floods back, pressure overshoots, and the heart rate slows sharply through a reflex involving the vagus nerve; see [[Vagus Nerve]] and [[Autonomic Nervous System]].

That sequence makes it useful:

- **Ears.** Pushing air up the tube connecting the throat and middle ear equalizes pressure during descent in a plane or a dive; see [[Eustachian Tube]] and [[Mammalian Dive Reflex]].
- **Heart rhythm.** The vagal surge can stop some abnormally fast heart rhythms. A 2015 trial found that following the strain by lying back with legs raised roughly doubled the success rate over the standard technique.
- **Diagnosis.** Doctors use it to bring out certain heart murmurs and to test the nervous control of circulation; see [[Heart Rate Variability]].
- **Lifting.** Weightlifters hold a breath against a closed throat to stiffen the trunk. Pressure in the abdomen acts like an inflated cylinder supporting the spine, which helps protect it under heavy loads; see [[Strength Training]] and [[Pascal's Principle]].

The bracing comes at a price. During maximal lifts with breath held, blood pressure can briefly reach extreme levels; one study of experienced bodybuilders recorded readings above 400 over 300. For most healthy people this is harmless, but it's a concern for those with certain heart or eye conditions.

The drop in blood returning to the heart can also cause fainting. Faints while straining, coughing hard, or lifting in a hot room follow this pattern, as the brain is briefly starved of blood; see [[Syncope]].

Breathing out while lifting, and controlling breath with the diaphragm, avoids the pressure spike at the cost of some stability; see [[Diaphragmatic Breathing]]. Holding a sneeze in with the nose and mouth closed produces a sudden, uncontrolled version; see [[Sneeze]].
