**Appetite regulation** is the set of signals that decide when people feel hungry, how much they eat, and when they feel full. It mixes short-term signals about the current meal, long-term signals about stored energy, and learned habits and rewards. It's a [[Homeostasis]] system, but a loose one, and it was shaped by an environment in which food was often scarce.

**Short-term signals** govern a single meal:

- **Ghrelin**, made mainly by the stomach, rises before meals and falls after eating. It is the only known hormone that increases hunger, and its rise often follows the usual mealtime, a conditioned response.
- **Satiety hormones** from the gut, such as GLP-1, PYY, and CCK, are released as food arrives and signal fullness to the brain. Protein and fiber trigger them more strongly than refined carbohydrate. Fullness lags the first bites by roughly twenty minutes, which is why eating slowly helps.
- **Stretch.** Stomach stretch is sensed through the [[Vagus Nerve]], one reason high-volume food fills people up.

**Long-term signals** reflect energy stores. **Leptin**, discovered in 1994, is made by fat cells in proportion to fat mass. Falling leptin signals that stores are shrinking and increases hunger while lowering energy expenditure. Rising leptin should do the opposite, but in people with obesity the brain often becomes less responsive to it, much as cells become less responsive to insulin; see [[Insulin Sensitivity]].

The asymmetry matters. After weight loss, leptin falls, hunger rises, and the [[Basal Metabolic Rate]] drops more than the smaller body accounts for. The system defends against loss far more firmly than against gain, which helps explain why most diets are regained. Drugs that mimic GLP-1, such as semaglutide, work largely by strengthening satiety signals, and their success has reframed appetite as biology rather than willpower.

Several everyday factors shift the system:

- **Sleep.** Even a few nights of short sleep raise ghrelin, lower leptin, and increase cravings for energy-dense food; see [[Circadian Rhythm]].
- **Exercise.** Hard sessions often suppress appetite briefly, then raise it later. Endurance athletes can under-eat without noticing, which leads to [[Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport]].
- **Stress.** Chronic stress and [[Cortisol]] tend to shift preference toward palatable food; see [[Allostatic Load]].
- **Reward.** Highly palatable food activates reward circuits, and the [[Reward Prediction Error]] signal makes eating cues compelling, so eating becomes partly a matter of [[Habit Formation]].
- **Interoception.** Noticing hunger and fullness depends on [[Interoception]], which varies widely between people.

The [[Gut Microbiome]] may also influence appetite through metabolites it produces, though human evidence is still early.
