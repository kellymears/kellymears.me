**Frostbite** is injury from tissue freezing. It usually strikes the parts of the body farthest from its core and most exposed: fingers, toes, the nose, ears, and cheeks. Damage ranges from **frostnip**, a numb, pale patch that recovers fully on rewarming, to deep frostbite that kills skin, muscle, and bone and can end in amputation.

The body chooses to risk it. In the cold, it narrows blood vessels in the skin and extremities to keep warm blood in the trunk, where the vital organs are; see [[Thermoregulation]] and [[Cold Exposure]]. That defense against hypothermia leaves fingers and toes cooling toward the air temperature. As a partial compromise, cold hands periodically flush with blood for a few minutes, a cycle known as **cold-induced vasodilation**, which is stronger in people used to working in the cold.

Freezing damages tissue in several ways at once. Ice crystals form between cells, drawing water out of them and disrupting their membranes; see [[Phase Transition]]. Small vessels are injured, and when the tissue thaws, blood clots and inflammation in those vessels can starve it again, much as in other injuries where blood returns to damaged tissue; see [[Inflammation]] and [[Watershed Blood Supply]]. Much of the final damage is done after rewarming, not during freezing.

Risk rises with anything that reduces heat or blood flow:

- **Wind and wet.** Moving air and damp clothing strip heat far faster than still, dry air; see [[Wind chill]].
- **Altitude.** Low oxygen, dehydration, and exhaustion on high mountains combine with extreme cold; see [[Altitude Sickness]] and [[Fluid Balance]].
- **Tight boots and gloves, smoking, and alcohol,** which narrow vessels or blunt judgment.

Treatment follows hard-won rules. Frozen tissue should be rewarmed quickly in water a little above body temperature, not rubbed and not held near a fire, since numb skin burns without warning. It should not be thawed at all if it might refreeze, because a freeze-thaw-freeze cycle is far worse than staying frozen until shelter is reached. Napoleon's chief surgeon, Dominique-Jean Larrey, recorded during the retreat from Moscow in 1812 that soldiers who thawed frozen limbs beside campfires fared worse than those who did not.

Surgeons wait before amputating, often for weeks, because the line between dead and living tissue takes time to declare itself, summed up in the old saying "frozen in January, amputate in July." Newer treatments that restore blood flow early, including clot-dissolving drugs and a vessel-dilating drug approved in the United States in 2024, have reduced amputations when given soon enough.

Survivors often have lasting sensitivity to cold, numbness, and pain in affected areas; see [[Paresthesia]] and [[Scar Tissue]].
