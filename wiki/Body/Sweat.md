**Sweat** is the fluid released through the skin by sweat glands, mostly water with dissolved salt. Its main job is cooling. When sweat evaporates, it takes heat with it, because turning liquid water into vapor absorbs a large amount of energy; see [[Latent Heat]]. Humans are unusually good sweaters, and that ability is a large part of why people can keep working and running in heat that would overwhelm most other mammals; see [[Thermoregulation]].

There are two main kinds of gland. **Eccrine glands**, numbering in the millions, cover nearly the whole body and produce the watery sweat used for cooling. **Apocrine glands**, concentrated in the armpits and groin, become active at puberty and release a thicker secretion. That secretion is nearly odorless; body odor comes from skin bacteria breaking it down. Deodorants target the bacteria or mask the smell, while antiperspirants use aluminum salts to temporarily plug the sweat ducts.

Sweat rates vary enormously. At rest in mild weather, people lose a small amount without noticing. During hard exercise in heat, rates of one to two liters an hour are common and some athletes exceed that. Salt content also varies severalfold between individuals, so some people finish a long event with white streaks of salt on their clothes. Replacing water without salt during very long events can dilute the blood's sodium dangerously, while losing too much water strains the heart and cooling system; see [[Fluid Balance]].

Evaporation depends on the air. In dry heat, sweat evaporates almost as soon as it forms. In humid heat, it drips off without cooling, and the body heats up despite producing plenty of it. The best single measure of this limit is the wet-bulb temperature, which combines heat and humidity; see [[Dew Point]]. When cooling fails, core temperature can climb to dangerous levels; see [[Heat Stroke]].

The body adapts. After one to two weeks of training in heat, people start sweating earlier, sweat more, and lose less salt per liter; see [[Heat Acclimation]].

Not all sweating is about temperature. Emotional sweating, controlled by the sympathetic nervous system, appears on the palms, soles and forehead during stress or fear; see [[Fight-or-Flight Response]] and [[Autonomic Nervous System]]. Changes in skin conductance from this sweating are measured in psychology experiments and in polygraph tests, which detect arousal rather than lies. Spicy food triggers sweating on the face through the same heat-sensing receptors that respond to real warmth; see [[Capsaicin]]. A fever often breaks with a drenching sweat as the body's set point drops back; see [[Fever]].

**Hyperhidrosis**, excessive sweating beyond what cooling requires, affects a small percentage of people and can be socially disabling. Persistent moisture against the skin also raises the risk of blisters and chafing; see [[Blister]].
