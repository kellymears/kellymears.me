**Vertigo** is the illusion of movement, most often a sense that the room is spinning or that oneself is turning, when nothing is moving. It's a symptom rather than a disease, and it usually means something is wrong with the vestibular system, the balance organs of the inner ear, or the brain areas that process their signals; see [[Vestibular System]].

People use "dizziness" for several different experiences. Vertigo, with its sense of motion, differs from lightheadedness or feeling faint, which usually involves blood pressure; see [[Syncope]]. It also differs from unsteadiness on one's feet, which can come from problems with sensation in the legs; see [[Proprioception]].

Vertigo often comes with nausea and vomiting, because the brain receives conflicting information from the eyes, inner ears, and body; see [[Motion Sickness]]. The eyes may flicker rhythmically, a sign doctors use to locate the problem.

Common causes include:

- **Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo**, the most common cause. Tiny calcium carbonate crystals that normally sit in one part of the inner ear come loose and drift into the fluid-filled canals that sense rotation. Turning over in bed, looking up, or bending down then sets off brief, intense spinning.
- **Vestibular neuritis**, inflammation of the nerve from the inner ear, often after a viral infection, which causes days of severe vertigo.
- **Ménière's disease**, with attacks of vertigo, ringing in the ears, fullness, and fluctuating hearing loss; see [[Tinnitus]].
- **Vestibular migraine**, where vertigo occurs with or instead of headache.
- **Head injury**; see [[Concussion]].

Less often, vertigo comes from the brain itself, including stroke. Vertigo with double vision, slurred speech, weakness, or difficulty walking needs emergency care.

The crystals in BPPV can often be returned to where they belong. The Epley maneuver, developed by the American physician John Epley in the 1980s, is a sequence of head positions that uses gravity to roll the crystals out of the canal. It frequently resolves symptoms in one or two sessions, and people prone to recurrence can learn a version to perform at home. Without treatment, BPPV often settles by itself within weeks.

For lasting balance problems, vestibular rehabilitation uses exercises that repeatedly provoke mild symptoms. Over time, the brain adapts and recalibrates; see [[Neuroplasticity]] and [[Habituation]].

Everyday experiences show the system at work. Spinning in place, then stopping, produces a moment of vertigo as fluid in the canals keeps moving. Alcohol temporarily changes the density of structures in the inner ear, which partly explains the spins of drunkenness and some hangovers; see [[Hangover]]. Virtual reality can provoke similar disorientation when what the eyes see doesn't match the inner ear; see [[Virtual Reality]].
