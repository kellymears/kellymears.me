**Brain freeze**, known medically as a cold-stimulus headache, is the sudden stabbing pain in the forehead or temples that comes from eating or drinking something cold too quickly. It starts within seconds of the cold touching the roof of the mouth or the back of the throat, peaks quickly, and usually fades within a minute. It is harmless, and one of the few headaches that can be produced on demand, which makes it useful for studying headaches in general.

The likely mechanism involves blood flow. Cold on the palate causes blood vessels in the area to constrict and then rapidly dilate as the body tries to warm the tissue; see [[Thermoregulation]]. A 2012 study had volunteers sip ice water through a straw pressed against the palate and found that blood flow increased sharply in an artery supplying the front of the brain as the pain began, and fell as it ended. Because the skull is a closed space, a sudden rise in blood volume can raise pressure and stretch pain-sensitive tissue around the vessels; see [[Blood Pressure]].

The pain is felt in the forehead, not the mouth, because the same large nerve, the trigeminal, carries sensation from both. The brain receives the signal and assigns it to the wrong place, a classic case of [[Referred Pain]]; see also [[Nociception]].

A few patterns:

- **Speed matters.** A small, tongue-in-cheek study published in the British Medical Journal in 2002 had schoolchildren eat ice cream either quickly or slowly; brain freeze was about twice as common in the fast group.
- **Migraine links.** People who get migraines are more prone to brain freeze, and for some of them cold can trigger a fuller headache, suggesting shared pathways.
- **The fix is warmth.** Pressing the tongue against the roof of the mouth, or sipping something warm, speeds recovery by rewarming the palate.

Brain freeze sits in a family of sensations produced by temperature receptors in the mouth and face. Chili heat fools the heat-sensing receptor, menthol fools the cold-sensing one, and cold water on the face triggers a slowing of the heart; see [[Capsaicin]] and [[Mammalian Dive Reflex]]. In each case the body reacts to the temperature signal it receives, not to any real threat.

Research has also used it as a model of how blood flow changes relate to headache pain more broadly, since most headaches arrive unpredictably and can't be caught in the laboratory. That the pain tracks the dilation so closely supports a role for blood vessels in headaches, though most researchers now see migraine as starting in the brain itself, with the vessels as one link in the chain; see [[Central Sensitization]].
