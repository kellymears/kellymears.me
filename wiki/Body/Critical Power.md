**Critical power** is the boundary between efforts that can be sustained in a stable state and efforts that inevitably lead to exhaustion. Below it, the body reaches a steady state where oxygen use, lactate, and fatigue level off. Above it, they climb continuously until the athlete must slow down. The model comes from Hugh Monod and Jean Scherrer, who described it for individual muscles in 1965; it was later extended to cycling, running, rowing, and swimming.

The model has two parameters. **Critical power** (CP), in [[Watts]], is the sustainable ceiling. **W′**, pronounced "W prime", is a finite amount of work, measured in kilojoules, that can be done above CP. Plotting how long an athlete can hold each power output gives a curve that drops steeply, then flattens toward CP. Mathematically the relationship is hyperbolic: the time an effort can last equals W′ divided by how far its power exceeds CP. A rider with a CP of 250 watts and a W′ of 20 kilojoules could hold 350 watts for about 200 seconds, and 300 watts for about 400.

W′ behaves like a battery, and racers call spending it "burning matches". Every surge above CP drains it; riding below CP recharges it, faster the further below CP the rider goes. Since 2012, W′ balance models track the battery second by second from power data.

That gives the model practical uses:

- **Pacing.** For a time trial, the fastest strategy is close to even pacing at a power that empties W′ exactly at the finish. Starting too hard spends the battery early.
- **Tactics.** Attacks, sprints, and short climbs are paid from W′. Knowing its size helps a rider decide which attacks to follow.
- **Training zones.** CP marks the line between the *heavy* domain, where effort is hard but stable, and the *severe* domain, where it is not, a more physiologically grounded boundary than percentages of maximum heart rate. It sits close to the second threshold described under [[Lactate Threshold]].

CP and [[Functional Threshold Power]] measure similar things. FTP is defined practically as the power sustainable for about an hour, while CP is usually sustainable for somewhere between twenty and forty minutes, so FTP tends to be a little lower. CP and W′ are estimated from several all-out efforts, typically three to twenty minutes long.

The two numbers respond to different training. Long sessions at moderate intensity, the base of [[Polarized Training]], raise CP by building aerobic capacity and [[VO2 Max]]. Hard intervals and sprints enlarge W′, which depends more on fast-twitch muscle, as described under [[Muscle Fiber Types]], and anaerobic [[Energy Systems]]. The model simplifies: it misjudges very short and very long efforts and ignores the fatigue of hours in the saddle.
