The **anterior cruciate ligament**, or ACL, runs diagonally through the middle of the knee, connecting the thigh bone to the shinbone. Together with its partner, the posterior cruciate ligament, it forms a cross, which is what "cruciate" means. The ACL stops the shinbone from sliding forward and limits twisting, which matters most when the body plants a foot and changes direction; see [[Tendon and Ligament]].

An ACL tear is a signature injury of sports that involve cutting, pivoting and jumping, such as soccer, basketball, handball and skiing. Roughly two-thirds or more happen without any contact. A typical scenario is landing from a jump or decelerating to change direction with the knee nearly straight and collapsing inward. Many athletes report hearing or feeling a pop, followed by rapid swelling as blood fills the joint, and a sense that the knee gives way.

Female athletes tear their ACLs at several times the rate of male athletes in the same sports. Proposed reasons include differences in pelvis and knee alignment, landing mechanics, muscle strength, joint laxity and possibly hormonal effects on ligaments; see [[Hypermobility]] and [[Menstrual Cycle]]. None alone explains the gap, and the difference in exposure, coaching and access to strength training is also debated.

Prevention works better than expected for an injury that seems like bad luck. Neuromuscular training programs combining jump-landing technique, balance work, strengthening and agility drills, such as the FIFA 11+ warm-up, have cut ACL injuries by about half in many studies; see [[Warm-Up]], [[Plyometrics]] and [[Proprioception]]. The key cue is learning to land softly, with knees bent and tracking over the toes rather than caving inward.

Treatment depends on the person. Active people who want to return to pivoting sports usually have **ACL reconstruction**, in which surgeons replace the torn ligament with a graft taken from the patellar tendon, a hamstring tendon or the quadriceps tendon. Over the following months the graft is slowly remodeled into ligament-like tissue. Return to sport typically takes at least nine months, and returning earlier is associated with higher reinjury rates. Rehabilitation leans heavily on [[Strength Training]] and on relearning movement patterns; see [[Motor Learning]].

Surgery is not always necessary. A Swedish [[Randomized Controlled Trial]] published in 2010 compared early reconstruction with structured rehabilitation plus optional later surgery and found similar outcomes after two and five years, with many in the rehabilitation group never needing an operation.

Whatever the treatment, the knee carries a lasting cost. People with ACL injuries, especially those with accompanying damage to the cartilage pads in the knee, face a much higher risk of early [[Osteoarthritis]], sometimes within a decade or two.
