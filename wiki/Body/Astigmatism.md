**Astigmatism** is a focusing error of the eye in which the front surface, the cornea, or the lens inside is curved unevenly. A perfectly focusing eye has a cornea shaped like part of a basketball, curved equally in every direction. An astigmatic cornea is shaped more like part of a rugby ball or the back of a spoon, curved more steeply one way than the other.

Because light bending depends on surface curvature, the steep and flat directions focus light at different distances; see [[Refraction]] and [[Lens]]. Instead of converging to a single point, light from a point source spreads into a blurred line or smear. That differs from nearsightedness, where distant objects blur but close ones are clear, and from age-related loss of near focus; see [[Myopia]] and [[Presbyopia]]. Astigmatism blurs vision at every distance, often more in one orientation than another, so some lines on a chart look sharper than lines at right angles to them. Uncorrected, it can cause squinting, headaches and tired eyes during close work; see [[Digital Eye Strain]].

Small amounts are nearly universal and go unnoticed, and larger amounts are common, often inherited and usually stable through adulthood. A progressive thinning and bulging of the cornea called keratoconus causes increasing, irregular astigmatism and needs specialist treatment.

The condition was first described by the English physician and physicist Thomas Young, who measured it in their own eye in 1801. The astronomer George Airy had the first cylindrical correcting lens made in 1825. Ordinary spherical lenses bend light equally in every direction; a **cylindrical** lens bends it more in one direction only, canceling the eye's uneven curvature. An eyeglass prescription lists a cylinder value for the amount of correction and an axis angle for its orientation. Toric contact lenses, weighted so they don't rotate on the eye, do the same job, and laser eye surgery can reshape the cornea to reduce it.

Astigmatism is most noticeable at night. When the pupil widens in darkness, more of the uneven cornea is used, and point lights such as headlights and street lamps sprout streaks and starbursts; see [[Dark Adaptation]]. Those streaks resemble the artifacts that cameras and rendering engines produce on bright highlights; see [[Lens Flare]].

The word also names a specific flaw in optical systems. In telescopes, cameras and microscopes, a lens or mirror whose curvature differs by direction or which receives light off its axis shows the same split focus, one of the classic optical aberrations alongside chromatic aberration; see [[Post-Processing]].

Vision tests measure **visual acuity**, the finest detail an eye can resolve at a set distance. Astigmatism is one of the refractive errors that lower it, along with conditions affecting color and the light-sensitive retina itself; see [[Color Vision Deficiency]] and [[Blind Spot]].
