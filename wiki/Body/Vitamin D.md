**Vitamin D** is less a vitamin than a hormone. The skin makes it when ultraviolet B light converts a cholesterol derivative; the liver and kidneys then convert it in two steps to its active form, calcitriol. Few foods contain much of it naturally, oily fish and egg yolks being the main exceptions, so for most people sunlight is the main source. Its classic job is regulating calcium and phosphate, making it central to the [[Endocrine System]] and to bones.

Without enough vitamin D, the gut absorbs only a small fraction of dietary calcium, and the body draws calcium from the skeleton instead. In children this causes **rickets**, soft and bowed bones, which was widespread in industrial cities in the 19th century, where smoke and indoor work blocked sunlight. Cod liver oil and fortified milk nearly eliminated it in the 20th century. In adults deficiency weakens bone, a condition called osteomalacia, and contributes to osteoporosis; see [[Bone Remodeling]].

Who runs low:

- **People at high latitudes in winter.** North of about 37 degrees, the sun is too low from roughly November to February for the skin to make meaningful amounts.
- **People with darker skin**, since melanin absorbs UVB, especially when living far from the equator.
- **People who spend little time outdoors**, cover their skin, or use sunscreen consistently.
- **Older adults**, whose skin produces less.

For athletes, low vitamin D is associated with stress fractures, particularly alongside [[Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport]], and some military studies found supplementing calcium and vitamin D reduced fractures in recruits. It may also play a role in [[Fracture Nonunion]] risk.

Vitamin D receptors exist in many tissues, including the [[Immune System]] and muscle, and observational studies linked low levels to almost everything: heart disease, cancer, depression, infection, [[Sarcopenia]], and overall mortality. That prompted huge enthusiasm for supplements. Then large randomized trials, notably VITAL in 2018 with over 25,000 participants, found supplementing healthy adults did little for cancer, heart disease, or most other outcomes. The likely explanation is reverse causation and confounding: being ill, inactive, or overweight lowers vitamin D, rather than low vitamin D causing illness. It's a textbook case of [[Correlation and Causation]].

What holds up: correcting real deficiency matters for bones, and modest daily supplementation is reasonable for people in the risk groups. Very high doses can cause calcium to build up in the blood, so more isn't better.

Sunlight has effects beyond vitamin D. Bright daylight sets the [[Circadian Rhythm]], and time outdoors in childhood protects against [[Myopia]], probably through light intensity rather than vitamin D.
