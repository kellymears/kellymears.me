**Low back pain** is the leading cause of disability worldwide. Most people experience it at some point, and it accounts for an enormous share of lost work days and medical visits. It's also one of the clearest cases where intuition about pain and damage leads people astray.

About 90 percent of cases are classed as **nonspecific**: no particular structural cause can be identified. A small fraction involve a specific problem, such as a compressed nerve root causing **sciatica**, pain running down the leg; see [[Referred Pain]]. A very small fraction signal something serious, such as infection, fracture, or cancer, and guidelines list red flags that warrant urgent attention: numbness in the groin, loss of bladder control, fever, or unexplained weight loss.

Imaging rarely helps and can hurt. MRI scans of people with no back pain at all frequently show disc bulges, degeneration, and other findings; studies find them in a majority of pain-free people over 40. When such findings appear on a scan of someone in pain, they're easily blamed, and the label "damaged disc" can make people fearful of movement. Guidelines therefore advise against routine imaging without red flags. This is a [[Base Rate Fallacy]] trap: an abnormality that's common in everyone explains little.

Pain and tissue damage are only loosely linked. Back pain is shaped by [[Nociception]], but also by sleep, stress, mood, beliefs, and expectations. People who believe their back is fragile move stiffly and avoid activity, which tends to prolong pain, a pattern called fear-avoidance. Negative expectations can produce the [[Nocebo Effect]]. In long-lasting cases, [[Central Sensitization]] can keep pain going after tissues have healed.

What works, according to current guidelines:

- **Staying active.** Bed rest, once standard, slows recovery. Continuing normal activity as far as possible works better.
- **Reassurance and education.** Explaining that most back pain is not dangerous, and that hurt doesn't always mean harm, improves outcomes.
- **Exercise.** No single type is clearly best. [[Strength Training]], walking, and movement practices all help, and the best exercise is the one people will do.
- **Psychological approaches.** [[Cognitive Behavioral Therapy]] targeting fear and catastrophizing helps persistent pain.

Medication helps less than people expect. Common pain relievers offer small benefits, and opioids carry risks of [[Physical Dependence]] with little long-term gain; see [[Analgesic Ladder]]. Surgery helps specific conditions such as severe sciatica, but not nonspecific pain.

Most acute episodes improve within weeks, which makes almost any treatment look effective: [[Regression to the Mean]] at work. Recurrence is common, though, so the long-term aim is confidence and capacity, not a cure.
