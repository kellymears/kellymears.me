The **Eustachian tube** is a narrow channel, about three and a half centimeters long in adults, that connects the middle ear to the upper throat behind the nose. It's named after Bartolomeo Eustachi, a sixteenth-century Italian anatomist who described it. Its main job is to keep air pressure in the middle ear equal to the pressure outside, so the eardrum can vibrate freely.

The middle ear is an air-filled cavity sealed off from the outside by the eardrum. If outside pressure changes and the middle ear's doesn't, the eardrum bulges inward or outward, muffling sound and causing pain. The tube is normally closed, but small muscles in the throat pull it open briefly during swallowing, yawning, and chewing. The **pop** people feel in their ears is the tube opening and pressure equalizing; see [[Yawning]].

Pressure changes follow the gas laws. As a plane climbs, outside pressure falls and the air trapped in the middle ear expands, pushing outward; the tube vents this fairly easily. On descent, outside pressure rises and the trapped air contracts, pulling the eardrum inward; see [[Ideal Gas Law]] and [[Atmospheric Pressure]]. Air has to be pushed back in, and the tube's shape makes that harder, which is why landing hurts more than takeoff. Swallowing, chewing, or deliberately pushing air up the tube helps; see [[Valsalva Maneuver]].

Diving magnifies the problem. Water pressure rises quickly with depth, and divers must equalize every few meters on the way down. Failing to equalize can cause pain, bleeding into the middle ear, or a ruptured eardrum, an injury known as **barotrauma**. Freedivers learn gentler techniques that use the tongue and throat instead of forced exhalation; see [[Mammalian Dive Reflex]].

Anything that swells the lining of the tube can block it. Colds and allergies commonly do, which is why flying with a congested nose is painful; see [[Allergy]] and [[Sneeze]]. A blocked tube lets fluid collect behind the eardrum, dulling hearing; see [[Hearing Loss]].

Children are especially vulnerable. Their tubes are shorter, narrower, and more horizontal than adults', so they drain poorly and let germs travel from the throat to the ear more easily. This is a major reason middle ear infections are so common in early childhood. Surgeons treat repeated cases by placing tiny ventilation tubes through the eardrum.

The opposite problem also occurs. A tube that stays open hears the body from the inside: people with this condition hear their own voice and breathing booming in their ears.

The middle ear sits next to the balance organs of the inner ear, and pressure imbalances can sometimes cause brief dizziness; see [[Vestibular System]] and [[Vertigo]]. Mountain travel produces pressure changes too; see [[Altitude Sickness]].
