**Ulnar neuropathy** is irritation or compression of the **ulnar nerve**, one of the three main nerves of the hand. It runs from the neck, behind the elbow, into the little-finger side of the hand. When it's compressed, people feel numbness, tingling, or burning in the little finger and half of the ring finger, and in more severe cases weakness and clumsiness in the hand.

The nerve is vulnerable at two places:

- **At the elbow**, where it passes through the *cubital tunnel*. This is the "funny bone." Long periods with the elbow bent, such as holding a phone or sleeping with arms folded, or leaning on the elbow at a desk, stretch and press the nerve. This is **cubital tunnel syndrome**, the second most common nerve compression in the arm, after carpal tunnel syndrome; see [[Repetitive Strain Injury]].
- **At the wrist**, where it passes through a narrow passage called Guyon's canal at the heel of the hand. Sustained pressure here, from handlebars, tools, or crutches, compresses the nerve directly.

Compression at the wrist is common enough in cyclists to be called **cyclist's palsy** or **handlebar palsy**. Long rides with weight on the outer heel of the hand, especially with the wrist bent back, press the nerve against the bones. Riders notice numb fingers during or after long rides, and in some cases weakness lasting days or weeks, such as difficulty with buttons or spreading the fingers.

Prevention is mostly about distributing load and changing position:

- **Fit.** Too much weight on the hands usually means the saddle is tilted forward, the reach too long, or the bars too low; see [[Bike Fit]].
- **Core strength** lets the rider support the torso rather than leaning on the bars; see [[Strength Training]].
- **Hand position.** Changing positions every few minutes, and keeping wrists relatively straight, spreads pressure.
- **Padding.** Padded gloves and thicker bar tape help, though too much padding under the wrong spot can itself press on the nerve.

At the desk, avoiding prolonged elbow bending, not resting elbows on hard surfaces, and keeping the keyboard at a height where the elbows can open past 90 degrees reduce pressure on the cubital tunnel.

Most mild cases resolve with rest and changes in position or equipment. Persistent numbness or weakness, muscle wasting in the hand, or symptoms that spread warrant medical evaluation, since nerves heal slowly and long compression can cause lasting damage. Numbness can also come from the neck, where nerve roots can be pinched, a reminder that symptoms aren't always where the problem is; see [[Referred Pain]] and [[Low Back Pain]].

Nerve compression involves both pressure and reduced blood flow; see [[Compartment Syndrome]] for a related problem in muscle compartments.
