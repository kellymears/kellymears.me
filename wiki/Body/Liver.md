The **liver** is the largest internal organ, weighing about a kilogram and a half in an adult, and it sits under the right ribs doing hundreds of chemical jobs. It is unusual in its blood supply. Besides an artery, it receives the portal vein, which carries blood straight from the intestines. Nearly everything absorbed from a meal passes through the liver before reaching the rest of the body.

That arrangement gives it first claim on nutrients and toxins alike. Many swallowed drugs are partly broken down on this first pass, a process called **first-pass metabolism**, which is why some medicines must be given by injection or under the tongue, and why oral doses are often larger than intravenous ones; see [[Xenobiotic Metabolism]].

Its main jobs fall into a few groups:

- **Fuel.** After a meal the liver stores glucose as [[Glycogen]]. Between meals it releases glucose to keep blood sugar steady, and when glycogen runs low it makes new glucose from amino acids and other molecules, called **gluconeogenesis**. During long fasts it turns fat into ketones for the brain; see [[Metabolic Flexibility]] and [[Intermittent Fasting]].
- **Building.** It makes albumin, which holds water in the bloodstream, and most of the proteins needed for [[Blood Clotting]]. It also produces most of the body's [[Cholesterol]].
- **Digestion.** It secretes **bile**, which emulsifies fat in the gut so enzymes can reach it.
- **Clearance.** It converts toxic ammonia from protein breakdown into urea for the [[Kidney]] to excrete, and it inactivates hormones, drugs and alcohol. The byproducts of alcohol processing contribute to the misery of a [[Hangover]].
- **Storage.** It holds iron, vitamin A and vitamin B12, and performs the first step in activating [[Vitamin D]].

Acetaminophen, also called paracetamol, is safe at recommended doses, but overdoses overwhelm the liver's detoxifying capacity and are a leading cause of acute liver failure in the United States and Britain.

The most common liver condition worldwide is **fatty liver disease**, fat accumulating in liver cells. Once tied mainly to alcohol, it now most often accompanies excess weight and insulin resistance, and in 2023 it was renamed metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease to reflect that; see [[Insulin Sensitivity]]. Estimates suggest it affects roughly a quarter to a third of adults. Fat in the liver can lead to inflammation and scarring. Years of damage from alcohol, viral hepatitis or fat can end in **cirrhosis**, in which scar tissue replaces working cells; see [[Scar Tissue]].

The liver is also unmatched among internal organs in its ability to regrow. Surgeons can remove more than half of a healthy liver, and the remainder grows back toward its original size within weeks to months. That is what makes partial donation from a living donor possible.
