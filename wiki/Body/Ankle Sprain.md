**An ankle sprain** is an injury to the ligaments that hold the ankle bones together. Most happen when the foot rolls inward, stepping on uneven ground, landing on another player's foot, or misjudging a curb, which overstretches or tears the ligaments on the outside of the ankle. Ankle sprains are among the most common injuries in sport and in everyday life.

Ligaments are tough bands of connective tissue that limit how far joints move; see [[Tendon and Ligament]]. In a typical sprain, the ligament at the front outside of the ankle gives way first. Clinicians describe sprains in grades:

- **Grade I.** Ligaments are stretched, with mild pain and swelling.
- **Grade II.** A partial tear, with more swelling, bruising, and some looseness; see [[Bruise]].
- **Grade III.** A complete tear, with marked instability.

A less common high ankle sprain injures the ligaments that bind the two lower leg bones together and usually takes much longer to heal.

Distinguishing a sprain from a fracture matters. The Ottawa ankle rules, developed in Canada in the early 1990s, use a short list of findings, such as tenderness over specific bone points and the ability to bear weight, to decide who needs an X-ray. They cut unnecessary imaging substantially without missing many fractures; see [[Checklist]]. Persistent pain in a specific bone spot after a sprain can point to a fracture missed earlier; see [[Stress Fracture]].

Treatment has shifted. Rest, ice, compression, and elevation were long standard, but evidence now favors protecting the ankle briefly, then early movement and weight bearing as pain allows, often with a brace or tape. Prolonged immobilization in a cast tends to slow recovery. Swelling reflects the body's healing response, and new collagen remodels along the lines of stress the ankle experiences; see [[Inflammation]] and [[Scar Tissue]].

The biggest risk after a sprain is another one. Many people who sprain an ankle sprain it again, and a substantial share develop chronic ankle instability, with repeated giving way. Part of the problem is mechanical looseness, but much is a loss of the sense of joint position and the quick reflexes that correct a roll; see [[Proprioception]]. Balance training, such as standing on one leg or on a wobble board, retrains those reflexes and reduces recurrence; see [[Motor Learning]].

Repeated sprains and instability can damage the joint's cartilage over years, contributing to arthritis in the ankle; see [[Osteoarthritis]]. Prevention focuses on balance exercises, bracing or taping for those with prior sprains, and good preparation before sport; see [[Warm-Up]].

A sprain that seems mild can still deserve attention. Inability to bear weight, numbness, or a deformed ankle are reasons for urgent assessment; see [[Fracture Fixation]].
