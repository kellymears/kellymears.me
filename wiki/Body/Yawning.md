A **yawn** is an involuntary sequence: the mouth opens wide, a long deep breath is drawn in, the jaw and often the whole body stretch, and the breath is let out quickly. A typical yawn lasts about six seconds. Nearly all vertebrates yawn, from fish to birds to dogs, and human fetuses yawn in the womb by late in the first trimester. Despite how common it is, its purpose is still argued over.

The classic explanation, that yawning brings in oxygen when blood levels run low, has not held up. In experiments in the 1980s, the psychologist Robert Provine had volunteers breathe air with extra carbon dioxide or pure oxygen and found that neither changed how often they yawned; exercise, which raises breathing sharply, does not bring on yawns either.

Current hypotheses include:

- **Brain cooling.** The deep breath and the stretching of the jaw increase blood flow to the head and bring in air that may be cooler than the blood, possibly cooling the brain. Studies have found people yawn more when the outside air is moderately cooler than the body and less in hot weather; see [[Thermoregulation]].
- **Shifting state.** Yawns cluster around transitions: after waking, before sleep, during [[Boredom]], and, less obviously, before stressful events like a parachute jump or a performance. A yawn may help move the brain from one level of alertness to another; see [[Sleep Pressure]], [[Circadian Rhythm]], and [[Yerkes-Dodson Law]].
- **Stretching.** The yawn engages muscles across the face, neck, and chest, resembling the full-body stretch it often comes with.

Yawns are **contagious**. Seeing someone yawn, hearing one, or even reading about one, can set off a yawn in many people, and some readers will have yawned by this point. Chimpanzees catch yawns from each other, and some studies report dogs catching them from people. Contagious yawning appears in children only after the first few years, around the time they start to understand other minds.

That timing led to a popular idea that catching yawns reflects empathy; see [[Mirror Neurons]]. Some evidence fits: one study of natural interactions found people caught yawns most readily from family, then friends, then strangers. But a large later study found only a weak link with measures of empathy and a stronger decline with age, so the connection remains unclear. Contagion fits a broader pattern of behaviors spreading through groups; see [[Social Proof]].

Yawning is controlled by the brainstem and hypothalamus and is affected by several chemical messengers; some medications increase it markedly. Unusually frequent yawning can accompany some neurological conditions, though in most people it means little more than tiredness or a dull meeting; see [[Autonomic Nervous System]] and [[Vigilance Decrement]].
