A **bike fit** is the adjustment of a bicycle to its rider. A rider touches the bike at three places, the saddle, the handlebars, and the pedals, and the positions of those contact points determine the angles of the knees, hips, back, and arms through every pedal stroke. A cyclist turning the cranks at a typical [[Cadence]] of 90 makes over five thousand revolutions an hour, so a small misalignment repeated that many times becomes a [[Repetitive Strain Injury]].

**Saddle height** matters most. Too low, and the knee stays sharply bent, loading the front of the knee and wasting power. Too high, and the hips rock to reach the pedals, straining the back of the knee and the saddle contact. Rules of thumb include Greg LeMond's inseam times 0.883, measured from the bottom bracket, but fitters today usually aim for a knee angle of roughly 25 to 35 degrees at the bottom of the stroke. The rough diagnostic is that pain at the front of the knee suggests a saddle too low or too far forward, and pain behind the knee one too high.

Other adjustments follow:

- **Saddle setback.** How far behind the bottom bracket the saddle sits, which shifts weight between seat and hands.
- **Reach and drop.** The distance and height difference from saddle to bars, which sets the torso angle. Too long a reach strains the neck, shoulders, and hands; numb hands often mean too much weight forward.
- **Cleats.** Fore-aft position under the foot, rotation to match the natural angle of the foot, and *float*, the free rotation the pedal allows. Misaligned cleats twist the knee on every stroke, loading [[Tendon and Ligament]] tissue that adapts slowly.

Frame geometry now describes a bike by **stack and reach**, the vertical and horizontal distances from the bottom bracket to the top of the head tube, which makes frames of different brands comparable.

Fit always balances competing aims. A lower, narrower position reduces [[Aerodynamic Drag]], which dominates resistance at racing speeds, but closes the hip angle and can cost sustainable power and comfort. Time-trial riders accept that trade; long-distance riders usually do not. The best position is the one a rider can hold for the whole ride.

Good fitters change one thing at a time, in small steps of a few millimeters, and let the body adapt for several rides before judging. A sudden large change is effectively a spike in [[Training Load]] on tissues that were tuned to the old position. Riders' sense of their own body position, [[Proprioception]], also adjusts, so a correct new position can feel wrong for a week. [[Stretching|Flexibility]], [[Strength Training|strength]], and injury history all shape the right fit, so fits change over time.
