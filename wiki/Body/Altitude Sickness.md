**Altitude sickness** is a set of illnesses caused by the low oxygen at high altitude. The air at the top of a mountain has the same proportion of oxygen as at sea level, but the pressure is lower, so each breath delivers fewer oxygen molecules; see [[Atmospheric Pressure]] and [[Ideal Gas Law]]. At about 2,500 meters, many people start to feel the effects, and at the summit of Everest the air holds roughly a third as much oxygen as at sea level.

The most common form is **acute mountain sickness**, which feels like a bad hangover: headache, nausea, fatigue, dizziness, and poor sleep, usually starting 6 to 12 hours after arrival. It usually resolves within a few days as the body adjusts.

Two forms are dangerous:

- **High-altitude pulmonary edema** (HAPE). Low oxygen constricts blood vessels in the lungs unevenly, raising pressure until fluid leaks into the air sacs. Symptoms include breathlessness at rest, a persistent cough, and extreme fatigue. It is the leading cause of altitude-related death.
- **High-altitude cerebral edema** (HACE). Fluid builds up in the brain, causing confusion, poor coordination, and drowsiness, and it can progress to coma.

Both require immediate descent, which is the most effective treatment; even a few hundred meters can help.

The body adapts to altitude through **acclimatization**. Breathing deepens within hours, the heart beats faster, and the kidneys adjust the blood's chemistry to allow faster breathing; see [[Autonomic Nervous System]] and [[Cardiac Output]]. Over weeks, the body makes more red blood cells to carry oxygen, the same response athletes seek through [[Altitude Training]]. At night, breathing can cycle between deep breaths and pauses, disrupting sleep; see [[Sleep Stages]].

Prevention relies on ascending slowly. A common guideline is that above 3,000 meters, the altitude where one sleeps should rise by no more than about 300 to 500 meters a day, with a rest day every few days. Climbers follow the rule of climbing high and sleeping low. The drug acetazolamide speeds acclimatization by changing blood chemistry. Fitness doesn't protect against altitude sickness, and some fit people get into trouble by climbing too fast.

Susceptibility varies widely and is partly genetic. Populations that have lived at high altitude for thousands of years, such as Tibetans and Andean peoples, have adapted in different ways: many Tibetans breathe more and avoid excess red blood cells, while Andean highlanders have more of them.

Altitude affects other things too. Water boils at a lower temperature, so cooking takes longer; see [[Boiling Point]]. The body loses more water through breathing dry air, and lower oxygen reduces endurance performance; see [[Fluid Balance]] and [[VO2 Max]]. Mild altitude sickness is easy to mistake for dehydration or a hangover, which is why it often goes unrecognized; see [[Homeostasis]].
