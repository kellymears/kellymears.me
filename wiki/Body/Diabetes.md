**Diabetes mellitus** is a condition in which blood glucose stays too high. The name comes from Greek and Latin for "passing through" and "honey-sweet," because untreated diabetes produces large volumes of sugary urine. Glucose spills into the urine once blood levels exceed what the [[Kidney]] can reclaim, dragging water with it, which causes the classic thirst and frequent urination.

Glucose control depends on insulin, a hormone from beta cells in the pancreas that lets muscle, fat and liver take up glucose; see [[Insulin Sensitivity]]. The two main types fail in different ways:

- **Type 1 diabetes** is autoimmune. The immune system destroys the beta cells, usually starting in childhood or young adulthood, and insulin production stops; see [[Immune System]]. It makes up roughly 5 to 10 percent of cases and requires insulin for life.
- **Type 2 diabetes** begins with insulin resistance, tissues responding weakly, while the pancreas gradually fails to keep up. It accounts for about 90 percent of cases and is strongly tied to excess weight, especially fat around the organs and in the [[Liver]], along with inactivity, genetics, poor sleep and age; see [[Body Composition]] and [[Sleep Apnea]].

Before insulin was isolated, type 1 was a death sentence within months or a few years. In 1921 and 1922 Frederick Banting, Charles Best, John Macleod and James Collip in Toronto extracted it from animal pancreases, and in January 1922 a 14-year-old named Leonard Thompson became the first person treated.

Treatment creates its own hazard. Too much insulin relative to food or exercise causes **hypoglycemia**, low blood sugar, which brings shaking, sweating, confusion and in severe cases seizures. Without insulin at all, the body burns fat so fast that ketones accumulate and acidify the blood, an emergency called diabetic ketoacidosis, quite different from the mild ketosis of fasting; see [[Metabolic Flexibility]].

The standard long-term measure is **HbA1c**, the fraction of hemoglobin with glucose attached. Because red blood cells live about three months, it reflects average blood sugar over that period. Continuous glucose monitors now show levels in real time.

Persistently high glucose damages blood vessels. Small vessels suffer in the eyes, kidneys and nerves, causing vision loss, kidney failure and numbness in the feet; see [[Paresthesia]]. Large vessels develop [[Atherosclerosis]], raising the risk of heart attack and [[Stroke]]. Poorly healing foot wounds can lead to amputation.

Type 2 diabetes is often preventable. In the Diabetes Prevention Program, a large [[Randomized Controlled Trial]] published in 2002, a lifestyle program with modest weight loss and regular activity cut progression from prediabetes by 58 percent, more than the drug metformin. Exercise also lets muscle take up glucose through a route that does not need insulin, and newer drugs that mimic gut hormones reduce appetite and weight; see [[Appetite Regulation]].
