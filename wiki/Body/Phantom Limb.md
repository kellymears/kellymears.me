A **phantom limb** is the sensation that an amputated limb is still present. Nearly everyone who loses a limb experiences it: the missing hand may feel warm, itch, grip, or rest in a fixed position. Most also experience **phantom pain**, which can be burning, stabbing, or cramping, as if a fist were clenched with nails digging into the palm. The phenomenon also follows the removal of other body parts, such as breasts, teeth, and eyes.

The American physician Silas Weir Mitchell named it in 1871 after treating amputees from the Civil War, though surgeons had described it centuries earlier. Many patients hid it, fearing they would be thought mad.

Phantoms show that the sense of having a body is built by the brain. The brain keeps a map of the body, the **body schema**, assembled from touch, joint position, and movement; see [[Proprioception]]. When a limb is lost, the map doesn't vanish with it. In the somatosensory cortex, each body part has its own patch, and the face's patch sits next to the hand's.

In the 1990s the neuroscientist V. S. Ramachandran found that touching an arm amputee's cheek could produce a sensation in the missing hand, with each part of the face mapping onto particular fingers. The deprived hand area had been taken over by input from its neighbor, a sign that the adult brain rewires itself after injury; see [[Neuroplasticity]].

Why phantoms hurt is less settled. Leading explanations:

- **Cortical remapping.** Reorganization of the body map correlates with pain intensity.
- **Peripheral nerves.** Cut nerve endings in the stump form tangled growths that fire spontaneously; see [[Nociception]].
- **Central sensitization.** Pain before amputation can leave the spinal cord and brain amplifying signals afterward; see [[Central Sensitization]].
- **Motor mismatch.** The brain sends commands to move the limb and gets no feedback, a conflict that may itself register as pain.

Ramachandran's **mirror box** addressed the last. The patient puts the intact limb beside a mirror, so its reflection appears where the missing limb should be, then moves it. Seeing the "phantom" move can unclench a cramped phantom and ease pain for some patients, though trials are small and results mixed. Virtual reality versions extend the idea; see [[Virtual Reality]]. Blocking nerves before and during amputation was hoped to prevent phantom pain, with mixed results; see [[Regional Nerve Block]].

The body schema can also be fooled in intact people. In the rubber hand illusion, a fake hand stroked in sync with a person's hidden real one soon feels like their own.

Phantom sensation sits among other cases where pain is felt somewhere it doesn't originate; see [[Referred Pain]]. Prostheses that feed touch back to the nerves have reduced phantom pain in some users.
