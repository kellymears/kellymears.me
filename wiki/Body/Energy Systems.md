Muscles run on one fuel: **ATP** (adenosine triphosphate). Splitting off one of its phosphate groups releases the energy that makes muscle fibers contract. The catch is that muscles store only enough ATP for a few seconds of hard effort, so it must be rebuilt continuously. Three **energy systems** do that rebuilding, and which one dominates depends on how hard and how long the effort is.

- **Phosphocreatine system.** Muscles hold a small reserve of phosphocreatine, which can donate its phosphate to rebuild ATP almost instantly. It powers maximal efforts like a jump, a lift, or the first seconds of a sprint, but runs low within about ten seconds. It refills over a few minutes of rest, which is why sprint intervals need long recoveries.
- **Glycolytic system.** Glucose, from the blood or from stored [[Glycogen]], is broken down without needing oxygen, producing ATP quickly but inefficiently. It dominates hard efforts lasting from roughly ten seconds to two minutes. Its end product, pyruvate, becomes lactate when produced faster than it can be used, and the point at which lactate starts to accumulate defines the [[Lactate Threshold]].
- **Oxidative system.** Inside [[Mitochondria]], carbohydrate and fat are burned completely with oxygen. This yields far more ATP per molecule of glucose, and fat stores are almost unlimited, but the process is slower to ramp up and limited by how fast oxygen can be delivered, measured as [[VO2 Max]].

The systems don't take turns; all three run at once, and their shares shift with intensity. Even in a 400-meter sprint the aerobic system contributes a substantial part, and in events lasting more than about two minutes it supplies most of the energy. As intensity rises, the body shifts from fat toward carbohydrate, a pattern George Brooks and Jacques Mercier called the *crossover concept* in 1994. That is why glycogen, not fat, limits performance in hard endurance events, and why running out of it ends them.

Muscle fibers reflect the split. Slow-twitch fibers are dense with mitochondria and specialize in oxidative work; fast-twitch fibers rely more on glycolysis and fatigue quickly. See [[Muscle Fiber Types]].

Training targets each system differently. Short sprints with full recovery develop the phosphocreatine system. Repeated hard intervals improve glycolytic capacity and tolerance. Long, easy work builds mitochondria and capillaries, expanding the aerobic engine, the rationale behind [[Polarized Training]] and the lower [[Heart Rate Zones]]. Power-based training tracks these through markers like [[Functional Threshold Power]], and the cumulative strain is modeled as [[Training Load]].

The systems are also inefficient in a useful way: only about a quarter of the energy released ends up as mechanical work, and the rest becomes heat, which is why exercise demands so much from [[Thermoregulation]]. How quickly muscles take up glucose afterward depends on [[Insulin Sensitivity]].
