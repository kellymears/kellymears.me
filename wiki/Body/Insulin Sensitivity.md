**Insulin sensitivity** is how responsive the body's cells are to insulin, the hormone that tells them to take glucose out of the blood. Insulin is made by beta cells in the pancreas and released when blood glucose rises, typically after a meal. It signals muscle, fat, and liver cells to absorb glucose and store it, as [[Glycogen]] or fat, keeping blood glucose within a narrow range.

When cells respond well, a small amount of insulin does the job. When they respond poorly, a state called **insulin resistance**, the pancreas must release more insulin to achieve the same effect. For years, this extra output keeps blood glucose normal, hiding the problem. If the beta cells eventually can't keep up, blood glucose rises, leading to prediabetes and type 2 diabetes. Insulin resistance is also associated with high blood pressure, fatty liver, and cardiovascular disease.

Skeletal muscle is the main destination for glucose after a meal, absorbing the large majority of it. Inside muscle cells, glucose enters through transporters called **GLUT4**, which normally sit inside the cell and move to the surface when insulin signals. Contracting muscle can move GLUT4 to the surface by itself, without insulin. This is why exercise lowers blood glucose even in people with insulin resistance, and why a single session improves insulin sensitivity for roughly a day or two afterward. Regular training extends the benefit.

Factors that raise sensitivity:

- **Exercise**, both aerobic and resistance training. Building muscle through [[Muscle Hypertrophy]] enlarges the body's glucose sink, and aerobic training increases [[Mitochondria]] that burn fuel.
- **Breaking up sitting.** Short walks after meals blunt glucose spikes.
- **Weight loss**, especially of fat around the organs.

Factors that lower it:

- **Sleep loss.** A 1999 study by Karine Spiegel and colleagues found that a week of four-hour nights left healthy young men with glucose tolerance resembling that of prediabetes. See [[Sleep Pressure]].
- **Circadian disruption.** Glucose tolerance is lower in the evening than the morning, and shift work and [[Jet Lag]] scramble this timing. See [[Circadian Rhythm]].
- **Chronic stress.** Cortisol raises blood glucose and opposes insulin, part of [[Allostatic Load]].
- **Inactivity and chronic [[Inflammation]]**, which interfere with insulin signaling.

Continuous glucose monitors, small sensors worn on the arm, have made blood glucose visible in real time and are popular beyond diabetes care, though healthy people's normal glucose fluctuations are often over-interpreted.

For athletes, insulin sensitivity is generally high, and post-exercise is when muscles refill glycogen fastest, which is why recovery nutrition focuses on carbohydrate after hard sessions. The fuel choices muscles make during exercise are described under [[Energy Systems]]. The body's capacity to adapt to stress and recover, shaped by [[Training Load]], also shapes how efficiently it handles fuel.
