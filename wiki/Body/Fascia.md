**Fascia** is connective tissue made mostly of collagen that wraps around and between muscles, organs, blood vessels, and nerves. It forms sheets, bands, and layers, from the thin film around each muscle fiber to tough sheets in the thigh and lower back. For much of the history of anatomy, fascia was cut away and discarded to reveal the muscles underneath, but research since the 2000s has paid much more attention to it.

Fascia has several roles:

- **Structure.** It holds muscles and organs in place and separates them into compartments.
- **Force transmission.** It links muscles to each other and to bone, so tension in one muscle can affect neighboring ones; see [[Tendon and Ligament]].
- **Gliding.** Layers of fascia slide over each other, lubricated by fluid, so muscles can move independently.
- **Sensation.** Fascia contains many nerve endings that detect stretch and pressure, and may contribute to the body's sense of position and to pain; see [[Proprioception]] and [[Nociception]].

Fascia can also cause serious medical problems. Compartments enclosed by tough fascia can't expand, so swelling inside them can cut off blood flow, an emergency treated by cutting the fascia open; see [[Compartment Syndrome]]. Plantar fasciitis, pain in the band along the sole of the foot, is one of the most common causes of heel pain. After surgery or injury, layers can stick together as tissue heals; see [[Scar Tissue]].

Fascia has become popular in fitness and alternative therapy, often with exaggerated claims. Practitioners describe fascia as "tight," "knotted," or "stuck," and say techniques like **foam rolling** and **myofascial release** loosen it. But dense fascia is extremely strong, and studies suggest that the force needed to permanently deform it is far greater than hands or foam rollers can apply. Whatever these techniques do, they probably aren't reshaping fascia.

Foam rolling does seem to have short-term effects. It can increase range of motion for several minutes without reducing strength, and it may reduce muscle soreness after hard exercise; see [[Delayed Onset Muscle Soreness]] and [[Warm-Up]]. The likely explanation is the nervous system: pressure changes how sensitive tissues feel and how much stretch people tolerate, similar to how rubbing a sore spot dulls pain; see [[Gate Control Theory]] and [[Stretching]]. Feeling cared for also helps; see [[Placebo Effect]].

Some researchers propose that whole-body chains of fascia, linking distant parts of the body, explain how problems in one place cause pain in another. Anatomical studies show some continuity between muscles, but the clinical theories built on these chains remain largely untested; see [[Referred Pain]].

Fascia changes with age, injury, and activity, becoming stiffer with inactivity and remodeling in response to load, as tendons do. Like most tissue, it adapts to how it is used; see [[Elasticity]] and [[Low Back Pain]].
