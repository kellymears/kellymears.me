**Delayed onset muscle soreness (DOMS)** is the aching and stiffness that appears a day or two after unaccustomed exercise. It typically begins 12 to 24 hours afterward, peaks between 24 and 72 hours, and fades within a week. It is different from the burn felt during hard effort, which passes within minutes.

A persistent myth blames lactic acid. It is wrong. Lactate is cleared from muscle within an hour or so of exercise, long before soreness begins, and it is a fuel rather than a waste product (see [[Lactate Threshold]]). The actual cause is mechanical strain, particularly from **eccentric contractions**, in which a muscle produces force while lengthening. Running downhill, lowering a weight, and descending stairs are all eccentric. The strain causes microscopic damage to muscle fibers and their connective tissue. The repair process, an [[Inflammation]] response, sensitizes nerve endings in the muscle over the following day, which is why tenderness builds rather than appearing at once (see [[Nociception]]).

That explains common patterns:

- **Downhill hurts more than uphill.** A downhill run produces far more soreness than an uphill run of the same effort.
- **Cyclists are mostly spared.** Pedaling is almost entirely concentric, which is why cyclists who start running are often surprised by how sore they get.
- **The repeated bout effect.** One session of a new exercise strongly protects against soreness from the same exercise for weeks. The muscle, nerves, and connective tissue adapt quickly, an instance of [[Supercompensation]] and of [[Hormesis]].

Soreness is a poor measure of a workout. It reflects novelty more than effectiveness: a familiar hard session may produce none, while an easy but unfamiliar one produces plenty. Chasing soreness is a form of [[Goodhart's Law]]. Training while sore is generally safe, though force and range of motion are reduced, so it is a poor time to attempt a personal best. Muscle damage and the soreness that follows add to [[Training Load]] beyond what heart rate or power numbers capture.

Remedies have weak evidence. Massage modestly reduces perceived soreness. Light movement helps temporarily. Stretching doesn't prevent DOMS, and anti-inflammatory drugs reduce pain somewhat but may blunt the adaptation the damage triggers. Many products sold for recovery owe much of their effect to the [[Placebo Effect]].

Severe pain, marked swelling, or dark urine after extreme exercise isn't DOMS but a possible sign of rhabdomyolysis, a breakdown of muscle tissue that can damage the kidneys. It can rarely accompany exertional [[Compartment Syndrome]] and needs medical care.
