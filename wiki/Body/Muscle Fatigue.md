**Muscle fatigue** is the drop in a muscle's ability to produce force or power during exertion. The last repetitions of a heavy set feel slow; legs burn and weaken at the end of a hard climb; a grip slips after holding on too long. The muscle hasn't been damaged in the way a strain damages it. It has temporarily lost capacity, and with rest it recovers.

Fatigue arises at two broad levels. **Peripheral fatigue** happens inside the muscle itself. **Central fatigue** happens in the nervous system, which reduces the drive it sends to the muscle; see [[Central Governor Theory]]. Most real-world fatigue involves both, in proportions that depend on the task.

For much of the twentieth century, the main culprit was thought to be lactic acid. Experiments in the early 1900s found that tired frog muscles accumulated it, and the idea of muscles "poisoned" by lactic acid became common sense among athletes and coaches. The picture has since changed. Lactate is produced during intense exercise, but it's also a fuel that other muscles and the heart burn readily, and its accumulation doesn't by itself explain fatigue; see [[Lactate Threshold]] and [[Energy Systems]].

The current view points to several mechanisms within the muscle:

- **Phosphate buildup.** Breaking down the muscle's immediate energy currency releases inorganic phosphate, which interferes with the proteins that generate force.
- **Calcium handling.** Contraction depends on calcium released inside the muscle cell, and repeated contractions impair its release.
- **Fuel depletion.** In long efforts, running out of stored carbohydrate leads to a profound loss of power; see [[Glycogen]].
- **Acidity.** Accumulating hydrogen ions contribute, though less than once thought.

Muscle fiber type shapes how quickly fatigue sets in. Fast-twitch fibers produce high force but tire rapidly, while slow-twitch fibers sustain modest force for hours; see [[Muscle Fiber Types]] and [[Mitochondria]].

Fatigue sets the limits of performance. The highest power that can be sustained without fatigue piling up defines a boundary between efforts that can continue for a long time and those that can't; see [[Critical Power]]. Interval training works partly by repeatedly pushing into fatigue and recovering; see [[Interval Training]]. Supplements that increase the muscle's fast energy reserves can delay fatigue in short, repeated efforts; see [[Creatine]].

Fatigue differs from the soreness that comes a day or two later, which reflects damage and repair; see [[Delayed Onset Muscle Soreness]]. In extreme cases, overwhelmed muscle cells break down and release their contents into the blood, a medical emergency; see [[Rhabdomyolysis]].

Recovery from fatigue, followed by adaptation, is the basis of training. Too little recovery between hard sessions accumulates into longer-lasting fatigue; see [[Supercompensation]] and [[Training Load]].
