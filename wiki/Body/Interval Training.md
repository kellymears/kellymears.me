**Interval training** alternates periods of hard effort with periods of easier recovery. Because the recovery lets the body partly reset, an athlete can accumulate far more total time at high intensity than in one continuous effort. Four four-minute efforts near maximum, separated by rest, add up to sixteen minutes at an intensity that couldn't be held for more than about eight minutes straight.

The method has a long history. Swedish *fartlek*, "speed play," mixed fast and slow running in the 1930s. German coaches in the same era developed structured intervals, and the Czech runner Emil Zátopek became famous in the late 1940s and early 1950s for enormous sessions of repeated 400-meter runs.

Interval formats vary along a few dimensions: effort intensity, effort duration, recovery duration, and number of repeats. Common types include:

- **Threshold intervals.** Several efforts of 8–20 minutes near [[Lactate Threshold]] or [[Functional Threshold Power]], with short recoveries, building the ability to sustain hard effort.
- **VO2 max intervals.** Efforts of 3–5 minutes at roughly the highest power sustainable for that long, aiming to push the heart and lungs to their limit; see [[VO2 Max]].
- **Short intervals.** Efforts of 30 seconds to a minute, sometimes with equally short recoveries, such as 30/30s.
- **Sprint intervals.** All-out efforts of 10–30 seconds with long recoveries, training the anaerobic systems and peak power; see [[Energy Systems]].
- **Tabata.** Eight rounds of 20 seconds all-out and 10 seconds rest, from a 1996 study by Izumi Tabata. The protocol is now often used loosely for any short circuit.

The appeal of **HIIT** in the fitness world is efficiency. Studies have shown that short, very hard interval sessions can produce improvements in aerobic fitness and [[Insulin Sensitivity]] comparable to much longer moderate sessions, at least in untrained or moderately trained people over a few weeks.

The limits matter as much as the benefits. Hard intervals are stressful, need more recovery, and produce diminishing returns when done too often. Endurance athletes typically do most of their training at low intensity and only a small share at high intensity, a pattern that seems to work better than mostly moderate or mostly hard training; see [[Polarized Training]] and [[Heart Rate Zones]]. Too many hard sessions without enough recovery lead to stagnation or overtraining; see [[Supercompensation]] and [[Training Load]].

Gains from intervals also come and go faster than those from base training. A block of intervals can sharpen fitness in weeks, but the adaptations fade quickly without maintenance; see [[Detraining]]. That's why coaches place intense interval blocks before important events rather than year-round; see [[Periodization]].

Power meters and heart rate monitors have made intervals precise, but heart rate lags behind effort during short intervals, so power or pace is a better guide; see [[Critical Power]].
