The **autonomic nervous system** controls the body's automatic functions: heart rate, blood pressure, breathing, digestion, sweating, pupil size, and much else. It works without conscious control, constantly adjusting the body to its circumstances. It has two main branches that often act in opposition, plus a third, the enteric nervous system, a dense network of neurons running the gut.

The **sympathetic nervous system** prepares the body for action, the "fight-or-flight" response named by the physiologist Walter Cannon in the early twentieth century. When activated, it:

- Speeds the heart and strengthens its contractions; see [[Cardiac Output]].
- Raises blood pressure and redirects blood from the gut and skin to the muscles; see [[Blood Pressure]].
- Opens the airways and dilates the pupils.
- Releases glucose from storage and stimulates sweating; see [[Thermoregulation]].
- Triggers the adrenal glands to release adrenaline; see [[Endocrine System]].

The **parasympathetic nervous system** supports "rest and digest" functions. It slows the heart, stimulates digestion and salivation, and promotes recovery and repair. Much of its output travels through the [[Vagus Nerve]], which runs from the brainstem to the heart, lungs, and gut.

The branches aren't an on/off switch. Both are always active, and the balance shifts moment to moment. At rest, the heart beats slower than its natural pacemaker rate because parasympathetic activity holds it back. The small beat-to-beat variations that result are measured as [[Heart Rate Variability]], widely used as a rough index of the balance and of recovery.

The system is central to [[Homeostasis]], keeping internal conditions stable. It responds to signals from inside the body, such as falling blood pressure on standing up, as well as to perceived threats. The brain's sense of the body's internal state relies heavily on autonomic signals; see [[Interoception]].

Problems arise when the stress response is activated too often or for too long. Chronic sympathetic activation is associated with raised blood pressure, poor sleep, digestive problems, and impaired recovery, part of the wear described as [[Allostatic Load]]. In training, an athlete whose resting heart rate rises and heart rate variability falls may not be recovering; see [[Supercompensation]].

Several practices shift the balance toward parasympathetic activity. Slow breathing, especially with long exhales, increases vagal activity and lowers heart rate within minutes. Regular aerobic exercise raises baseline vagal tone over time. Cold water on the face triggers the *diving reflex*, sharply slowing the heart; see [[Cold Exposure]]. Some therapies for anxiety use these techniques to calm physical arousal; see [[Window of Tolerance]].

The idea of a single unified stress response has been refined over time. Responses vary by threat and person, and include freezing as well as fighting or fleeing. The popular "polyvagal theory" built on vagal anatomy is disputed by many physiologists, though its practical emphasis on safety and calm remains influential.
