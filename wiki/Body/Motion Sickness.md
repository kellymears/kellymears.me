**Motion sickness** is a cluster of symptoms, including pallor, cold sweating, yawning, drowsiness, nausea, and vomiting, triggered by real or apparent motion. Its many names include seasickness, carsickness, space sickness, and, for screens and headsets, cybersickness. Nearly everyone with a working inner ear can get it under some conditions.

The most widely accepted explanation is **sensory conflict theory**, developed by James Reason and Joseph Brand in 1975. The brain predicts what each sense should report based on experience, and sickness follows when the reports disagree; see [[Predictive Processing]]:

- **In a ship's cabin**, the inner ear feels the rolling, but the eyes see a room that appears still.
- **Reading in a car**, the eyes see a steady page while the body feels acceleration and turns.
- **In virtual reality**, the eyes see movement through a scene while the inner ear reports the body is still; see [[Vestibular System]].

Why conflict should cause nausea is less clear. One hypothesis, from Michel Treisman in 1977, is that the brain mistakes the mismatch for poisoning, since many toxins disturb the senses, and vomiting would be the right response. A rival theory, from Gary Riccio and Thomas Stoffregen, holds that sickness comes from losing control of posture, not from conflict itself. People without working inner ears are essentially immune.

Patterns:

- **Slow, rolling motion** is most provocative, especially vertical oscillation around one cycle every five seconds, the rhythm of a ship in swell.
- **Drivers rarely get sick**, while passengers do. Controlling the motion lets the brain predict it; see [[Proprioception]].
- **Children** between about 2 and 12 are most susceptible; infants rarely are.
- **Adaptation.** Sailors get their "sea legs" after a few days, then sometimes feel the ground swaying when they return to land.

Nausea and the other symptoms run through the body's automatic nervous system and the nerves linking gut and brain; see [[Autonomic Nervous System]] and [[Vagus Nerve]].

For screens, design choices matter:

- **Latency and frame rate.** Delay between head movement and the image updating is a major trigger in headsets; see [[Input Latency]] and [[Frame Rate]].
- **Field of view.** Narrowing the visible area during movement reduces symptoms, a common comfort option in games.
- **Movement options.** Teleporting instead of gliding, and avoiding artificial acceleration and rotation, help many players; see [[Virtual Reality]].
- **Interface motion.** Large animations, parallax effects, and zooms trouble some users, who can ask apps to reduce motion; see [[Reduced Motion]] and [[Parallax Scrolling]].

Remedies include looking at the horizon, sitting where motion is smallest, fresh air, and medications such as antihistamines and scopolamine, which work best taken before travel. Ginger has modest evidence.

Feeling the stomach churn is itself a form of internal sensing; see [[Interoception]].
