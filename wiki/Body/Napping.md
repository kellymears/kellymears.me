A **nap** is a short period of sleep during the day. Most mammals sleep in several bouts across a day, and humans are unusual in consolidating sleep into one long night; see [[Segmented Sleep]]. Naps are common in young children, in older adults, and in cultures with a midday rest such as the Mediterranean siesta.

Many people feel sleepy in the early afternoon, whether or not they have eaten lunch. This **post-lunch dip** comes from the interaction of two processes. Sleep pressure builds steadily from waking, while the circadian alerting signal has a small lull in the early afternoon before rising again toward evening; see [[Sleep Pressure]] and [[Circadian Rhythm]]. The dip is deeper after a short night, and its timing shifts with [[Chronotype]].

Length decides what a nap does:

- **Ten to twenty minutes.** Mostly light sleep. Alertness and reaction time improve within minutes of waking, with little grogginess. This is the classic **power nap**.
- **Thirty to sixty minutes.** Long enough to reach deep slow-wave sleep. Waking from it brings sleep inertia, a groggy, slow state that can last half an hour; see [[Sleep Stages]].
- **Around ninety minutes.** A full sleep cycle, ending in lighter sleep, with benefits for memory but a larger hit to night sleep.

A NASA study published in 1995 gave long-haul pilots a planned 40-minute rest in the cockpit; they slept about 26 minutes on average and showed markedly better alertness and fewer microsleeps late in the flight than pilots without one. Naps also help learning: a nap after study can protect new memories as well as part of a night's sleep; see [[Memory Consolidation]].

The **coffee nap** exploits timing. Caffeine takes about twenty minutes to take effect, so drinking a cup just before a short nap means it begins working as the person wakes, while the nap itself has cleared some of the sleep-inducing adenosine.

Naps are not free. A late or long nap releases sleep pressure that the night needed, which makes it harder to fall asleep. People with [[Insomnia]] are usually told to avoid naps for this reason. Habitual long daytime naps in older adults are associated with poorer health, but the arrow may point the other way: illness and poor night sleep cause the napping; see [[Sleep Apnea]].

Naps recover some, not all, of what lost sleep takes. They restore alertness but don't fully reverse the effects of chronic short sleep on mood and metabolism; see [[Sleep Deprivation]]. Shift workers and long-distance sailors use planned naps to manage night duty.

Workplaces have experimented with nap pods and quiet rooms. Japan's *inemuri*, dozing in public or at meetings, is often read as a sign of hard work rather than laziness.
