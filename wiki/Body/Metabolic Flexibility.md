**Metabolic flexibility** is the body's ability to switch fuels as conditions change: burning mostly fat at rest and during easy effort, shifting toward carbohydrate as intensity rises or after a meal, and back again. A flexible metabolism matches fuel to supply and demand. An inflexible one, common with [[Insulin Sensitivity]] problems, gets stuck burning carbohydrate even when fasting and handles neither fuel well.

The two fuels have different strengths. Fat stores are huge, enough energy for days of exercise, but fat is oxidized slowly and needs more oxygen per unit of energy. Carbohydrate, stored as [[Glycogen]], is limited but fast. As intensity rises, the body shifts from fat toward carbohydrate, a pattern known as the [[Crossover concept]]. The intensity at which fat burning peaks in absolute terms is called **FatMax**, typically around 60 to 65 percent of [[VO2 Max]] in trained people.

Endurance training raises fat oxidation. Regular aerobic work increases the number and density of [[Mitochondria]], the capillaries around muscle fibers, and the enzymes that break down fat. A trained athlete burns more fat at any given pace, sparing glycogen for later. This is a large part of the rationale for low-intensity training in [[Polarized Training]] and long sessions in the lower [[Heart Rate Zones]].

**Fat adaptation** pushes this further with diet. Eating very little carbohydrate, especially in a ketogenic diet that produces **ketosis**, forces the body to rely on fat and ketones, and fat oxidation rates during exercise rise dramatically, roughly doubling in some studies. In the 2010s this was promoted as a way to escape dependence on carbohydrate and avoid bonking.

The performance results disappointed. A series of studies led by Louise Burke with elite race walkers found that high-fat diets did raise fat burning but also made athletes less economical, using more oxygen at the same speed, and impaired performance at race intensities. The body also appears to lose some of its ability to use carbohydrate quickly when it's needed most; see [[Exercise Economy]]. For events decided at high intensity, carbohydrate remains the better fuel.

A more modest strategy survives: periodically training with low glycogen, called "train low," to amplify adaptation, while fueling well for hard sessions and races. Evidence for benefit is mixed, and doing it carelessly risks under-fueling and [[Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport]].

Flexibility also matters outside sport. Exercise, adequate sleep, and avoiding sustained overeating preserve it, and its loss is one of the early signs of metabolic disease, often years before blood sugar rises. Both [[Basal Metabolic Rate]] and [[Appetite Regulation]] interact with which fuels are available.
