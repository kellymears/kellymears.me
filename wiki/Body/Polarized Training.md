**Polarized training** is an approach to endurance training in which most sessions, roughly 80%, are done at low intensity, below the first [[Lactate Threshold]], and most of the rest at high intensity, above the second, with relatively little time in between. The physiologist Stephen Seiler described the pattern in the early 2000s by studying what elite cross-country skiers, rowers, runners, and cyclists actually did, rather than what training theory said they should do. Across sports and countries, they mostly trained easy.

The reasoning goes:

- **Easy volume drives the base adaptations.** More mitochondria, capillaries, fat oxidation, and a larger heart come chiefly from many hours of work. Low intensity is what makes those hours possible without wrecking recovery.
- **Hard work drives the ceiling.** Short, very hard intervals stress [[VO2 Max]] and the top end in ways easy riding never does.
- **The middle is a trap.** Moderate "tempo" efforts feel productive but cost much more recovery than easy work, without the specific stimulus of truly hard work. Seiler and others call this the *grey zone*. Amateurs left to themselves drift into it: easy days too hard, hard days not hard enough.

The evidence is supportive but not settled. Some controlled studies, notably Stöggl and Sperlich in 2014, found polarized programs beat threshold-focused or volume-only programs. Others found smaller or no differences, and many elite athletes train *pyramidally*, with more threshold work than a strict polarized model allows. Counting by time or by sessions also changes the percentages. The consistent finding is the large easy base. The exact split of the remainder matters less.

There is a [[Survivorship Bias]] caveat. Elite athletes train 15 to 30 hours a week, and someone with six hours may reasonably spend more of them at moderate intensity. Still, the core lesson carries over. Easy days have to be truly easy, judged by [[Heart Rate Zones]] or power rather than by feel on a good day, so that hard days can be hard. That is how a training plan avoids becoming a [[Hedonic Treadmill]] of medium-hard rides that leave someone tired and no fitter.
