**Functional threshold power (FTP)** is the highest average power, in watts, that a cyclist can sustain for roughly an hour. The concept was popularized by Andrew Coggan and Hunter Allen in *Training and Racing with a Power Meter* (2006) as a practical field stand-in for the [[Lactate Threshold]]. It is "functional" because it comes from riding a bike, not from a laboratory blood test.

Power meters made it possible. A strain gauge in the crank, pedal, or hub measures [[Torque]], and multiplying by angular velocity gives power directly, the actual rate of work. Unlike heart rate, which lags effort by a minute or more and drifts with heat, hydration, and fatigue, power responds instantly and means the same thing on every day. That made precise, repeatable intensity targets possible outside a laboratory for the first time.

Few people ride all-out for an hour to find their FTP. Common estimates:

- **A 20-minute test**, taking 95% of the average.
- **A ramp test**, where power climbs every minute until failure, taking about 75% of the best one-minute power.
- **Modeling** from hard efforts in ordinary rides, which most training software now does automatically.

Each method has error bars, and the ramp test in particular overstates FTP for riders with a strong anaerobic kick.

FTP anchors everything else. Coggan's training levels are percentages of it: recovery below 55%, endurance up to 75%, tempo up to 90%, threshold around 91–105%, and [[VO2 Max]] work above that. Intensity Factor and Training Stress Score, the inputs to [[Training Load]] models, are defined against it. Divided by body mass, it gives **watts per kilogram**, which largely determines climbing speed, since gravity scales with weight. On the flat, [[Aerodynamic Drag]] matters more and absolute watts win.

The number invites [[Goodhart's Law]]. Riders retest, chase the figure, and train for the test protocol rather than for the hours and terrain they actually ride. FTP says little about durability: how much of that power remains after three hours and [[Work and Power|2,000 kilojoules of work]]. For long events, durability often matters as much as the headline number.
