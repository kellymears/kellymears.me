**Goosebumps** are small bumps that rise on the skin when tiny muscles at the base of each hair follicle contract, pulling the hairs upright. The technical name is **piloerection**. The bumps resemble the skin of a plucked bird, which gave them their name in English and in many other languages.

The muscles, called arrector pili, are smooth muscle controlled by the sympathetic nervous system, the same branch that drives the stress response. So goosebumps aren't voluntary, and they appear with:

- **Cold.** In furry animals, raised hair traps a thicker layer of insulating air. Humans have too little body hair for this to warm them, which makes goosebumps a leftover; see [[Thermoregulation]] and [[Cold Exposure]].
- **Fear and threat.** Bristling fur makes animals look larger, the reason a frightened cat puffs up; see [[Fight-or-Flight Response]].
- **Strong emotion**, such as awe, nostalgia, or being moved.

The response may not be entirely useless. A 2020 study from Ya-Chieh Hsu's lab at Harvard found that in mice, the sympathetic nerve and the muscle that raise the hair also regulate the stem cells that regrow it, so cold that triggers goosebumps can also stimulate hair growth over time.

**Frisson**, also called aesthetic chills, is the experience of goosebumps and shivers in response to music, art, a speech, or an idea. Estimates of how many people experience it vary from about half to more than 80%. Music triggers it most often, at particular moments:

- **Sudden changes** in loudness or texture, such as a choir entering or a solo voice emerging.
- **Unexpected harmonies** or a melody returning in a new form.
- **Climaxes** after a long buildup.

In 2001 the neuroscientists Anne Blood and Robert Zatorre scanned people listening to music that gave them chills and found activity in reward circuits also engaged by food and sex. Later work found that dopamine is released both at the peak and in anticipation of it, consistent with chills marking a rewarding resolution of expectations; see [[Reward Prediction Error]]. Game composers deliberately structure music to build to such moments; see [[Adaptive Music]].

People who experience frisson often tend to score higher on openness to experience, and a 2016 study found they had more connections between auditory regions and those processing emotion. Being moved by something vast or profound overlaps strongly with awe, which often comes with chills; see [[Awe]].

Goosebumps are a visible signal from an internal state, one many people learn to notice; see [[Interoception]]. They come from the same system that controls sweating, heart rate, and blood vessels in the skin; see [[Autonomic Nervous System]] and [[Homeostasis]].

Some people can raise goosebumps voluntarily, a rare ability documented in a 2018 survey of people who reported it.
