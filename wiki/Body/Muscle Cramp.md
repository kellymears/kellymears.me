A **muscle cramp** is a sudden, involuntary, painful contraction of a muscle that does not relax on command. Calves, hamstrings, and feet are the usual sites. Cramps can last seconds or minutes, sometimes leave soreness for a day, and strike during exercise, at rest, or in the middle of the night.

Two explanations compete for **exercise-associated muscle cramps**:

- **Dehydration and electrolytes.** The traditional account holds that sweat losses of water and sodium make nerves and muscles excitable. It is the basis of most sports drink marketing and of the advice to take salt for cramps.
- **Neuromuscular fatigue.** The newer account holds that a tiring muscle's control loop goes out of balance. Muscle spindles, which signal stretch and drive contraction, become more active, while Golgi tendon organs, which signal tension and inhibit contraction, become less active. The motor neurons are left firing unchecked.

The evidence favors the second, at least as the main cause. Studies of endurance athletes generally find that those who cramp are no more dehydrated and no lower in blood sodium than those who do not. Cramps tend to strike muscles that are working hard, often near the end of a race or at an unusually high pace, and they stay local rather than appearing across the body as a fluid problem would. That fits fatigue and [[Training Load]] better than [[Fluid Balance]]. Plenty of people still swear by salt, a reminder to ask for a [[Plausible Mechanism]] and to separate [[Correlation and Causation]].

Two observations support the nervous-system account:

- **Stretching works.** Stretching a cramping muscle loads the tendon, activates the Golgi tendon organs, and inhibits the contraction within seconds. This is [[Proprioception]] doing the work.
- **Pickle juice works too fast.** A 2010 study found that a small swallow of pickle juice ended induced cramps in about 85 seconds, too quickly for anything to be absorbed into the blood. The likely route is a reflex from receptors in the mouth and throat that reduces motor neuron firing. Products built on spicy or sour compounds now try to trigger the same reflex.

**Nocturnal leg cramps** are a separate problem, common in older adults and in pregnancy. Their cause is unclear; shortened calf muscles from sleeping with pointed toes may contribute. Quinine was once a standard treatment, but the US Food and Drug Administration warned against it in 2006 because of serious side effects. Regular calf stretching before bed is the safest option.

Cramps are different from [[Delayed Onset Muscle Soreness]], the ache that arrives a day after unfamiliar exercise, and from the pain of [[Compartment Syndrome]], which builds and does not release. Cramps that come often without exertion can signal nerve, thyroid, or medication problems worth checking.
