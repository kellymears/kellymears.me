The **vestibular system** is the sensory apparatus, housed in the inner ear alongside the cochlea, that detects head position and motion and feeds that information into balance, posture, and gaze control. It shares an organ with hearing but not a function — vestibular damage impairs balance without necessarily touching hearing, and the two systems can fail independently even though they sit millimeters apart.

Two components split the sensing job. The **semicircular canals** — three fluid-filled loops oriented roughly orthogonal to each other — detect rotational (angular) head movement: fluid inertia lags behind the canal walls during a head turn, bending sensory hair cells and registering the rotation, direction included, because the three canals' different orientations let the brain resolve motion in any rotational axis. The **otolith organs** (utricle and saccule) detect linear acceleration and, critically, the constant pull of gravity — they contain small calcium carbonate crystals resting on a hair-cell bed that shifts under linear force, which is how the brain knows which way is down even with eyes closed and the head perfectly still.

The vestibular system drives the **vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR)**: eye movements that counter-rotate automatically to cancel head motion, keeping gaze fixed on a target while the head moves — the reason a page stays readable while walking but a photo taken mid-stride blurs, since a camera has no VOR to compensate.

**Motion sickness** is widely explained by **sensory conflict**: nausea arises when the vestibular system, the eyes, and proprioception disagree about what's moving — reading in a moving car has the inner ear reporting motion while the eyes, fixed on a stationary page, report none, a mismatch the brain apparently interprets as evidence of poisoning and responds to accordingly.
