**Relative energy deficiency in sport**, or RED-S, is the set of health and performance problems that follow when an athlete does not eat enough to cover both the energy cost of training and the energy the body needs to run itself. The key quantity is **energy availability**: dietary energy intake minus exercise energy expenditure, usually expressed per kilogram of fat-free mass. What is left over is what the body has for everything else, from repairing tissue to making hormones. When it falls too low, roughly below 30 kilocalories per kilogram of fat-free mass per day in research settings, the body starts cutting back.

The idea began with the *female athlete triad*, named in 1992 and formalized in 1997: disordered eating, loss of menstrual periods, and low bone density, seen together in female athletes. In 2014 the International Olympic Committee broadened it into RED-S, recognizing that it affects men too and reaches well beyond those three.

The body's response is triage. With too little energy to go around, it downgrades systems it can live without for a while:

- **Hormones.** Reproductive hormones fall, so periods stop and testosterone drops. Thyroid output falls, lowering [[Basal Metabolic Rate]].
- **Bones.** Without estrogen, testosterone, and enough energy, [[Bone Remodeling]] tilts toward loss, leading to stress fractures and, over years, low bone density that may never fully recover.
- **Immunity.** Frequent colds and slow healing, since the [[Immune System]] is metabolically expensive.
- **Performance.** Poor recovery, flat legs, falling power, and depleted [[Glycogen]], so the training produces fatigue rather than the adaptation described under [[Supercompensation]].
- **Mind.** Irritability, poor concentration, low mood, and sometimes depression.

Low energy availability is often unintentional. Long training days burn thousands of calories, hard exercise suppresses appetite, and athletes simply fail to eat enough. Sports that reward low body weight, such as distance running, climbing, and road cycling, add pressure. Male road cyclists turn out to be a notable risk group: the sport is non-weight-bearing, so it does little to load bone, and low weight is prized. Treating a weight target as the goal, rather than performance, is a version of [[Goodhart's Law]].

Diagnosis is difficult because no single test exists. Warning signs include stalled performance despite training, recurrent injuries, missed periods, low libido, frequent illness, and blood markers such as low iron, described under [[Iron Deficiency]], or low thyroid hormones.

The treatment is mostly to eat more, especially around training, and often to train somewhat less until hormones recover, with professional help when disordered eating is involved. Fueling long sessions with carbohydrate, eating enough protein, and adding [[Strength Training]] for bone are the standard preventive habits. The body treats energy as a budget, and a sustained deficit is paid for by its quieter systems.
