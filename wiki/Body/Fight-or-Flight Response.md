The **fight-or-flight response** is the body's rapid mobilization in the face of a threat. Within seconds of danger, real or perceived, the sympathetic branch of the [[Autonomic Nervous System]] fires, and the adrenal glands release adrenaline, also called epinephrine. The physiologist Walter Cannon named the response in 1915, describing it as preparation for violent muscular action.

The changes serve one purpose, delivering energy and oxygen to muscles fast:

- **Heart and blood.** Heart rate and the force of each beat rise, and [[Blood Pressure]] climbs; see [[Cardiac Output]]. Blood vessels in muscles widen while those in the skin and gut narrow, which is why people go pale.
- **Fuel.** The liver releases glucose from its stores, and fat is broken down for energy; see [[Glycogen]].
- **Breathing.** Airways widen and breathing quickens.
- **Senses.** Pupils dilate, attention narrows onto the threat, and peripheral vision and hearing can fade, which people describe as tunnel vision.
- **Shutdown.** Digestion, salivation, and other slow processes pause, which brings dry mouth and the stomach's butterflies.
- **Pain.** The body releases its own painkillers, so injuries may go unnoticed until later; see [[Runner's High]].

A slower hormonal wave follows through the [[Endocrine System]]: the brain signals the adrenal glands to release cortisol, which sustains the energy supply over minutes to hours. When the threat passes, the parasympathetic branch, much of it carried by the [[Vagus Nerve]], brings the body back down; see [[Heart Rate Variability]].

The full repertoire is wider than the name. Many animals freeze before they flee, staying still to avoid being spotted, and some go limp under extreme threat. The psychologist Shelley Taylor proposed in 2000 that people, especially women, also respond to stress by protecting others and seeking company, which she called tend-and-befriend.

Arousal helps performance up to a point and then hurts it. Moderate stress sharpens focus, but too much degrades fine motor control and complex thinking; see [[Yerkes-Dodson Law]] and [[Choking Under Pressure]]. Trembling hands and a blank mind during a presentation are the response doing its job in the wrong setting.

The system evolved for brief physical threats, and modern triggers are mostly neither brief nor physical. A tense meeting or a worrying message can set it off, and nothing is done with the mobilized energy. When it fires repeatedly, the wear accumulates; see [[Allostatic Load]]. A panic attack is the response firing with no external threat at all, and fear of the sensations themselves can keep it going; see [[Window of Tolerance]].

Slow breathing with long exhales is one of the few direct ways to signal safety back to the body; see [[Diaphragmatic Breathing]]. Exercise uses up the same mobilized resources and trains the system to recover faster.
