**Digital eye strain**, also called **computer vision syndrome**, is the cluster of symptoms that comes from long periods looking at screens: tired or aching eyes, dryness, burning, blurred vision, difficulty refocusing at a distance, headaches, and neck and shoulder pain. Surveys suggest most people who work at computers have some symptoms.

Several mechanisms combine:

- **Reduced blinking.** People blink about 15–20 times a minute normally, but often less than half as much while concentrating on a screen. Blinks also become incomplete. The tear film evaporates, leaving the surface of the eye dry and irritated.
- **Sustained near focus.** Looking at something close requires the eye's focusing muscles to contract and the eyes to turn inward. Holding that for hours fatigues the system, so distant objects may blur afterward.
- **Uncorrected vision.** Small uncorrected prescriptions or the age-related loss of near focus, starting in the forties, force extra effort.
- **Glare and contrast.** Reflections, a screen much brighter or dimmer than the room, and low-contrast text all add strain; see [[Color Contrast]].
- **Posture.** Screens too high, too low, or too close lead to neck and shoulder tension and wider eye opening, which increases evaporation.

Contrary to a common belief, there's little evidence that the blue light from screens damages the eyes or causes eye strain. Screen blue light is far weaker than daylight, and blue-light glasses haven't helped eye strain in controlled trials. Blue light in the evening does affect sleep timing, a separate issue; see [[Circadian Lighting]].

Practical remedies address the causes:

- **The 20-20-20 rule.** Every 20 minutes, look at something about 20 feet away for 20 seconds. It relaxes the focusing muscles and restores blinking; see [[Pomodoro Technique]].
- **Deliberate blinking** and, where needed, lubricating eye drops.
- **Screen placement.** Arm's length away, with the top of the screen at or slightly below eye level, so the gaze angles down and the eyes open less widely.
- **Matching brightness** between screen and room, and reducing reflections from windows and overhead lights.
- **Readable text.** Larger fonts, good contrast, and comfortable line lengths reduce effort; see [[Font Rendering]] and [[Line Length]].
- **Correct prescription**, including dedicated computer glasses for people who need help with near focus.

Some people are sensitive to the flicker of displays dimmed by rapidly switching the backlight on and off, which can contribute to headaches; see [[LED Flicker]]. Dark interfaces help some people and not others; see [[Dark Mode]].

Digital eye strain is temporary and doesn't damage the eyes. But a separate concern is that long hours of close work, particularly in childhood and with little time outdoors, are associated with developing nearsightedness; see [[Myopia]].

Long uninterrupted stretches of close focus strain more than the eyes; see [[Repetitive Strain Injury]] and [[Attention Restoration Theory]].
