**Detraining** is the partial or complete loss of training adaptations when exercise is reduced or stopped. Exercise scientists call it the principle of *reversibility*: the body builds capacity in response to demand, and when the demand disappears, it stops paying to maintain it. Fitness gained through [[Supercompensation]] is maintained only as long as the stimulus continues.

Adaptations fade at different rates:

- **Blood volume** goes first. Plasma volume can drop noticeably within days of stopping, which raises heart rate at a given effort and accounts for much of the early loss in endurance. It is also why [[Heat Acclimation]] fades within a few weeks.
- **[[VO2 Max]]** falls next. In trained athletes, several weeks without training typically cuts it by somewhere around 5 to 15 percent, more with longer breaks.
- **[[Mitochondria]]** and their enzymes decline over weeks, reducing the muscles' ability to burn fat and raising the pace at which lactate accumulates, as [[Lactate Threshold]] describes.
- **[[Insulin Sensitivity]]** worsens within days of inactivity, one reason short periods of bed rest affect metabolic health.
- **Strength** lasts longest. Muscle size declines over weeks, but strength is often largely retained for a month or more, partly because the nervous-system learning of [[Motor Learning]] persists.

The most dramatic demonstration is the **Dallas Bed Rest and Training Study** of 1966. Five healthy young men spent three weeks in bed, and their aerobic capacity fell by about 27 percent. Thirty years later, the same men were tested again, and the three weeks of bed rest had done more damage than three decades of aging. Astronauts face an extreme version, losing muscle and bone in microgravity despite daily exercise, the opposite of the loading that drives [[Bone Remodeling]].

Retraining is faster than first-time training, an effect popularly called muscle memory. Part of it is skill. Part may be structural: muscle fibers gain extra nuclei during growth, and some studies suggest those nuclei persist through detraining and speed later regrowth in [[Muscle Hypertrophy]], though this is debated in humans.

The practical good news is how little it takes to hold on. Studies from the 1980s found that cutting training volume by as much as two-thirds, while keeping the intensity, maintained endurance fitness for months. A taper before a race exploits this, as does [[Polarized Training]] with a few hard sessions holding the top end. A short break of a week or two costs little and can aid recovery, as [[Training Load]] models show.

Detraining matters most for older adults. An illness or hospital stay that would cost a young athlete a few weeks can push an older person into lasting decline, accelerating [[Sarcopenia]].
