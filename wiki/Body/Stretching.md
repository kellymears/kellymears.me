**Stretching** is moving a joint toward the end of its range and holding or repeating the position to improve flexibility, the range of motion a joint can achieve. It comes in several forms:

- **Static.** Holding a stretched position, typically for 15 to 60 seconds.
- **Dynamic.** Moving through a range repeatedly in a controlled way, such as leg swings and walking lunges.
- **PNF.** Proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation alternates contracting and relaxing the stretched muscle, often with a partner.
- **Ballistic.** Bouncing at the end of the range, generally discouraged.

Regular stretching does increase range of motion. Surprisingly, most of the short- and medium-term gain does not come from muscles getting longer. Studies point instead to **stretch tolerance**: the nervous system becomes willing to allow a greater stretch before producing discomfort, a change in the sensory side of [[Nociception]] and [[Proprioception]] rather than in tissue. Longer-term training, especially loading muscles in lengthened positions, can add actual tissue length.

Many popular beliefs about stretching have not held up:

- **Soreness.** Stretching before or after exercise has almost no effect on [[Delayed Onset Muscle Soreness]], according to systematic reviews.
- **Injury prevention.** Evidence that stretching prevents injury overall is weak and inconsistent. Some muscle strain injuries may be reduced in sports involving sprinting and kicking, but overuse injuries, such as a [[Repetitive Strain Injury]], are better addressed by managing [[Training Load]].
- **Warm-ups.** Long static stretches, held for more than about a minute per muscle, can briefly reduce strength and power. Short holds have little effect. Modern warm-ups favor dynamic movement that raises muscle temperature and rehearses the activity.
- **Cramps.** Stretching relieves a [[Muscle Cramp]] in progress, but routine stretching does little to prevent them.

Strength work through a full range improves flexibility about as well as stretching does, while also building strength, a point in favor of [[Strength Training]] with full range of motion. *Mobility* training blends the two, aiming for usable range that a person can control, not just passive range.

How much flexibility someone needs depends on their activity. Gymnasts, dancers, and martial artists need a lot; cyclists need enough to hold their position comfortably, and hamstring or hip tightness is often addressed alongside a [[Bike Fit]] rather than by forcing the body into one. More is not always better. People with hypermobility, joints that move beyond the typical range, often benefit more from strengthening than stretching, since stability is their limitation. Hypermobility is more common in autistic people and in conditions affecting [[Tendon and Ligament]] tissue.

Stretching is pleasant for many people and a reasonable way to unwind, and it does increase flexibility. It is simply not the protective ritual it was long thought to be.
