**Homeostasis** is the body's ability to keep its internal conditions stable while the outside world changes. Core temperature stays near 37°C in snow or summer. Blood pH holds between about 7.35 and 7.45. Blood sugar, sodium, oxygen, and carbon dioxide are each held within a narrow band. In the 1850s the French physiologist Claude Bernard called this stable inner environment the *milieu intérieur* and argued that keeping it constant is the condition of free life. The American physiologist Walter Cannon coined "homeostasis" in 1926 and popularized it in *The Wisdom of the Body* (1932).

Most homeostatic control is **negative feedback**, the same structure as a thermostat:

- **Sensor.** Something measures the variable: temperature-sensitive neurons in the hypothalamus, glucose-sensing cells in the pancreas, chemoreceptors tracking carbon dioxide.
- **Setpoint.** A target, or more accurately a range, the system defends.
- **Effector.** Something pushes back when the variable drifts: sweating or shivering for [[Thermoregulation]], insulin or glucagon for blood sugar (see [[Insulin Sensitivity]]), faster breathing when carbon dioxide rises.

The feedback opposes the change, so the system settles. Positive feedback, which amplifies change, is rare and used for events that should run to completion, such as blood clotting and childbirth.

Setpoints are not fixed. Core temperature follows the [[Circadian Rhythm]], lowest before dawn. In a [[Fever]], the immune system raises the temperature setpoint on purpose, which is why you shiver while already hot. Thirst and kidney function defend [[Fluid Balance]], but the target shifts with posture, heat, and exercise.

That flexibility led Peter Sterling and Joseph Eyer to propose **allostasis** in 1988: stability through change. Rather than waiting for errors, the brain predicts needs and adjusts in advance, raising heart rate before standing and releasing glucose before a race. It is the physiological side of [[Predictive Processing]], and [[Interoception]] is how the brain reads the results. The price of running those adjustments constantly, under chronic stress, is [[Allostatic Load]].

Training exploits homeostasis. Exercise deliberately disrupts it with heat, fuel depletion, and acid buildup, and the body responds by building capacity so the same disturbance matters less next time. That is the logic of [[Supercompensation]] and [[Hormesis]]. [[Heat Acclimation]] is a clear case: two weeks of heat exposure expands plasma volume and starts sweating earlier.

Engineers will recognize the patterns. A control loop with too much gain oscillates, and one with too little responds sluggishly, much as a system with [[Backpressure]] slows producers to keep a queue in range. Some illnesses are homeostasis failing at that tuning, such as diabetes, where blood glucose control loses its effector, and some are feedback working against the person, as in [[Central Sensitization]].
