**Herd immunity** is the indirect protection a population gains once enough people are immune to an infection that it can no longer spread widely. People who aren't immune, such as newborns, those too ill to be vaccinated, and people whose vaccine didn't take, are protected because the disease rarely reaches them.

The key number is the **basic reproduction number**, R0: the average number of people one infected person infects in a population with no immunity. If R0 is 3, each case causes three more, and the outbreak grows exponentially. As people become immune, the effective reproduction number falls. Once it drops below 1, each case causes less than one new case on average and the outbreak shrinks.

That gives a simple threshold. If a fraction of the population is immune, the effective number is R0 times the remaining susceptible fraction, so it falls below 1 once immunity reaches 1 − 1/R0.

- **Measles**, with an R0 of 12 to 18, needs about 95 percent immunity.
- **Polio**, with an R0 of 5 to 7, needs about 80 percent.
- **Seasonal flu**, with an R0 near 1.3, needs much less, but the virus changes each year.

Immunity comes from vaccination or from surviving infection; see [[Immune System]]. Vaccination reaches the threshold without the deaths and disability that infection would cause on the way. Smallpox was eradicated in 1980 by combining vaccination with targeted ring vaccination around each case, and rinderpest, a cattle disease, followed in 2011.

The simple threshold assumes people mix at random, and they don't. Unvaccinated people cluster in schools, congregations, and neighborhoods, so a country at 95 percent coverage can still have pockets where measles spreads freely; see [[Small-World Network]]. Spread is also uneven between people. A minority of infected people cause most transmission, so-called superspreaders, which makes outbreaks bursty and hard to predict; see [[Pareto Principle]]. The same mathematics describes rumors and data spreading through computer networks; see [[Gossip Protocol]].

Herd immunity is a public good, and it has the problems of one. Each person benefits from others' vaccination whether or not they vaccinate, so declining can look individually rational even as it erodes protection for all; see [[Free Rider Problem]] and [[Externality]].

Success hides itself. When a vaccine works, the disease disappears from daily life, and the vaccine's rare side effects become more visible than the illness it prevents. A 1998 paper falsely linking the measles vaccine to autism, later retracted, spread through repeated media coverage, and measles returned to places that had eliminated it; see [[Availability Cascade]].

In 2020 some proposed letting COVID-19 spread to reach herd immunity through infection; critics pointed to the deaths that would cost and to immunity that waned.

Fewer infections also mean fewer antibiotics prescribed; see [[Antibiotic Resistance]].
