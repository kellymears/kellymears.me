**Capsaicin** is the chemical that makes chili peppers taste hot. It produces no heat at all. It fools the nervous system by binding to a receptor on pain-sensing nerve endings that normally opens when tissue reaches about 43 °C, the point where heat starts to cause damage. The brain receives the same signal it would get from a burn, and responds with pain, flushing, sweat, and a running nose; see [[Nociception]] and [[Thermoregulation]].

The receptor, called **TRPV1**, was identified in 1997 by David Julius's laboratory, which used capsaicin as bait to find the gene for whatever it acted on. The work showed how the body converts temperature into nerve signals, and it shared the 2021 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Related receptors explain other chemical sensations: menthol triggers a cold receptor, which is why mint feels cool, and the compounds in mustard and wasabi act on another.

The heat is a defense, and it is aimed. Mammals chew seeds and destroy them, while birds swallow them whole and spread them in their droppings. The TRPV1 of birds does not respond to capsaicin, so birds eat chilies freely while mammals are deterred. Many pepper plants seem to have evolved to choose their seed dispersers this way.

Pungency is measured on the **Scoville scale**, devised in 1912 by the pharmacist Wilbur Scoville. A pepper extract was diluted in sugar water until a panel of tasters could no longer detect it, and the number of dilutions became the score. Bell peppers score zero, a jalapeño a few thousand, and pure capsaicin about sixteen million. Laboratories now measure the chemical directly, but report results in the old units.

Some practical consequences:

- **Water doesn't help.** Capsaicin dissolves in fat and alcohol, not water, so milk and yogurt work better.
- **Inflammation lowers the threshold.** Inflamed or sunburned skin makes TRPV1 fire at lower temperatures, which is why a warm shower stings after a day in the sun; see [[Sunburn]] and [[Inflammation]].
- **Tolerance is real.** Regular exposure blunts the response, partly by desensitizing the receptors and the nerves that carry them; see [[Habituation]].

That last effect has medical use. High-strength capsaicin patches can quiet overactive pain nerves for weeks in some kinds of nerve pain, by temporarily disabling the nerve endings; see [[Central Sensitization]] and [[Analgesic Ladder]]. Pepper spray uses the same mechanism in the other direction.

Why people seek out a sensation designed to repel them is less settled. One explanation, from the psychologist Paul Rozin, is "benign masochism," the enjoyment of a threat the mind knows is harmless, much like a roller coaster. Heat also carries flavor, combining with taste and smell into one experience; see [[Retronasal Olfaction]] and [[Umami]].
