**Sleep pressure** is the drive to sleep that builds steadily the longer you stay awake and drains away during sleep. It is one of two forces that decide when you feel sleepy. In the *two-process model*, proposed by the sleep researcher Alexander Borbély in 1982, *Process S* (sleep pressure) rises across the waking day, while *Process C*, the [[Circadian Rhythm]], swings up and down on a roughly 24-hour cycle regardless of how long you have been awake. You fall asleep easily when pressure is high and the circadian alerting signal has dropped, and you wake when pressure has drained and the circadian signal rises.

The circadian signal explains a familiar oddity: the second wind. After an all-nighter, pressure is enormous, yet people often feel more alert the next morning than at 4 a.m., because the circadian clock has started pushing for wakefulness. It also explains [[Jet Lag|jet lag]], in which pressure and clock disagree, a body still running on another [[Time Zone]].

Much of the pressure is carried by **adenosine**, a by-product of the brain's energy use. As neurons burn ATP through the day, adenosine accumulates outside cells and binds to receptors that dampen arousal-promoting circuits. Sleep, especially deep slow-wave sleep, clears it. Slow-wave sleep is concentrated early in the night for this reason, since that is when there is most pressure to discharge (see [[Sleep Stages]]).

**Caffeine** works by blocking adenosine receptors. It doesn't remove adenosine or the underlying need for sleep. It hides the signal. When it wears off, the adenosine that accumulated meanwhile is still there, which is the familiar caffeine crash. Caffeine's half-life averages around five hours but varies from about two to ten between people, mostly due to differences in the liver enzyme CYP1A2 that breaks it down, a case of [[Xenobiotic Metabolism]]. An afternoon coffee can still be half active at bedtime, delaying sleep and reducing deep sleep. Regular use produces [[Physical Dependence]]: the brain adds adenosine receptors, and quitting brings withdrawal headaches and fatigue for a few days.

Naps discharge some pressure. A short nap of 20 minutes or so restores alertness without much grogginess, while a longer one risks waking from deep sleep into sleep inertia and makes falling asleep at night harder. [[Segmented Sleep]], sleeping in two blocks with a waking interval between, is a pattern in which pressure and clock interact differently.

Low pressure and a misaligned clock are both reasons people can't sleep, and light exposure is the main lever on the clock side (see [[Circadian Lighting]]). Accumulated sleep loss degrades [[Working Memory]] and [[Executive Function]] before people notice, and it slows recovery from training, since adaptation (see [[Supercompensation]]) happens mostly during rest.
