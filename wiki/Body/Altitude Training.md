**Altitude training** uses the thin air of high elevation to provoke adaptations that improve endurance at sea level. The air at altitude contains the same 21 percent oxygen, but lower pressure means each breath delivers fewer oxygen molecules. At 2,500 meters, the pressure of oxygen is about a quarter lower than at sea level, a condition called *hypoxia*. The body notices and responds.

The key response comes from the kidneys, which sense low oxygen in the blood and release the hormone **erythropoietin (EPO)**. EPO tells bone marrow to produce more red blood cells, increasing the blood's capacity to carry oxygen, measured as total hemoglobin mass. Over weeks, this can raise [[VO2 Max]] and endurance once the athlete returns to lower ground.

Interest grew after the 1968 Olympics in Mexico City, at 2,240 meters, where endurance times were slow and athletes from high-altitude countries did well. The trouble with simply living and training at altitude is that thin air also limits how hard one can train, so athletes lose intensity even as their blood improves.

The answer was **live high, train low**. In a 1997 study, Benjamin Levine and James Stray-Gundersen had runners live at about 2,500 meters but descend to about 1,250 meters for hard sessions. They improved their 5,000-meter times, while those who lived and trained at sea level did not. Common guidelines today suggest living at roughly 2,000 to 2,500 meters for at least three to four weeks, with at least 12 hours a day at altitude. Where mountains aren't available, athletes sleep in *altitude tents* or rooms with reduced oxygen.

The effects vary widely. Some athletes respond strongly and others barely at all, partly because of genetics and partly because the stress interacts with iron stores, illness, and training. Iron is essential for building new red cells, so low iron blunts the response. Sleep at altitude is often poorer, with more awakenings and less deep sleep (see [[Sleep Stages]]), and recovery can suffer. Monitoring tools like [[Heart Rate Variability]] help catch excessive stress, and [[Training Load]] typically needs to be reduced in the first days.

Altitude also carries risks. Above roughly 2,500 meters, many people develop acute mountain sickness: headaches, nausea, and poor sleep.

Altitude training is a textbook case of [[Hormesis]] and [[Supercompensation]]: a stress that, dosed correctly and followed by recovery, leaves the body stronger. Heat produces some similar blood adaptations, through plasma volume expansion, and [[Heat Acclimation]] is sometimes called the poor athlete's altitude training. Synthetic EPO and blood transfusions, which produce the same end result directly, are banned in sport, and testing for them through the athlete biological passport, which tracks blood values over time, has been a central part of anti-doping work since the late 2000s.
