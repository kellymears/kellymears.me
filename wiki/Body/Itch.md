An **itch**, or pruritus, is an unpleasant sensation that provokes the desire to scratch. It is one of the most common symptoms in medicine, caused by everything from insect bites and dry skin to eczema, kidney and liver disease, and some medications. Brief itches are trivial, but chronic itch, lasting more than six weeks, can be as disruptive to sleep and quality of life as chronic pain.

For a long time, itch was considered a weak form of pain. Research since the 2000s has shown that it has its own dedicated nerve fibers and pathways in the spinal cord, distinct from those for pain, though the two systems interact closely; see [[Nociception]]. Itch can be triggered by **histamine**, released by immune cells in allergic reactions, which is why antihistamines relieve hives. But many chronic itches aren't driven by histamine at all, which is why antihistamines often fail for them.

Scratching relieves itch by producing mild pain, which suppresses itch signals in the spinal cord; see [[Gate Control Theory]]. Cooling, heat, and vibration can do the same. The relief is genuinely pleasurable, which helps explain why scratching is so hard to resist. Painkillers that block pain can make itch worse: opioids commonly cause itching, partly by removing pain's suppression of it.

This creates the **itch-scratch cycle**. Scratching damages the skin, which releases inflammatory signals that cause more itch, which leads to more scratching; see [[Inflammation]] and [[Immune System]]. In conditions like eczema, the cycle can thicken the skin and keep the disease going. Breaking it is a major goal of treatment, through moisturizers, anti-inflammatory creams, covering the skin, and habit-reversal training; see [[Habit Formation]].

Chronic itch also involves the nervous system becoming more sensitive. Nerves can begin to fire spontaneously, and light touch that wouldn't normally itch can trigger it, parallel to changes seen in chronic pain; see [[Central Sensitization]]. Newer drugs target specific immune signals that drive itch in eczema and have transformed treatment for many patients.

Itch is strongly affected by attention and suggestion. Reading about lice, or watching someone scratch, makes many people itch; this contagious itch has been observed in monkeys and mice as well as humans; see [[Social Proof]] and [[Nocebo Effect]]. Trying not to think about an itch can make it more noticeable; see [[Ironic Process Theory]]. Stress and anxiety tend to worsen itch, and distraction reduces it.

Itch likely evolved to protect the skin from parasites and irritants, prompting the body to remove insects and plants before they cause harm. That protective function becomes a burden when the alarm keeps sounding with no threat present, as in chronic pain; see [[Interoception]] and [[Sensory Processing]].

Some people scratch or pick at their skin compulsively, a pattern related to other body-focused repetitive behaviors; see [[Stimming]].
