**Cold exposure** challenges the body to keep its core near 37°C while losing heat to the environment. Water is the extreme case: it conducts heat roughly twenty-five times faster than air at the same temperature, so cold water chills the body far faster than cold air. Wind and wet clothing raise heat loss in air too, which is why a long descent on a damp bike can be colder than any ride up.

The body defends against cold through [[Thermoregulation]]:

- **Vasoconstriction.** Blood vessels in the skin and limbs narrow, keeping warm blood in the core at the cost of cold hands and feet.
- **Shivering.** Rapid involuntary muscle contractions turn chemical energy into heat, raising metabolic rate several-fold above [[Basal Metabolic Rate]], but quickly burning [[Glycogen]].
- **Brown fat.** A specialized fat tissue, packed with [[Mitochondria]], that releases energy directly as heat without shivering. It was long thought to exist only in infants, until imaging studies in 2009 found active brown fat in many adults, more in those regularly exposed to cold.

Sudden immersion in cold water triggers the **cold shock response**: an involuntary gasp, rapid breathing, and a spike in heart rate and blood pressure, lasting about a minute or two. It, not hypothermia, causes most cold-water drownings, since a gasp underwater inhales water. Safety guidance summarizes the timeline as roughly one minute to get breathing under control, about ten minutes of useful movement before cold muscles fail, and around an hour before hypothermia causes unconsciousness. The face plunging into cold water also triggers the *diving reflex*, a slowing of the heart through the [[Vagus Nerve]].

**Hypothermia** begins when core temperature falls below 35°C. Shivering, clumsiness, and confusion give way, as it deepens, to the cessation of shivering, slurred speech, and poor judgment, so victims often fail to recognize their own danger. Rewarming must be gradual and handled gently.

The body adapts to repeated cold. After as few as five or six immersions, the cold shock response is substantially blunted, a form of [[Habituation]], one reason open-water swimmers acclimatize before long swims. Cold acclimation is less dramatic and less studied than [[Heat Acclimation]].

Deliberate cold exposure, through ice baths and cold plunges, has become popular. Some claims hold up: it reduces perceived soreness after hard exercise, described under [[Delayed Onset Muscle Soreness]], and many people report improved mood. Others are overstated. Regular ice baths straight after strength sessions blunt gains in muscle size and strength, since the cold dampens the [[Inflammation]] and signaling that drive [[Muscle Hypertrophy]]. Cold as a mild stressor fits the idea of [[Hormesis]], but the dose and timing matter, and people with heart conditions should be cautious about the cardiovascular jolt.
