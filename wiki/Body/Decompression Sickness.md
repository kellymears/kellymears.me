**Decompression sickness**, known as **the bends**, is illness caused by gas bubbles forming in the body when surrounding pressure falls too quickly. It mainly affects divers returning to the surface, but also workers in pressurized chambers, aviators in unpressurized aircraft, and astronauts going on spacewalks.

The physics is the same as opening a bottle of soda. The amount of gas that dissolves in a liquid rises with the pressure of that gas above it; see [[Henry's Law]]. A diver breathing compressed air at depth absorbs extra nitrogen into the blood and tissues, more the deeper and longer the dive. If pressure drops slowly, the nitrogen diffuses back into the blood and is breathed out. If it drops too fast, it comes out of solution as bubbles inside the body; see [[Nucleation]] and [[Ideal Gas Law]].

Bubbles cause harm by blocking blood vessels, stretching tissues and triggering inflammation and clotting; see [[Inflammation]]. Symptoms usually appear within hours of surfacing. The most common is deep, aching joint pain, especially in shoulders and elbows. The name came from the stooped posture of sufferers. Skin rashes and itching occur. More serious forms affect the spinal cord, causing numbness or paralysis, or the lungs, causing breathlessness, sometimes called the chokes; see [[Watershed Blood Supply]].

The disease was first widely seen in the nineteenth century among workers in caissons, pressurized chambers used to dig foundations for bridge piers underwater. Workers building the Brooklyn Bridge in the 1870s suffered it, and some died. In 1908, the Scottish physiologist John Scott Haldane published decompression tables based on experiments with goats, introducing staged ascents with pauses that let gas leave the tissues gradually. Modern dive computers continue this approach.

Treatment is recompression. A patient is put in a hyperbaric chamber and pressurized, which shrinks bubbles and forces gas back into solution, while breathing pure oxygen to speed nitrogen removal. Prompt treatment usually leads to full recovery, though spinal cases can leave lasting damage.

Several factors raise risk. Flying soon after diving lowers pressure further, so divers are advised to wait twelve to twenty-four hours. A small hole between the heart's upper chambers, present in about a quarter of people, can let bubbles bypass the lungs' filter. Dehydration, cold and hard exercise also contribute.

Divers face other pressure-related problems. Air spaces in the ears and sinuses must equalize as pressure changes; see [[Eustachian Tube]]. Breath-hold divers rely on reflexes that conserve oxygen, and can get decompression sickness from repeated deep dives; see [[Mammalian Dive Reflex]]. Hyperventilating before a breath-hold dive can cause sudden blackout underwater; see [[Hyperventilation]]. Going up rather than down produces a different set of problems from low oxygen; see [[Altitude Sickness]].
