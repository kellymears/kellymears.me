The **menstrual cycle** is the recurring sequence of hormonal changes that prepares the body for a possible pregnancy. It is counted from the first day of bleeding, and in adults it typically lasts between about 24 and 38 days, with 28 as a common average rather than a rule. It is orchestrated by the brain's hypothalamus and pituitary gland signaling to the ovaries; see [[Endocrine System]].

It has two main halves, divided by **ovulation**:

- The **follicular phase** begins with **menstruation**, the shedding of the uterine lining. Follicles in the ovary mature, and rising estrogen rebuilds the lining.
- **Ovulation** occurs when a surge of luteinizing hormone releases an egg, usually around two weeks before the next period.
- The **luteal phase** follows. The empty follicle produces progesterone, which prepares the lining and raises resting body temperature by a few tenths of a degree, a shift used in fertility tracking; see [[Thermoregulation]]. If no pregnancy occurs, progesterone falls and the lining sheds again.

Many people notice symptoms in the days before a period, such as bloating, breast tenderness, mood changes and fatigue, grouped as **premenstrual syndrome**. A severe mood-centered form, premenstrual dysphoric disorder, is recognized separately. Falling estrogen before a period can also trigger [[Migraine]], and water retention shifts with the hormones; see [[Fluid Balance]].

The cycle is a sensitive gauge of energy balance. When training demands outrun food intake, the brain dials down reproductive hormones and periods become irregular or stop. That loss of periods is a central warning sign of [[Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport]], and the low estrogen it brings weakens bone; see [[Stress Fracture]]. Monthly blood loss is also why menstruating athletes are especially prone to [[Iron Deficiency]].

Whether athletes should train around their cycle phases is popular advice with thin evidence. A 2020 meta-analysis by Kelly McNulty and colleagues found that performance might be trivially reduced in the early follicular phase, but the studies were small and inconsistent, and the authors recommended individual approaches over general rules; see [[Training Load]]. The higher rate of knee ligament injury in female athletes has also been tentatively linked to cycle phase, without firm conclusions; see [[Anterior Cruciate Ligament Injury]].

A famous 1971 study by Martha McClintock reported that women living together synchronized their cycles. Later, larger studies mostly failed to find the effect, and many researchers now attribute it to chance overlap of cycles of differing lengths; see [[Replication Crisis]].

**Menopause** is the permanent end of menstruation, defined after twelve months without a period, at an average age of about 51. Estrogen falls sharply, bringing hot flashes and sleep disruption for many, and accelerating bone loss; see [[Osteoporosis]].
