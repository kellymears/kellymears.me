A **runner's high** is a state of calm well-being, mild euphoria, reduced anxiety, and dulled pain that some people feel during or after sustained exercise. Cyclists, swimmers, and rowers report it too. It tends to appear after twenty minutes or more of continuous, moderate-to-hard effort, rather than during short sprints or easy strolls.

For decades the standard explanation was **endorphins**, the body's own opioids. Blood endorphin levels do rise during prolonged exercise, and a 2008 brain imaging study found opioid release in emotion-related regions of runners' brains that correlated with their reported euphoria. But endorphins mostly don't cross the blood-brain barrier, so blood levels say little about the brain.

The newer explanation centers on **endocannabinoids**, the body's own versions of the active compounds in cannabis. Levels of one, anandamide, rise substantially during moderate running. Unlike endorphins, endocannabinoids cross into the brain easily. In a 2015 study, mice that ran on wheels became calmer and less sensitive to pain; blocking their cannabinoid receptors removed both effects, while blocking opioid receptors didn't. A 2021 human study found that people given an opioid blocker still experienced a runner's high. Both systems probably contribute, but cannabinoids seem to carry the euphoria.

An evolutionary explanation is that humans evolved as endurance animals, able to run prey to exhaustion. A system that makes long, moderate effort feel good and blunts its discomfort would have encouraged it. Supporting this, dogs, which also evolved to run long distances, show endocannabinoid rises after running, while ferrets, which didn't, don't.

The high is far from universal and hard to trigger on demand. It tends to be more likely when:

- **Intensity is moderate.** Around 70–80% of maximum heart rate seems to work better than very easy or all-out effort; see [[Heart Rate Zones]].
- **Duration is long enough.** Usually at least half an hour of continuous work.
- **Fitness is adequate.** If the effort feels like a struggle, discomfort can override the reward.

The pain relief is real and measurable, and is part of a broader capacity of the nervous system to turn down pain signals during stress or effort; see [[Gate Control Theory]] and [[Nociception]]. So injuries can go unnoticed until afterward.

Beyond the acute high, regular exercise reliably improves mood and reduces anxiety and depression symptoms, with effect sizes comparable to some treatments. Better sleep and improved [[Heart Rate Variability]] contribute. For some people, though, the pleasure of training can become compulsive, a problem that pairs badly with ignoring pain; see [[Supercompensation]] and [[Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport]].

The experience overlaps with absorbed, effortless focus during an activity; see [[Flow]]. It's also related to the "second wind" some endurance athletes describe, though that phrase often refers to shifts in metabolism and breathing; see [[Energy Systems]].
