**Gate control theory** is the proposal that pain signals pass through a "gate" in the spinal cord that other signals can open or close. Ronald Melzack and Patrick Wall published it in 1965, changing how pain was understood. Before it, pain was largely seen as a direct line: injury sends a signal, the brain feels it in proportion to the damage. Gate control explained why the same injury can hurt a lot, a little, or not at all.

The original model works like this. In the dorsal horn of the spinal cord, where nerves from the body connect to the spinal cord, small inhibitory neurons control whether pain signals continue upward to the brain:

- **Thin fibers** carry pain signals and tend to open the gate; see [[Nociception]].
- **Thick fibers** carry touch, pressure, and vibration, and tend to close it by activating the inhibitory neurons.
- **Signals from the brain** travel down the spinal cord and can open or close the gate too, depending on attention, emotion, and expectation.

That explains a universal instinct: rubbing a bumped elbow or shaking a stung hand. The touch signals from rubbing activate thick fibers that partly close the gate on the pain signals.

The theory led directly to **TENS**, transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation, in which a small device sends mild electrical pulses through the skin to stimulate touch fibers. Evidence for TENS is mixed, but many people find it helpful for some kinds of pain. Spinal cord stimulators, implanted devices for severe chronic pain, came from the same idea.

The details of the circuit have been revised many times. The real wiring is far more complex than the original diagram, and some specifics turned out to be wrong. But the central idea held up: pain isn't a simple readout of damage. It's shaped at multiple levels of the nervous system, including by the brain's own descending control. That's why:

- **Context matters.** Soldiers and athletes sometimes don't notice serious injuries until later; see [[Runner's High]].
- **Attention matters.** Distraction can reduce pain, while focusing on it can amplify it; see [[Selective Attention]].
- **Expectation matters.** Placebos can reduce pain partly by triggering the brain's own pain-dampening systems; see [[Placebo Effect]].
- **Emotion matters.** Anxiety and fear tend to open the gate.

Melzack later broadened the idea into the *neuromatrix* theory, in which pain is an output of the brain, generated from many inputs, rather than an input from the body. This view underlies modern understanding of chronic pain, where the system can stay sensitized long after tissue has healed; see [[Central Sensitization]] and [[Referred Pain]].

Gate control also explains some approaches to pain management that don't involve drugs, such as exercise, heat, massage, and cognitive therapies; see [[Analgesic Ladder]] and [[Cognitive Behavioral Therapy]].
