**Syncope** is a temporary loss of consciousness caused by too little blood reaching the brain. A person faints, falls, and usually recovers within seconds to a minute once lying flat restores blood flow. The near-faint, with lightheadedness, tunnel vision, muffled hearing, nausea, and sweating but no loss of consciousness, is called presyncope.

Fainting is common. Many people faint at least once in their lives, and episodes cluster in adolescence and old age. Most causes are benign, but a few are serious.

**Reflex syncope** is the most common type. A trigger causes the nervous system to suddenly slow the heart and widen blood vessels, so blood pressure falls and the brain briefly runs short; see [[Vasovagal syncope]] and [[Autonomic Nervous System]]. Triggers include:

- standing still for a long time, especially in heat;
- the sight of blood or needles, or pain;
- emotional shock;
- coughing, straining, or urinating.

Soldiers standing at attention on parade, and people waiting in long lines in the sun, are classic examples. Standing still lets blood pool in the legs because the muscles that normally squeeze it back toward the heart aren't working; see [[Fluid Balance]] and [[Heat Stroke]].

**Orthostatic syncope** happens on standing up. Normally a rapid reflex tightens blood vessels and raises heart rate to keep blood pressure steady; see [[Baroreflex]]. Dehydration, some medicines, alcohol, and certain nerve disorders blunt that reflex.

Athletes sometimes collapse just after crossing a marathon finish line. Running keeps blood flowing back from the legs, and stopping abruptly removes that pump while blood vessels in the muscles are still wide open. Walking slowly for a few minutes after finishing, rather than stopping dead, helps prevent it; see [[Cardiac Output]].

**Cardiac syncope** is rarer but more dangerous. Heart rhythm problems or structural heart disease can cause fainting without warning, sometimes during exercise. Fainting during exertion, with chest pain, or with a family history of sudden death warrants prompt medical assessment.

Many people with reflex syncope can prevent episodes. Counterpressure maneuvers, such as crossing the legs and tensing the leg, buttock, and arm muscles when symptoms begin, can raise blood pressure enough to stay conscious. People who faint at the sight of blood can learn applied tension, a technique combined with gradual exposure to feared situations; see [[Exposure Therapy]]. Fear of blood and needles is unusual among phobias because blood pressure first rises, then drops, rather than staying high as it does with most fear.

Fainting differs from vertigo, a false sense of spinning, though people describe both as dizziness; see [[Vertigo]]. Falls from fainting can cause head injuries; see [[Concussion]].
