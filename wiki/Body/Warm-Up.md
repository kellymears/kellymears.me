A **warm-up** is a period of lighter activity before hard exercise. Coaches have prescribed it for as long as there have been coaches, and here the evidence largely agrees with tradition: a good warm-up improves performance, especially in short, intense efforts. Its claimed role in preventing injury is less certain.

Several mechanisms are involved:

- **Temperature.** Working muscles heat up, and warmer muscle contracts faster and more forcefully, roughly 2 to 5 percent more power per degree Celsius. Nerve signals travel faster, and muscles and tendons become less stiff. This is the effect the name refers to; see [[Thermoregulation]].
- **Oxygen delivery.** At the start of exercise, the aerobic system takes a minute or two to ramp up, and the gap is filled by [[Energy Systems]] that produce lactate and deplete phosphocreatine. A warm-up raises [[Cardiac Output]] and oxygen uptake beforehand, so a hard start relies less on that limited reserve.
- **Neural readiness.** Rehearsing the movements of the event, strides before a race or a few hard pedal strokes, primes coordination and the sense of position from [[Proprioception]].
- **Potentiation.** A few brief, near-maximal efforts leave muscles temporarily able to produce more force, called post-activation performance enhancement, though fatigue cancels the benefit if overdone.

The right warm-up depends on the event. Sprints and short time trials need longer warm-ups with some hard efforts, because the event leaves no time to ramp up. Long endurance events need less, since the opening miles serve as a warm-up and saving [[Glycogen]] matters more. A gap of more than about 15 minutes between warming up and starting loses much of the temperature benefit.

Stretching fits in with nuance. Long static stretches right before explosive exercise can reduce power slightly, so warm-ups now emphasize **[[Dynamic stretching]]**, moving through the range under control. Any small performance cost from static stretching mostly disappears when followed by sport-specific activity; see [[Stretching]].

Psychological effects are real too. A familiar routine settles nerves and focuses attention, partly through expectation, which overlaps with the [[Placebo Effect]].

The **cool-down** is the warm-up's popular partner, and the evidence is less kind to it. An easy period after hard exercise helps clear lactate from the blood faster, but lactate clears within an hour anyway and doesn't cause soreness. Studies find cool-downs do little for [[Delayed Onset Muscle Soreness]], injury rates, or next-day performance. They do let heart rate and breathing settle gradually, which can prevent dizziness after hard efforts as blood pools in the legs, and they're a reasonable place for easy volume. Most of their reputation rests on habit.
