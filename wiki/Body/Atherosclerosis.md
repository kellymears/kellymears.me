**Atherosclerosis** is the buildup of plaque inside the walls of arteries. Over decades, deposits of cholesterol, immune cells, and scar tissue thicken the vessel wall, narrow the channel, and stiffen the artery. It is the underlying cause of most heart attacks and many strokes, and makes cardiovascular disease the leading cause of death worldwide.

The process starts with the lining of the artery. Cholesterol-carrying particles in the blood, especially low-density lipoprotein, or LDL, slip into the artery wall, particularly where blood flow is turbulent, such as at branch points. There the particles are chemically modified, and the immune system treats them as a threat. White blood cells move in and engulf them, becoming swollen "foam cells" that collect into a fatty streak; see [[Immune System]] and [[Inflammation]].

Over years, the streak grows into a plaque: a core of fat and dead cells covered by a fibrous cap. Some plaques grow large enough to restrict blood flow, causing chest pain during exertion when the heart needs more oxygen than the narrowed vessel can supply; see [[Cardiac Output]]. The more dangerous event is rupture. When the cap of a plaque tears, the blood clots on the exposed material, and the clot can block the artery within minutes. In a coronary artery the result is a heart attack; in an artery feeding the brain, a stroke.

The disease begins early. Autopsies of young American soldiers killed in the Korean War in the early 1950s found visible coronary plaque in a large share of men in their early twenties. Fatty streaks can appear in childhood.

Much of what is known about its causes came from the Framingham Heart Study, which began following residents of a Massachusetts town in 1948 and helped popularize the idea of a **risk factor**. Established risk factors include high LDL cholesterol, high blood pressure, smoking, diabetes, age, and family history; see [[Blood Pressure]] and [[Insulin Sensitivity]]. Because such studies observe instead of experiment, separating causes from things that merely travel with them took decades of further trials; see [[Observational Study]] and [[Confounding]].

Treatment and prevention target those factors. Drugs that lower LDL, first approved in the late 1980s, reduce heart attacks substantially in large trials, and the size of the benefit tracks how far LDL falls. Regular exercise improves several risk factors at once; see [[VO2 Max]]. Chronic stress and poor sleep add to the cumulative strain on the cardiovascular system; see [[Allostatic Load]].

Risk is best understood in absolute terms. A treatment that cuts relative risk by a quarter matters far more for someone at high baseline risk than for someone at low risk; see [[Relative Risk]].
