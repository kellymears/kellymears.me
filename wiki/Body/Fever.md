A **fever** is a regulated rise in body temperature, usually in response to infection. The body does not lose control of its temperature in a fever. It raises the target on purpose. That distinguishes fever from **hyperthermia**, such as heat stroke, where the setpoint is normal and the body is overwhelmed by heat it cannot shed.

The mechanism runs through the immune system. When immune cells detect an infection, they release signaling molecules called cytokines, among them interleukin-1, interleukin-6, and tumor necrosis factor. These prompt the brain to produce prostaglandin E2, which acts on the hypothalamus to raise the temperature setpoint. Fever is part of the wider [[Inflammation]] response.

The setpoint change explains how a fever feels:

- **Chills on the way up.** The body is suddenly colder than its new target, so it acts as if cold: shivering, clamping down blood flow to the skin, seeking blankets. You feel cold while your temperature rises.
- **Holding.** Once the new target is reached, [[Thermoregulation]] defends it, just as it normally defends 37°C. This is [[Homeostasis]] with a different setpoint.
- **Sweats when it breaks.** When the signal fades, the target drops back and the body is now too hot for it, so it sweats and flushes until it catches up.

Fever is ancient and widespread among animals, which suggests it is useful. In a 1975 experiment, Matthew Kluger infected desert iguanas, which regulate temperature by moving between sun and shade. They chose warmer spots, and those kept from warming up died more often. Higher temperatures slow the replication of many pathogens and speed some immune responses. The cost is steep: metabolic rate rises by roughly 10 percent per degree Celsius, which is part of why a fever is exhausting (see [[Basal Metabolic Rate]]).

Antipyretics such as ibuprofen and acetaminophen work by blocking prostaglandin production, lowering the setpoint. They are also painkillers, the first rung of the [[Analgesic Ladder]]. Treating a fever for comfort is generally fine for adults. They do little for heat stroke, because there the setpoint was never raised; cooling the body is the treatment.

"Normal" is less fixed than the 37°C figure suggests. Carl Wunderlich derived it in 1868 from over a million armpit readings, but modern averages run closer to 36.6°C, and temperature follows the [[Circadian Rhythm]], lowest before dawn and highest in late afternoon. Most guidelines treat 38°C or above as a fever.

For athletes the usual rule is not to train hard with a fever. It raises heart rate, lowers [[Heart Rate Variability]], and adds to [[Allostatic Load]], and some viral infections can inflame the heart muscle during intense exercise. Easy rest until the fever has been gone for a day or two is the conservative path.
