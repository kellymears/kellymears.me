**Iliotibial band syndrome** is an overuse injury that causes pain on the outside of the knee. It's one of the most common causes of knee pain in runners and a frequent problem in cyclists. The pain often begins at a predictable point in a run or ride, worsens with continued activity, and can become sharp enough to stop someone in their tracks. Downhill running, which keeps the knee slightly bent under load, tends to aggravate it.

The iliotibial band, often called the IT band, is a thick strip of connective tissue running down the outside of the thigh, from muscles at the hip to just below the knee; see [[Fascia]]. It helps stabilize the hip and knee.

For decades, the standard explanation was friction: the band was thought to rub back and forth over a bony bump on the outside of the knee as it bent and straightened, irritating it; see [[Friction]]. Anatomical studies have challenged that picture. The band is firmly anchored to the thigh bone and doesn't slide much. Many researchers now think the pain comes from the band compressing a layer of fat and nerve-rich tissue beneath it at a particular knee angle.

That shift has consequences for treatment. The IT band is extremely stiff, and studies suggest stretching or rolling it changes its length very little; see [[Stretching]] and [[Elasticity]]. Rolling may still ease pain or feel good, but it's unlikely to release the band itself.

Treatments with better support:

- **Reducing load.** Cutting mileage or intensity, avoiding downhill running, and then building back gradually; see [[Training Load]].
- **Hip strengthening.** Weak hip muscles may let the knee fall inward and increase strain; see [[Strength Training]].
- **Changing technique.** Taking shorter, quicker steps reduces load on the knee for some runners; see [[Cadence]].
- **Equipment.** In cycling, a saddle set too high or cleats rotated awkwardly are common contributors; see [[Bike Fit]].

Most cases settle within weeks to a few months with these changes alone. Injections may help in stubborn cases, and surgery is rarely needed.

ITBS belongs to a family of overuse injuries that appear when training rises faster than tissue can adapt. Others include shin splints and plantar fasciitis; see [[Shin Splints]] and [[Plantar Fasciitis]]. Like those, it often reflects a change in routine: a new race goal, more hills, new shoes, or a new bike; see [[Repetitive Strain Injury]].

Pain on the outside of the knee isn't always ITBS. Meniscus tears, ligament injuries, and problems with tendons around the knee can produce similar symptoms, and pain can be felt away from its source; see [[Tendinopathy]] and [[Referred Pain]].
