**Umami** is the savory taste, the fifth basic taste alongside sweet, sour, salty, and bitter. It is the taste of broth, aged cheese, ripe tomatoes, mushrooms, cured meat, soy sauce, and fish sauce. The Japanese chemist Kikunae Ikeda identified it in 1908. He traced the distinctive taste of kombu seaweed broth to **glutamate**, an amino acid, coined the name from the Japanese word for delicious, and patented a way to produce **monosodium glutamate (MSG)** as a seasoning.

Two more umami compounds followed: inosinate from dried bonito flakes (1913) and guanylate from shiitake mushrooms (1957), both nucleotides. Their most striking property is **synergy**. Glutamate combined with either nucleotide tastes far more intensely savory than either alone, much more than the sum. Classic pairings embody this without anyone knowing the chemistry: kombu and bonito in Japanese dashi, tomato and parmesan, meat and mushrooms. The receptor, a pair of proteins on the tongue, was identified around 2000, settling the question of whether umami was a true basic taste.

Glutamate is mostly locked into proteins, where it has no taste, and becomes tasteable only when released as a free molecule. Processes that break proteins down therefore build umami: aging cheese and ham, drying, slow cooking, and above all [[Fermentation]], which is why soy sauce, miso, and fish sauce are concentrated umami. Breast milk is rich in free glutamate. Umami combines with aroma via [[Retronasal Olfaction]] and with the roasted notes of the [[Maillard Reaction]] to make up most of what is called savory.

Western science was slow to accept umami as a basic taste, and MSG acquired a reputation that the evidence never supported. A 1968 letter to a medical journal coined "Chinese restaurant syndrome", and the idea spread rapidly. Repeated double-blind trials have not found a reliable effect at normal food doses, and the panic's specific focus on Chinese food, while glutamate-rich parmesan, tomatoes, and canned soups escaped suspicion, points to cultural prejudice as much as pharmacology. It is a textbook [[Moral Panic]], and reported reactions in people who believe they are sensitive fit the [[Nocebo Effect]].

*Kokumi*, a related Japanese concept, describes richness, depth, and mouthfulness from certain peptides that enhance other tastes without having a strong taste of their own. Whether it counts as a taste is still debated.
