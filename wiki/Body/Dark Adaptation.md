**Dark adaptation** is the eye's gradual gain in sensitivity after moving from light into darkness. Walking from a sunny street into a dim cinema, a person sees almost nothing at first; after a few minutes, the seats appear; after half an hour, they can see far more. Fully adapted, the eye is roughly a million times more sensitive than in bright light.

The retina has two kinds of light-sensitive cells:

- **Cones** work in daylight and provide color and sharp detail. They are concentrated in the center of vision.
- **Rods** are far more sensitive but see no color and less detail. They are absent from the very center and most dense a little way out.

Both rely on light-sensitive pigments that are bleached when they absorb light and must be regenerated. Bright light leaves rods almost fully bleached. In darkness, cones recover within about 10 minutes; rods take 30 to 40 minutes to reach full sensitivity. Plotting sensitivity over time shows a kink, the rod-cone break, where rods overtake cones. Adapting back to bright light is much faster, a matter of seconds to minutes.

Several familiar effects follow:

- **Night vision is colorless.** In true darkness only rods work, so the world turns gray.
- **Look slightly away.** Astronomers find faint stars by looking just beside them, since the center of vision has no rods.
- **The Purkinje effect.** Rods are most sensitive to blue-green light and nearly blind to red. At dusk, as vision shifts from cones to rods, red flowers fade toward black while blue and green ones seem brighter. The Czech physiologist Jan Purkinje described it in 1819.
- **Red light preserves adaptation.** Because rods barely respond to red, dim red light lets people read maps and instruments without losing night vision. Cockpits, submarines, and observatories have used it for this reason.

Adaptation slows with age, partly because older eyes let in less light and partly because the pigments regenerate more slowly. That's one reason night driving becomes harder; see [[Presbyopia]]. The rod pigment, rhodopsin, is built from vitamin A, and deficiency causes **night blindness**, still a major problem in parts of the world; see [[Retinoid]].

Screens and lighting interact with adaptation. A bright display in a dark room keeps the eyes adapted to the screen, making the surroundings seem darker and contrast uncomfortable; see [[Dark Mode]] and [[Digital Eye Strain]]. Evening light, especially blue light, also affects sleep timing; see [[Circadian Lighting]].

Games and films imitate the effect: when a character walks from darkness into light, the image blows out and slowly settles, a technique called eye adaptation or auto-exposure; see [[Tone Mapping]]. Perceived brightness follows a roughly logarithmic scale; see [[Weber-Fechner Law]].
