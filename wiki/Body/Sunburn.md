**Sunburn** is the skin's reaction to too much ultraviolet (UV) radiation, usually from the sun. The skin turns red, warm, and painful several hours after exposure, peaks within a day, and may blister and peel. Redness is only the visible sign of damage done to the DNA of skin cells.

Ultraviolet light lies just beyond violet in the [[Electromagnetic Spectrum]], with shorter wavelengths and more energy per photon than visible light. Two bands reach the ground:

- **UVB** causes most sunburn. Its energy is absorbed directly by DNA, fusing neighboring building blocks in a way that distorts the molecule.
- **UVA** penetrates deeper, contributes to skin aging and some cancers, and passes through window glass.

When cells detect DNA damage, they trigger an inflammatory response that widens blood vessels, causing redness and heat; see [[Inflammation]]. Badly damaged cells destroy themselves, which is why burned skin peels. Cells that survive with unrepaired damage can accumulate mutations that lead to skin cancer. Melanoma, the deadliest type, is strongly linked to blistering sunburns, especially in childhood.

Skin defends itself with **melanin**, a pigment that absorbs UV. Tanning is increased melanin production in response to damage, so a tan is a sign of harm already done, offering only modest protection. People with less melanin burn much more easily, while darker skin offers more protection but doesn't prevent damage entirely.

The **UV index**, introduced in the 1990s, forecasts the strength of UV radiation on a scale where 3 and above calls for protection. UV is strongest around midday, at high altitude, near the equator, and in summer. Snow, sand, and water reflect UV, and clouds block less of it than people expect. Thinning of the ozone layer, which absorbs most UVB, raised alarm in the 1980s and led to a successful global ban on the chemicals responsible.

Protection works in layers: shade, clothing, hats, sunglasses, and sunscreen. Sunscreens absorb or reflect UV and are rated by sun protection factor, which measures protection against burning under test conditions. In practice, people apply far less than the tested amount and get much less protection. Sunscreen users sometimes stay out longer, offsetting the benefit; see [[Risk Compensation]].

There is a trade-off with sunlight's benefits. UVB is also what lets skin make vitamin D, and some exposure helps set the body's clock; see [[Vitamin D]] and [[Circadian Rhythm]]. Brief, frequent exposure provides vitamin D without burning, and the amount needed is small for lighter skin.

Treatment for sunburn is supportive: cool compresses, moisturizer, fluids, and pain relief. Severe burns over large areas can cause fever and dehydration; see [[Fluid Balance]] and [[Thermoregulation]]. Some medications and skin treatments make skin more sensitive to sunlight; see [[Retinoid]].
