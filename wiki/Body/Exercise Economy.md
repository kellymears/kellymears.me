**Exercise economy** is how much energy an athlete spends to sustain a given speed or power. Two runners at the same pace can use noticeably different amounts of oxygen; the one who uses less is more economical and has more of their capacity left over. In running it is called **running economy** and measured as oxygen used per kilometer. In cycling, where power is measured directly, it is expressed as **gross efficiency**, the share of metabolic energy that reaches the pedals, typically 18 to 24 percent. The rest becomes heat, which is why [[Thermoregulation]] matters so much in long efforts.

Physiologists describe endurance performance with three factors: [[VO2 Max]], the size of the aerobic engine; [[Lactate Threshold]], the fraction of it that can be sustained; and economy, how far that sustained output carries you. Elite runners with similar VO2 max values can differ by 20 to 30 percent in economy, and economy often explains why one of them wins. A remarkable finding from studies of elite Kenyan runners was that many had unexceptional VO2 max values but outstanding economy.

What makes someone economical:

- **Tendon stiffness.** In running, the Achilles tendon and arch store energy on landing and return it like a spring; see [[Tendon and Ligament]].
- **Fiber type.** A higher proportion of slow-twitch fibers uses oxygen more efficiently; see [[Muscle Fiber Types]].
- **Body and limb mass.** Light lower legs cost less to swing.
- **Technique and coordination.** Wasted motion, from bobbing up and down to unnecessary muscle tension, costs energy. Years of practice refine movement through [[Motor Learning]], and [[Proprioception]] keeps it smooth.
- **Position and equipment.** On a bike, much of the economy is in the setup; a good [[Bike Fit]] and aerodynamics change how far a watt goes, though aerodynamics affects speed rather than metabolic efficiency.

Economy can be trained, though slowly. Heavy and explosive [[Strength Training]] improves running economy in many studies, likely by stiffening tendons and improving force production. Years of high volume refine it further. [[Cadence]] matters too: most people self-select a cadence close to their most economical one, and forcing a large change usually makes economy worse in the short term.

Economy is not fixed within a session. As an effort goes on, fatigue, rising temperature, and a shift toward recruiting less efficient fast-twitch fibers raise the energy cost of the same pace. That drift is part of why late-race pace becomes so hard to hold, especially as [[Glycogen]] runs low.

Shoes are the newest factor. Carbon-plated shoes with resilient foams, introduced around 2017, improved running economy by about 4 percent in laboratory tests, a step change in a sport where records had moved slowly. They work by returning more of the energy the foam stores on each step.
