The **endocrine system** is the body's slow messaging network. Glands release **hormones**, chemical signals carried in the blood, that act on distant tissues with matching receptors. Where the nervous system sends fast, targeted signals over wires, the endocrine system broadcasts: a hormone reaches every cell, but only cells with its receptor respond. Effects take minutes to months and coordinate growth, metabolism, stress, sleep, reproduction, and the balance of water and salt.

The major glands and some of what they do:

- **Hypothalamus.** A small brain region that links the nervous and endocrine systems. It senses temperature, blood chemistry, and time of day, and directs the pituitary.
- **Pituitary.** Releases hormones that control other glands, as well as growth hormone and antidiuretic hormone, which regulates [[Fluid Balance]].
- **Thyroid.** Sets the pace of metabolism, a major input to [[Basal Metabolic Rate]].
- **Adrenal glands.** Release adrenaline for fast stress responses and [[Cortisol]] for slower ones.
- **Pancreas.** Releases insulin and glucagon to control blood sugar; see [[Insulin Sensitivity]].
- **Pineal gland.** Releases [[Melatonin]] at night, signaling darkness to the body.
- **Gonads.** Release testosterone, estrogen, and progesterone.

Many tissues not usually called glands are endocrine too. Fat cells make leptin, the stomach makes ghrelin, bone makes hormones that influence metabolism, and the skin makes [[Vitamin D]]; see [[Appetite Regulation]].

Most hormones are controlled by [[Negative feedback]], the core mechanism of [[Homeostasis]]. The thyroid axis is typical: the hypothalamus signals the pituitary, the pituitary signals the thyroid, and rising thyroid hormone suppresses the first two signals. The same three-tier pattern runs the stress response through the [[HPA axis]] and reproduction through the gonadal axis. Feedback keeps levels within a range, but chronic stress can shift that range, which is part of [[Allostatic Load]].

Timing is part of the signal. Cortisol peaks shortly after waking, growth hormone surges in deep sleep, and melatonin rises in darkness, so many hormones follow the [[Circadian Rhythm]]. Disrupted sleep and jet lag disturb these patterns, and blood tests for some hormones only make sense at a specific time of day.

Hard training interacts with the system. Sustained low energy availability suppresses reproductive hormones and thyroid output, the endocrine core of [[Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport]], and in turn weakens [[Bone Remodeling]]. Short bursts of exercise raise growth hormone and testosterone temporarily, though these spikes matter less for muscle growth than once believed.

Endocrine disorders usually come from too much or too little of one hormone: type 1 diabetes, hypothyroidism, or Cushing's syndrome. Because hormones act everywhere, their symptoms are often vague and widespread, such as fatigue or weight change.
