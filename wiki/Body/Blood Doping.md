**Blood doping** is any method that artificially raises the number of red blood cells in an athlete's blood, increasing how much oxygen it carries to working muscles. Endurance performance depends heavily on oxygen delivery, so the gains are large: a few percent in time trials, more than years of training can add at the elite level; see [[VO2 Max]] and [[Cardiac Output]].

The share of blood volume made up of red cells is the **hematocrit**, normally around 40–50% in men and somewhat lower in women.

Methods:

- **Transfusion.** Blood is drawn weeks before competition, stored, and reinfused before the event, after the body has replaced what was taken. Using one's own blood (autologous) is hard to detect. Members of the US cycling team at the 1984 Olympics used transfusions, before the practice was banned.
- **EPO.** Erythropoietin, the kidney hormone that stimulates red cell production, became available as a drug in the late 1980s to treat anemia. It spread through endurance sport in the 1990s; see [[Altitude Training]] and [[Iron Deficiency]].
- **Altitude tents** and other hypoxic devices, which are legal in most sports, raise EPO naturally but less.

Professional cycling became the most notorious case. In 1998 French customs found a large stash of doping products in a Festina team car before the Tour de France, leading to raids and the team's expulsion. With no test for EPO, the sport's governing body in 1997 set a 50% hematocrit limit, which riders treated as a target. A urine test for EPO arrived in 2000, and riders shifted back to transfusions and smaller EPO doses that cleared quickly. In 2006 a Spanish investigation, Operación Puerto, found hundreds of bags of blood linked to athletes. In 2012 the US Anti-Doping Agency stripped Lance Armstrong of his seven Tour de France titles.

Detection moved from finding substances to watching for change. The **athlete biological passport**, adopted by cycling in 2008, tracks each athlete's blood values over time and flags patterns that are physiologically implausible, such as a hemoglobin rise without a matching rise in immature red cells. Plasma volume shifts from heat, altitude, and dehydration complicate the reading; see [[Heat Acclimation]] and [[Fluid Balance]].

Thicker blood carries risk. High hematocrit raises viscosity, making the heart work harder and increasing the risk of clots and strokes, especially when athletes are dehydrated or asleep with slow heart rates. A cluster of deaths among young cyclists around 1990 was widely suspected, though not proven, to involve EPO.

Doping has the structure of a social dilemma: each athlete gains by doping if others don't and loses by staying clean if others do; see [[Prisoner's Dilemma]]. Testing is an arms race much like cheat detection in games; see [[Anti-Cheat]].
