**Asthma** is a long-term condition in which the airways of the lungs become inflamed and overly sensitive. When triggered, the muscles around the airways tighten, the airway lining swells, and mucus builds up. Breathing becomes difficult, with wheezing, coughing, chest tightness, and shortness of breath. Attacks can range from mild to life-threatening.

Asthma affects several hundred million people worldwide. It often begins in childhood, though it can start at any age. Some children outgrow it, while adult-onset asthma tends to persist. Severity varies widely between people and within one person over time, and many people have long stretches with no symptoms at all between flare-ups.

Common triggers include:

- **Allergens** such as pollen, dust mites, mold, and animal dander; see [[Allergy]].
- **Infections**, especially viral colds.
- **Irritants** like smoke, air pollution, and strong fumes.
- **Cold, dry air**; see [[Cold Exposure]].
- **Exercise.**

At its core, asthma is a disease of the immune system. In many people, especially those whose asthma began in childhood, the airways show a type of inflammation linked to allergic responses; see [[Immune System]] and [[Inflammation]]. Why asthma has become more common in wealthy countries is debated. One idea links it to reduced early exposure to microbes; see [[Gut Microbiome]].

Exercise-induced bronchoconstriction, narrowing of the airways during or after hard exercise, affects many people with asthma and some without it. Breathing large volumes of air dries and cools the airways, which triggers narrowing. It's particularly common among elite endurance athletes, notably swimmers, who breathe chlorine-laden air, and cross-country skiers, who breathe cold, dry air. A gradual warm-up can produce a period during which the airways react less; see [[Warm-Up]].

Treatment mainly uses inhaled medicines:

- **Relievers** relax airway muscles quickly. Adrenaline was used historically, since it opens airways as part of the stress response; see [[Fight-or-Flight Response]]. Modern relievers act on the same receptors more selectively.
- **Controllers**, mainly inhaled corticosteroids, reduce the underlying inflammation over time.

International guidance shifted around 2019 away from relying on quick relievers alone, because overusing them without treating inflammation was linked to severe attacks. Newer biologic drugs target specific parts of the immune response in severe asthma.

In sport, asthma medicines raise questions of fairness. Some can only be used with official permission, and debates continue over whether high rates of asthma diagnoses in some elite teams reflect real disease or a search for advantage; see [[Blood Doping]].

Breathing retraining may help some people feel more in control of symptoms, though it doesn't replace medication; see [[Diaphragmatic Breathing]]. The airways' resting tone is set partly by the parasympathetic system, which can tighten them; see [[Autonomic Nervous System]].
