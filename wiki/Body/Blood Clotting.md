**Blood clotting**, or **hemostasis**, is how the body stops bleeding. It has to work within minutes after a cut, yet it must never start inside healthy vessels, so it is held in a tense balance between forces that build clots and forces that dissolve them.

The process runs in stages:

1. **Constriction.** The damaged vessel narrows, reducing flow.
2. **Platelet plug.** Platelets, small cell fragments in the blood, stick to exposed tissue under the vessel lining, activate, and recruit more platelets into a loose plug.
3. **Coagulation cascade.** A chain of clotting proteins, most of them made in the [[Liver]], activate one another in sequence. Each step amplifies the last, a deliberately self-reinforcing [[Feedback Loop]], ending in thrombin, which converts dissolved fibrinogen into strands of fibrin. The fibrin mesh traps red cells and turns the plug into a solid clot.
4. **Dissolution.** As the vessel heals, an enzyme called plasmin breaks the fibrin down, and repair proceeds; see [[Moist Wound Healing]] and [[Scar Tissue]].

Several clotting proteins need vitamin K to work. That dependence led to one of medicine's odder discoveries. In the 1920s, cattle in the northern United States and Canada bled to death after eating spoiled sweet clover. The culprit, dicoumarol, blocked vitamin K. Chemists in Wisconsin developed a stronger relative, first sold as rat poison and named **warfarin** after the Wisconsin Alumni Research Foundation, and it became one of the most widely used **anticoagulants**.

When clotting fails, bleeding is the danger. **Hemophilia** is an inherited lack of a clotting factor, carried on the X chromosome, so it mostly affects males. Queen Victoria was a carrier, and the condition spread through several European royal families. Bleeding under the skin after a knock is the everyday version; see [[Bruise]].

When clotting happens in the wrong place, it is called **thrombosis**. The physician Rudolf Virchow identified three contributors in the nineteenth century: slow blood flow, damage to the vessel wall, and blood that clots too readily. Long periods of immobility, such as a long-haul flight or bed rest after surgery, can let a clot form in a deep leg vein, a **deep vein thrombosis**. If it breaks loose and lodges in the lungs, it becomes a pulmonary embolism. In arteries, a clot forming on a ruptured plaque causes most heart attacks and many strokes; see [[Atherosclerosis]] and [[Stroke]].

Aspirin prevents platelets from clumping for the rest of their roughly ten-day life, which is why low doses are used after heart attacks, though the bleeding risk now outweighs the benefit for most people without heart disease; see [[Analgesic Ladder]]. Blood thickened by extra red cells clots more readily too, one of the dangers of [[Blood Doping]].
