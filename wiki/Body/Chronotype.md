A **chronotype** is a person's natural timing preference for sleep and activity: when they tend to get sleepy, wake up, and feel most alert. People range from **early birds**, or larks, who wake at dawn and fade early in the evening, to **night owls**, who come alive late and struggle to get up. Most people fall somewhere in between.

Chronotype reflects how a person's internal clock is aligned with the day. Everyone has a roughly 24-hour circadian rhythm, set by a master clock in the brain and adjusted each day mainly by light; see [[Circadian Rhythm]]. In people whose clocks run slightly longer than 24 hours, or respond more to evening light, everything shifts later.

Several factors shape it:

- **Genes.** Twin studies suggest that chronotype is substantially heritable, and large genetic studies have found hundreds of variants associated with being a morning or evening person.
- **Age.** Children tend to be early, adolescents shift sharply later, peaking around age 20, and adults drift earlier again with age. The teenage shift is biological, not just a habit.
- **Light.** Bright morning light pulls the clock earlier, and artificial light at night pushes it later. People camping without electric light shift earlier and converge toward a similar schedule; see [[Circadian Lighting]].

The main problem is mismatch with society's clock. Night owls forced to wake for early school or work accumulate sleep debt during the week and sleep late on weekends, a pattern called social jet lag because it resembles flying across time zones every weekend; see [[Jet Lag]] and [[Sleep Deprivation]]. Social jet lag has been associated with poorer mood, metabolic problems, and lower academic performance.

This has led to policy changes. Research showing that teenagers' clocks run late prompted many school districts to push start times later, and studies afterward found that students slept longer and had better attendance. Daylight saving time adds another mismatch, since clocks move while sunlight doesn't; see [[Time Zone]].

Chronotype also affects when people perform best. Alertness, reaction time, and some athletic performance peak at different times for larks and owls, and testing everyone in the morning disadvantages owls. Some research finds that owls are more often affected by [[ADHD]], which may partly reflect clashes with schedules rather than the condition alone.

The labels can be overstated. Chronotype is a spectrum rather than a set of types, and popular schemes that sort people into animal categories go beyond the evidence. Within limits, chronotype can be shifted with consistent morning light, regular meal times, and dim evenings, though strong owls rarely become larks. Sleep pressure, the drive that builds the longer someone is awake, interacts with the clock to set bedtime; see [[Sleep Pressure]] and [[Segmented Sleep]].
