**Reaction time** is the delay between a stimulus appearing and a person responding to it. For the simplest case, pressing a button when a light comes on, healthy young adults average roughly 200 to 250 milliseconds. Responses to sound and touch are somewhat faster than to sight, because the eye's chemistry is slower than the ear's mechanics.

The delay is made of several stages: the sense organ converts the stimulus into nerve signals, the signals travel to the brain, the brain detects and identifies the stimulus and selects a response, and a command travels down to the muscles, which take time to contract. Nerve conduction itself is a small part. In 1850 Hermann von Helmholtz measured it for the first time, finding speeds of tens of meters per second in frog nerve, far slower than the instant transmission many had assumed. Most of the delay is spent in the brain.

Measuring that brain time launched experimental psychology. In 1868 the Dutch physiologist Franciscus Donders compared simple reactions with ones requiring a choice between two lights and two buttons, and treated the difference as the time needed to make a decision. The approach, called **mental chronometry**, is still used to infer what the mind is doing from how long it takes.

Astronomy had found the problem earlier. In 1796 the Astronomer Royal dismissed an assistant whose timings of stars crossing a telescope's wire ran consistently later than his own. Decades later Friedrich Bessel showed that every observer had such a consistent offset, the **personal equation**, and observatories began correcting for it, one of the first recognitions of systematic human error in measurement; see [[Mean and Median]].

What changes reaction time:

- **Choices.** Time grows with the number of possible responses, roughly by a constant step for each doubling; see [[Hick's Law]]. Aiming at a target adds time that depends on its distance and size; see [[Fitts's Law]].
- **Practice.** Familiar, well-learned responses become faster and more automatic; see [[Motor Learning]].
- **Age.** Reactions slow gradually from the twenties onward, with variability increasing more than the average; see [[Sarcopenia]].
- **Fatigue and alcohol.** Lost sleep slows reactions and adds lapses, long delays and outright misses, which matter more than the average slowing; see [[Sleep Deprivation]] and [[Vigilance Decrement]].

The limits show up in rules and designs. In sprinting, a start less than a tenth of a second after the gun is ruled a false start, on the grounds that no one can genuinely react that fast. Road designers assume drivers need well over a second to perceive a hazard and begin braking. Games and interfaces are judged against human timing too, since a system's own delay adds directly to the player's; see [[Input Latency]], [[Response Time Limits]], and [[Esports]].
