The **lymphatic system** is a network of thin vessels and small organs that runs alongside the blood vessels. It does three jobs: it returns fluid that leaks out of the blood back into circulation, it filters that fluid through checkpoints where the immune system inspects it, and it carries fat absorbed from food.

Blood under pressure constantly seeps through the walls of the smallest vessels into the spaces between cells, bringing nutrients. Most is reabsorbed, but liters a day are not; see [[Fluid Balance]] and [[Blood Pressure]]. Lymphatic vessels collect this excess, now called **lymph**, and carry it through progressively larger channels that empty into large veins near the collarbones. Without this drainage, tissues would swell within hours.

Unlike blood, lymph has no heart. It moves because the vessels' walls contract rhythmically, because surrounding muscles squeeze them, and because breathing changes pressure in the chest; one-way valves keep it from flowing back. That is why swelling in the legs worsens with long periods of sitting and improves with walking; see [[Diaphragmatic Breathing]] and [[Cardiac Output]].

Along the way, lymph passes through **lymph nodes**, bean-shaped filters clustered in the neck, armpits, groin, and abdomen; a person has several hundred. Each is packed with immune cells. Fragments of bacteria, viruses, and damaged cells from the tissues arrive in the lymph and are presented to the cells that can recognize them, so a response can be launched; see [[Immune System]]. During an infection, nodes draining the area swell and grow tender as those cells multiply, the familiar "swollen glands"; see [[Inflammation]] and [[Fever]].

In the gut, specialized lymphatic vessels absorb fats from digested food, which are too large to enter blood vessels directly. After a fatty meal, the lymph from the intestine turns milky.

When drainage fails, fluid collects. **Lymphedema**, chronic swelling usually of an arm or leg, can follow surgery or radiation that removes or damages nodes, as in some breast cancer treatment. Worldwide, a common cause is a parasitic worm that blocks lymph vessels and causes the gross swelling called elephantiasis.

Cancer exploits the same routes. Tumor cells that break away often travel through lymph and lodge in the nearest nodes first, so surgeons examine the first node draining a tumor, the sentinel node, to judge whether the cancer has spread.

The brain was long thought to have no lymphatic vessels. In 2015 two research groups described vessels in the membranes surrounding it, and a related system clearing waste from brain tissue appears most active during sleep; see [[Sleep Stages]].

Massage marketed as "lymphatic drainage" has a real basis for treating lymphedema, but claims that it detoxifies healthy people are unsupported.
