**Muscle protein synthesis** is the process by which the body builds new proteins in muscle tissue. Muscle isn't static: its proteins are constantly broken down and rebuilt, with roughly 1–2% replaced each day. Whether muscle grows, holds steady, or shrinks depends on the balance between synthesis and breakdown over time.

Two main things stimulate synthesis above its baseline:

- **Resistance exercise.** Loading muscle against resistance raises synthesis for 24–48 hours afterward. This is the signal for growth; see [[Muscle Hypertrophy]] and [[Strength Training]]. Endurance exercise raises synthesis too, but mainly of the proteins in [[Mitochondria]] rather than contractile proteins.
- **Dietary protein.** Eating protein supplies amino acids, the building blocks of proteins, and triggers synthesis directly. One amino acid, **leucine**, acts as a signal that switches synthesis on.

Exercise and protein work together. Training without enough protein limits growth; protein without training does little for muscle mass.

Research has refined how much protein helps and when:

- **Total intake** matters most. For people trying to build muscle, a common evidence-based target is about 1.6 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day, with some benefit up to about 2.2. The general recommendation for adults, 0.8 grams per kilogram, is enough to avoid deficiency but not optimal for training.
- **Per-meal amounts.** Synthesis responds to protein meals up to a point, often cited as around 20–40 grams for most people, so spreading intake across several meals may help, though larger doses aren't wasted as once thought.
- **Timing.** The idea of a narrow **anabolic window**, a half hour after training when protein must be eaten, has been largely set aside. The window is hours wide, and total daily intake matters far more.
- **Quality.** Animal proteins are richer in leucine and more digestible; plant proteins work too but may need larger amounts or combinations.

Age changes the picture. Older muscles respond less to the same protein and exercise, a phenomenon called **anabolic resistance**, which contributes to [[Sarcopenia]]. Older adults seem to benefit from higher protein intake and from resistance training more than younger ones.

Energy matters too. During a calorie deficit, synthesis drops and breakdown rises, so higher protein and continued resistance training help preserve muscle while losing fat; see [[Body Composition]] and [[Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport]]. Sleep loss also impairs synthesis; see [[Sleep Deprivation]].

Inactivity has the opposite effect. Bed rest or immobilization cuts synthesis sharply, and muscle loss begins within days, one reason recovery from injury includes early movement where possible; see [[Detraining]].

Muscle damage and soreness after unfamiliar exercise aren't required for growth, despite the popular idea; see [[Delayed Onset Muscle Soreness]]. Gains depend on consistent loading over weeks, gradually increased, combined with enough protein and recovery; see [[Supercompensation]].
