**Hyperventilation** is breathing more than the body's metabolism requires. The problem isn't too much oxygen: blood leaving the lungs is already nearly saturated, so extra breathing adds little; see [[Pulse Oximetry]]. What changes is carbon dioxide. Overbreathing washes it out of the blood faster than the body produces it, a state called **hypocapnia**.

Carbon dioxide does more than wait to be exhaled. Dissolved in blood, it forms carbonic acid, so losing it makes the blood more alkaline, a shift called respiratory alkalosis; see [[pH]]. Carbon dioxide also controls the diameter of blood vessels in the brain. When it falls, those vessels constrict and brain blood flow drops, producing lightheadedness, blurred vision and a sense of unreality. The alkaline shift makes nerves more excitable, causing tingling around the mouth and in the fingers and, in stronger cases, cramping of the hands and feet into a characteristic claw; see [[Paresthesia]]. Some people faint; see [[Syncope]].

A cruel feature is that hyperventilation feels like not getting enough air. The brain's breathing drive is mostly set by carbon dioxide, and a sense of air hunger can persist even while someone is overbreathing. That makes it self-reinforcing in panic attacks. Fear speeds breathing, the symptoms that follow feel like a heart attack or suffocation, and the alarm speeds breathing further; see [[Fight-or-Flight Response]] and [[Interoception]]. Breathing into a paper bag, an old remedy, is now discouraged, because the same symptoms can come from asthma, a heart problem or a blood clot, where rebreathing would be dangerous; see [[Asthma]].

The calmer fix is slowing the breath, especially the exhale. Slow breathing from the diaphragm lets carbon dioxide recover and engages the calming side of the nervous system; see [[Diaphragmatic Breathing]] and [[Autonomic Nervous System]]. Some people with chronic anxiety develop a habit of mild overbreathing, sighing and breathing from the upper chest, and benefit from retraining it. Treatments for panic sometimes deliberately provoke the symptoms by having patients overbreathe, so the sensations lose their power to alarm; see [[Exposure Therapy]].

Breath-hold divers have used hyperventilation to extend their dives, and it's dangerous. Lowering carbon dioxide beforehand delays the urge to breathe, but it doesn't add meaningful oxygen. A diver can run out of oxygen and lose consciousness underwater before feeling any need to surface, a cause of drowning sometimes called shallow water blackout; see [[Mammalian Dive Reflex]].

At high altitude, faster breathing is a normal and useful adaptation. It raises oxygen intake in thin air, and the kidneys compensate for the resulting alkalosis over the following days; see [[Altitude Sickness]]. Hard exercise also drives breathing above metabolic need near maximum effort, helping buffer acid from working muscles; see [[Excess Post-Exercise Oxygen Consumption]].
