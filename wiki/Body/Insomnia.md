**Insomnia** is persistent difficulty falling asleep, staying asleep, or waking too early, despite adequate opportunity to sleep, with daytime consequences such as fatigue, irritability, and poor concentration. About a third of adults have occasional symptoms, and roughly one in ten meet the criteria for chronic insomnia: at least three nights a week for three months.

A common model, from the psychologist Arthur Spielman, explains it in three parts. **Predisposing** factors, such as a tendency to worry, make a person vulnerable. **Precipitating** events, such as illness, grief, or a new job, trigger poor sleep. **Perpetuating** factors keep it going after the trigger is gone, and they're mostly the person's own efforts to cope: going to bed earlier, sleeping in, napping, lying in bed awake, and worrying about sleep itself.

Two forces sustain it:

- **Hyperarousal.** People with insomnia show higher heart rate, metabolism, and brain activity at night, as if the body were on alert; see [[Window of Tolerance]] and [[Rumination]].
- **Conditioned arousal.** After many nights of lying awake frustrated, the bed itself becomes a cue for wakefulness. Many people with insomnia fall asleep easily on the couch or in a hotel.

The first-line treatment is **cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I)**, which works about as well as sleeping pills in the short term and better in the long term; see [[Cognitive Behavioral Therapy]]. Its components:

- **Sleep restriction.** Time in bed is cut to match the time actually spent asleep, then gradually extended. The mild deprivation builds [[Sleep Pressure]] and makes sleep deeper and more consolidated.
- **Stimulus control.** Use the bed only for sleep and sex; if awake for about twenty minutes, get up and return only when sleepy.
- **Cognitive work.** Challenge catastrophic beliefs about the effects of a bad night.
- **Consistent wake time.** A fixed rising time anchors the [[Circadian Rhythm]], even after a poor night.

Sleep hygiene advice, such as cutting caffeine and keeping the bedroom dark, helps healthy sleepers but rarely fixes chronic insomnia on its own; see [[Circadian Lighting]].

Sleeping pills help in the short term but lose effect and can cause rebound insomnia when stopped; see [[Physical Dependence]]. Alcohol helps people fall asleep but fragments the second half of the night.

Some people believe they barely sleep while sleep studies show near-normal sleep, a mismatch called sleep state misperception. Consumer sleep trackers can worsen anxiety about sleep, a pattern researchers have named *orthosomnia*; see [[Nocebo Effect]].

Waking in the middle of the night is not always a disorder. Historical records suggest people once routinely slept in two blocks with a waking interval; see [[Segmented Sleep]]. Insomnia can also mask other conditions, including a shifted body clock in night owls and [[Sleep Apnea]].
