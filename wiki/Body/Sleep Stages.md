Sleep isn't one uniform state. Through the night the brain cycles through distinct **sleep stages**, identified by brain waves (EEG), eye movements, and muscle tone. Each cycle lasts roughly 90 minutes, and a full night contains four to six of them. The overall pattern is called *sleep architecture*.

The stages fall into two kinds:

- **NREM stage 1 (N1).** The transition from waking, lasting a few minutes. People woken from it often deny they were asleep.
- **NREM stage 2 (N2).** Light but genuine sleep, about half the night. It is marked by *sleep spindles*, short bursts of fast activity linked to memory consolidation.
- **NREM stage 3 (N3).** Deep or *slow-wave sleep*, dominated by large, slow brain waves. It is hardest to wake from, and growth hormone release peaks during it. It discharges [[Sleep Pressure]], so it is concentrated in the first cycles of the night.
- **REM sleep.** Rapid eye movement sleep, discovered by Eugene Aserinsky and Nathaniel Kleitman in 1953. Brain activity resembles waking, and most vivid dreams happen here. Voluntary muscles are paralyzed, which keeps the body from acting out dreams. REM periods grow longer toward morning, so cutting sleep short cuts disproportionately into REM.

The stages have different jobs, though the details are still debated. Slow-wave sleep is associated with physical restoration and with consolidating facts and events, replaying the day's experiences from the hippocampus into longer-term storage. This is part of why [[Spaced Repetition]] across days works better than cramming. REM is associated with emotional processing and with integrating new information into existing knowledge. Both contribute to [[Motor Learning]]: skills practiced in the day often improve after a night's sleep without further practice.

Body regulation changes too. Core temperature drops as sleep begins, a reason a cool room helps. During REM, [[Thermoregulation]] is largely switched off: the body stops shivering or sweating. [[Heart Rate Variability]] rises during deep sleep as the parasympathetic nervous system dominates.

Disruptions alter the architecture. Alcohol helps people fall asleep but suppresses REM early in the night and fragments sleep later. Many sedatives produce sleep that looks different on an EEG from natural sleep. Waking during deep sleep produces **sleep inertia**, the heavy grogginess that can last up to half an hour, which is why alarms timed to lighter stages feel easier.

Consumer wearables estimate stages from movement and heart rate rather than brain waves. They track total sleep reasonably well but classify individual stages much less accurately than a lab study, so nightly stage breakdowns are best read as rough trends. The [[Circadian Rhythm]] sets when the night falls, and [[Segmented Sleep]] shows the cycles can be split into two blocks.
