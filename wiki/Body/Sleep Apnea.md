**Sleep apnea** is a disorder in which breathing repeatedly stops or becomes shallow during sleep. In the common **obstructive** form, the muscles of the throat relax, the soft tissue collapses, and the airway closes even as the chest keeps trying to breathe. Oxygen falls, the brain briefly rouses the sleeper enough to reopen the airway, usually with a gasp or snort, and the cycle repeats, sometimes hundreds of times a night, mostly without the person remembering.

Severity is measured by the apnea-hypopnea index, the number of pauses or near-pauses per hour of sleep: 5 to 15 is mild, 15 to 30 moderate, and above 30 severe. A 2019 estimate put the number of adults worldwide with at least mild obstructive sleep apnea near one billion, and most have never been diagnosed.

Snoring is the sound of air forced through a narrowed, fluttering airway, and it's the most common sign, though most snorers don't have apnea. Faster airflow through a narrow passage lowers pressure on its walls, pulling soft tissue further inward; see [[Bernoulli's Principle]].

Risk factors include excess weight, especially around the neck, a small jaw or large tonsils, older age, male sex, alcohol and sedatives that relax the throat, and sleeping on the back; see [[Body Composition]]. A rarer **central** form occurs when the brain fails to send the signal to breathe at all, seen in heart failure and at high altitude; see [[Altitude Sickness]].

Consequences come from fragmented sleep and repeated drops in oxygen:

- **Daytime sleepiness.** Sleep is broken into fragments with little deep sleep, so people wake unrefreshed however long they spend in bed; see [[Sleep Stages]] and [[Sleep Deprivation]]. Drowsy driving raises crash risk.
- **Cardiovascular strain.** Each event triggers a surge of stress hormones, raising [[Blood Pressure]] and the risk of heart rhythm problems and stroke; see [[Allostatic Load]].
- **Metabolic effects.** Apnea is linked to worse blood sugar control; see [[Insulin Sensitivity]].

Diagnosis uses an overnight sleep study, either in a lab or with a simplified home kit. The standard treatment is **CPAP**, continuous positive airway pressure, invented by the Australian physician Colin Sullivan in 1980: a mask delivers pressurized air that splints the airway open. It works when used, but many people find the mask hard to tolerate. Alternatives include oral appliances that pull the jaw forward, sleeping on the side, weight loss, and an implanted stimulator for the nerve that moves the tongue. In 2024 the US approved the weight loss drug tirzepatide for obstructive sleep apnea in people with obesity.

Apnea is often discovered by a bed partner, and is easily mistaken for [[Insomnia]], depression, or simple tiredness. Unexplained fatigue, morning headaches, and the urge to nap are reasons to ask; see [[Napping]].
