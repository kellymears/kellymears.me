**Cardiac output** is the volume of blood the heart pumps per minute. It is the product of two numbers: **heart rate**, beats per minute, and **stroke volume**, the blood ejected by the left ventricle with each beat. At rest an adult pumps about five liters a minute, roughly their whole blood volume. During hard exercise a trained endurance athlete can exceed thirty.

Because oxygen travels in blood, cardiac output sets much of the ceiling on aerobic performance. [[VO2 Max]] is cardiac output multiplied by how much oxygen the muscles extract from each liter, and in healthy people the heart's pumping, not the lungs or the [[Mitochondria]], is usually the limiting factor. Oxygen carried per liter depends on hemoglobin, which is why [[Iron Deficiency]] and [[Altitude Training]] affect endurance through the same route.

The two components respond differently:

- **Heart rate** rises almost linearly with effort up to a maximum that is largely fixed by age and genetics. Training barely changes it, which is why [[Heart Rate Zones]] are anchored to it.
- **Stroke volume** is trainable. Endurance work enlarges the left ventricle and expands blood plasma volume, so each beat moves more blood.

The **Frank-Starling law**, from work by Otto Frank and Ernest Starling in the 1910s, explains part of this: the more the heart fills before a beat, the more forcefully it contracts. More returning blood stretches the muscle fibers further, and the heart matches its output to its input without any signal from the brain.

These changes produce the **athlete's heart**. With a larger stroke volume, the same resting output needs fewer beats, so resting heart rates in the 40s are common in endurance athletes. Higher parasympathetic tone through the [[Vagus Nerve]] contributes too, and shows up as higher [[Heart Rate Variability]]. The adaptation is benign, though it can resemble disease on an ECG, and it partly reverses with [[Detraining]]: plasma volume falls within days, and stroke volume follows over weeks.

Cardiac output also explains **cardiac drift**. During a long effort in the heat, sweating reduces plasma volume and more blood is diverted to the skin for [[Thermoregulation]]. Stroke volume falls, so heart rate climbs to hold output steady, even though power stays the same. Good [[Fluid Balance]] and [[Heat Acclimation]], which expands plasma volume, both reduce it.

Blood pressure is cardiac output multiplied by the resistance of the blood vessels, so the two are linked; see [[Blood Pressure]]. During exercise, vessels in working muscle widen, which lets output rise severalfold while pressure rises only modestly.
