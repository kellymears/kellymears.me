A **sneeze** is an involuntary burst of air from the lungs out through the nose and mouth. Its job is to clear the nasal passages of irritants: dust, pollen, smoke, pepper, or the mucus produced by an infection. It's one of a family of protective reflexes, alongside coughing, blinking, and gagging.

The reflex starts in the lining of the nose, where nerve endings detect irritation and send signals along the trigeminal nerve to a sneeze center in the brainstem. The brainstem coordinates a sequence: a deep breath in, the throat closing as pressure builds in the chest, then a sudden release as the tongue and soft palate direct air through the nose. The eyes close reflexively. The whole sequence runs without conscious control, though people can sometimes suppress it once it starts; see [[Autonomic Nervous System]].

Many things trigger it. Allergens set off sneezing as part of an immune response, and seasonal allergies can produce sneezing fits; see [[Allergy]]. Viral infections inflame the nasal lining; see [[Inflammation]]. Chemical irritants work directly on nerve endings, which is why black pepper and chili sprays provoke sneezes; see [[Capsaicin]].

Some people sneeze when they step into bright light. The **photic sneeze reflex** affects somewhere around one in five to one in three people, varying by study, and runs in families. A collection of questions attributed to Aristotle's school already asked why the sun provokes sneezing. One explanation is that signals from the eye, adjusting to sudden brightness, spill over into the nearby nerve pathways that trigger sneezing; see [[Dark Adaptation]]. Its playful formal name has the acronym ACHOO.

Sneezes are efficient at spreading disease. A sneeze releases a cloud of droplets of many sizes. Larger droplets fall within a meter or two, while smaller ones can stay suspended in the air and travel farther, especially indoors; see [[Colloid]]. Often quoted speeds of a hundred miles per hour are exaggerated; measured speeds are far lower, but the cloud still carries a long way. Covering a sneeze with an elbow and staying home when ill slow the spread of infections through a population; see [[Herd Immunity]].

Holding a sneeze in by pinching the nose and closing the mouth traps the pressure, which has rarely caused injuries such as ruptured eardrums or throat tears; see [[Valsalva Maneuver]] and [[Eustachian Tube]]. People rarely sneeze during sleep, partly because the irritation that would trigger it doesn't register; see [[Sleep Stages]].

The sneeze belongs to a group of reflexes that feel compulsive and satisfying, along with the urge to scratch an itch and the unstoppable yawn; see [[Itch]], [[Yawning]], and [[Hiccup]]. Like those, it's surprisingly hard to produce on command.
