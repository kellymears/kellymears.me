The **vagus nerve** is the tenth cranial nerve and the longest, running from the brainstem down through the neck to the heart, lungs, and most of the digestive tract. Its name comes from the Latin for "wandering." It is the main pathway of the *parasympathetic nervous system*, the "rest and digest" branch that balances the sympathetic "fight or flight" branch.

The nerve carries signals both ways. Roughly 80 percent of its fibers are *afferent*, carrying information from the organs to the brain: stretch in the lungs and stomach, blood pressure, inflammation, and the state of the gut. This flow is a large part of [[Interoception]], the sense of the body's internal condition. The remaining fibers send commands outward.

Its best-known job is slowing the heart. The heart's natural pacemaker would beat at about 100 times a minute on its own; a resting heart rate around 60 to 70 reflects a steady vagal brake. When the vagus slows the heart a little during each exhale and releases it during inhalation, heart rate rises and falls with breathing. This rhythm is the main source of [[Heart Rate Variability]], which is why HRV is used as a window into vagal activity. Endurance training strengthens the brake, contributing to the low resting heart rates of athletes.

Other functions:

- **Digestion.** The vagus stimulates stomach acid, gut movement, and enzyme release, and relays signals of fullness.
- **Inflammation.** In 2002, Kevin Tracey described the *inflammatory reflex*, in which vagal signals damp the immune system's release of inflammatory molecules. See [[Inflammation]].
- **Swallowing and voice.** Branches control muscles of the throat and larynx.

Strong vagal activation can overshoot. In *vasovagal syncope*, the most common cause of fainting, a trigger such as the sight of blood, pain, or standing for a long time leads to a sudden drop in heart rate and blood pressure.

The vagus can be stimulated deliberately. Implanted *vagus nerve stimulation* devices were approved in the United States for epilepsy in 1997 and for treatment-resistant depression in 2005. Simpler techniques also engage it. Slow breathing with long exhales, at roughly six breaths per minute, increases heart rate variability and is a common component of relaxation practices, including [[Mindfulness]] and approaches to [[Emotional Regulation]]. Cold water on the face triggers the diving reflex, sharply slowing the heart.

Popular claims about "vagal tone" often outrun the evidence. Polyvagal theory, proposed by Stephen Porges in 1994, is influential in therapy circles, but many physiologists dispute its anatomical and evolutionary claims. The well-established picture is still notable: a nerve connecting brain and body in both directions, part of how chronic stress, described as [[Allostatic Load]], shows up in the heart and gut, and how calming the body can calm the mind.
