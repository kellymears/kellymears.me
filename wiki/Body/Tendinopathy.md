**Tendinopathy** is pain and impaired function in a tendon from overuse. It's common in runners (the Achilles and the patellar tendon below the kneecap), in racket sports (tennis elbow), and in desk and manual work (the wrist and shoulder). For decades it was called **tendinitis**, the suffix meaning inflammation, and treated with rest, ice, and anti-inflammatory drugs. Tissue studies from the 1990s onward changed that picture.

Biopsies of chronically painful tendons mostly show not inflammatory cells but disorganized collagen, increased ground substance, and new blood vessels and nerves growing into the tendon. This is a failed or incomplete healing response, sometimes called **tendinosis**. A widely used model by Jill Cook and Craig Purdam describes a continuum: a reactive stage after a sudden load increase, then disrepair, and eventually degenerative change. [[Inflammation]] plays some role, especially early, but it isn't the main story, which is why anti-inflammatories help less than expected.

Tendons adapt slowly. Their cells turn over collagen far more slowly than muscle remodels, and their blood supply is limited; see [[Tendon and Ligament]] and [[Watershed Blood Supply]]. So when training ramps up, muscles get stronger quickly while tendons lag behind, and the mismatch overloads them. Typical triggers:

- **Rapid change in load.** A jump in volume, hills, speedwork, or a new sport; see [[Training Load]].
- **Compression.** Tendons wrapping around bone, such as the Achilles at the heel, tolerate compression poorly.
- **Mechanics and setup.** A poorly set saddle or a sudden change in [[Cadence]] can load the knee; see [[Bike Fit]].
- **Systemic factors.** Age, some medications, and metabolic conditions raise risk.

The main treatment is loading, not rest. Complete rest weakens the tendon further, a form of [[Detraining]]. In the 1990s, Swedish surgeon Håkan Alfredson showed that **eccentric loading**, lowering a weight slowly while the muscle lengthens, such as heel drops off a step, relieved chronic Achilles pain in patients awaiting surgery. Later work found heavy slow resistance, both lifting and lowering, works about as well, so the key ingredient appears to be progressive load rather than the eccentric part specifically. The approach resembles [[Strength Training]] applied to a tendon, and fits the idea of [[Hormesis]]: the right dose of stress drives repair.

Pain guides the dose. A common rule allows pain up to about 3 or 4 out of 10 during exercise, provided it settles by the next morning. Recovery is slow, often three months or more. Long-standing cases may involve [[Central Sensitization]], where the nervous system amplifies pain beyond what the tissue state explains.

Tendinopathy overlaps with [[Repetitive Strain Injury]], the broader category of overuse problems in work and sport.
