Skeletal muscle is made of fibers that come in distinct types, distinguished by how quickly they contract and how they make energy. Every muscle contains a mixture, and the proportions shape what a person, or an animal, is good at.

- **Type I (slow-twitch).** Contract slowly with modest force, and resist fatigue almost indefinitely. They are packed with [[Mitochondria|mitochondria]], capillaries, and myoglobin, the oxygen-storing protein that makes them red, and they burn fat and carbohydrate aerobically. Posture muscles and the calf's soleus are rich in them.
- **Type IIa (fast oxidative).** Fast and fairly powerful, with decent aerobic capacity. They are the most adaptable type, and endurance or strength training pushes them toward one end or the other.
- **Type IIx (fast glycolytic).** The fastest and most powerful, relying on stored [[Glycogen]] and anaerobic energy. They fatigue within seconds to minutes.

The color difference is visible in food: chicken legs, used for standing all day, are darker than the breast, which powers short bursts of flapping.

Muscles recruit fibers in a fixed order, the **size principle**, described by Elwood Henneman in 1957. Small motor units of slow fibers are recruited first, and larger, faster units are added only as force demands rise. An easy ride runs almost entirely on Type I fibers, and a sprint calls on everything. That order is part of why easy training develops aerobic capacity without much fatigue, and why hard efforts drain glycogen fast. It underpins zone-based training (see [[Heart Rate Zones]] and [[Polarized Training]]): a rider holding [[Functional Threshold Power]] gradually brings in faster fibers as slower ones tire, a shift that shows up near the [[Lactate Threshold]].

Proportions vary widely. Elite distance runners commonly have around 70 to 80 percent slow-twitch fibers in their leg muscles, sprinters often a majority fast-twitch, and untrained people about half and half. The mix is largely inherited. Training changes it less than people hope, mainly shifting fibers between IIx and IIa, but training transforms what each fiber can do. Endurance work adds mitochondria and capillaries, raising [[VO2 Max]] and fatigue resistance, while strength work enlarges fast fibers. Age erodes fast-twitch fibers first, which is why power declines sooner than endurance.

Fast fibers are also more vulnerable to the strain behind [[Delayed Onset Muscle Soreness]]. Fiber type sets a range, and [[Training Load]] and [[Supercompensation]] decide where in it a person ends up.
