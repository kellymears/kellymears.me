**Diaphragmatic breathing** is breathing driven mainly by the diaphragm, the dome of muscle under the lungs, rather than by the muscles of the chest and neck. When the diaphragm contracts it flattens and pulls downward, the belly moves out, and air flows in; when it relaxes, air flows out. Chest breathing, with the shoulders rising, recruits accessory muscles and tends to be shallower and faster and is common under stress.

Breathing is unusual among bodily functions. It runs automatically, yet it can be overridden at will, which makes it the main voluntary handle on the [[Autonomic Nervous System]]. The heart speeds up slightly on each inhale and slows on each exhale, a rhythm called respiratory sinus arrhythmia, carried by the [[Vagus Nerve]]. Slow breathing with long exhales leans on that brake.

Common patterns:

- **Resonance breathing.** About six breaths a minute lines breathing up with the cardiovascular system's natural oscillation, which maximizes the swing in heart rate; see [[Heart Rate Variability]].
- **Box breathing.** Inhale, hold, exhale, and hold, each for a count of four. Popularized by military trainers as a way to stay steady under pressure; see [[Choking Under Pressure]].
- **Physiological sigh.** A double inhale through the nose followed by a long exhale, which people do spontaneously when crying ends. A 2023 Stanford study found five minutes a day of this cyclic sighing improved mood slightly more than mindfulness meditation; see [[Mindfulness]].
- **Nasal breathing.** The nose filters, warms, and humidifies air and adds resistance that slows the breath. Habitual mouth breathing is associated with snoring; see [[Sleep Apnea]].

Carbon dioxide, not oxygen, drives the urge to breathe. Overbreathing during anxiety blows off carbon dioxide, which narrows blood vessels in the brain and causes dizziness, tingling hands, and a feeling of not getting enough air, which feeds the panic; see [[Fight-or-Flight Response]] and [[Window of Tolerance]]. Slowing the breath reverses it. At altitude, faster breathing is the right response to thin air, though it brings its own side effects; see [[Altitude Sickness]].

The diaphragm also stabilizes the trunk. Lifters brace by breathing into the belly against a tight core, raising pressure inside the abdomen to protect the spine; see [[Strength Training]] and [[Low Back Pain]]. Holding the breath while straining spikes [[Blood Pressure]] sharply for a few seconds, which is why it is discouraged for people with heart disease.

Claims about breathwork often outrun the evidence. Slow breathing reliably shifts heart rate and perceived calm in the moment, but bolder promises, such as boosting immunity or curing disease, rest on small studies. Breath holds in water can cause blackout without warning.

Paying attention to the breath is a common entry point to sensing the body's internal state; see [[Interoception]].
