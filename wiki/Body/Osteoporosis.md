**Osteoporosis** is a condition in which bones lose mass and internal structure until they break easily, often from a fall from standing height or even a cough. It causes no symptoms until a fracture, typically of the hip, spine, or wrist. Worldwide, an estimated one in three women and one in five men over fifty will have a fracture caused by it.

Bone is living tissue that is constantly broken down and rebuilt; see [[Bone Remodeling]]. Through childhood and early adulthood, building outpaces removal, and bone mass peaks around age thirty. After that the balance slowly tips toward loss. In women, the drop in estrogen at menopause speeds the loss sharply for several years; see [[Endocrine System]]. Spongy bone inside the vertebrae and hip loses its internal struts first, and once a strut is gone it can't be rebuilt.

Diagnosis uses a low-dose X-ray scan called DEXA, which measures bone mineral density at the hip and spine. Results are given as a **T-score**, the number of standard deviations from the average healthy young adult; see [[Normal Distribution]]. A T-score of −2.5 or lower is osteoporosis, and between −1 and −2.5 is **osteopenia**, low bone mass. Density captures only part of fracture risk, so clinicians combine it with age, prior fractures, and other factors in risk calculators.

Risk factors:

- **Age and sex.** Older age, female sex, and early menopause.
- **Low body weight and underfueling.** Athletes who don't eat enough for their training lose hormones that protect bone; see [[Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport]].
- **Medications.** Long-term steroids are the most common cause of secondary osteoporosis.
- **Low vitamin D and calcium.** Both are needed for mineralization; see [[Vitamin D]].
- **Smoking and heavy drinking.**
- **Low impact loading.** Bone responds to force. Cyclists and swimmers, whose sports carry little impact, often have lower density than runners despite high fitness; see [[Stress Fracture]].

Prevention starts with loading. Bone adapts to the forces placed on it, so lifting weights and impact activities such as jumping and running build or preserve it; see [[Strength Training]]. Muscle loss and bone loss go together, and so does the frailty that makes falls more likely; see [[Sarcopenia]]. Balance training reduces falls; see [[Proprioception]].

Drugs can slow or reverse the loss. Bisphosphonates slow the cells that break bone down and reduce fractures substantially; newer drugs stimulate bone formation directly. Rare side effects of long-term bisphosphonate use, such as unusual thigh fractures, have made many people wary, though for those at high risk the prevented fractures far outnumber them.

Hip fractures are the gravest outcome. A large share of older patients die within a year, and many never regain independent living, which makes surgery within a day or two a priority; see [[Fracture Fixation]].
