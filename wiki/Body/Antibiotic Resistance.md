**Antibiotic resistance** is the ability of bacteria to survive drugs that once killed them. It is evolution by natural selection on a timescale people can watch. Any large bacterial population contains a few cells that happen to tolerate a drug. Treatment kills the rest, the tolerant ones multiply, and the next infection is harder to treat; see [[Survivorship Bias]] for the same filter in human reasoning.

Alexander Fleming, who discovered penicillin in 1928, warned in his 1945 Nobel lecture that underdosing would teach microbes to resist it. Within a few years, penicillin-resistant staphylococcus was common in hospitals. The pattern has repeated for nearly every antibiotic since. Methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus*, or MRSA, became the best-known superbug, and some strains of gonorrhea and tuberculosis now resist almost everything available.

Bacteria spread resistance in two ways:

- **Inheritance.** A mutation that helps survive a drug passes to every descendant, and bacteria can double in twenty minutes.
- **Horizontal gene transfer.** Bacteria swap small loops of DNA called plasmids, even across species, so a resistance gene that arises in a harmless gut bacterium can reach a dangerous one; see [[Gossip Protocol]] for an analogous spread through a network.

Every use of an antibiotic adds selection pressure, including uses that do no good. Antibiotics are often prescribed for colds and other viral infections they can't touch. Large quantities have gone into livestock, much of it historically to speed growth rather than treat disease. Each course also kills harmless bacteria in the gut, which can let resistant or dangerous ones take over; see [[Gut Microbiome]].

A 2022 analysis in *The Lancet* estimated that bacterial resistance directly caused about 1.27 million deaths in 2019, more than HIV or malaria.

The problem has the structure of a [[Tragedy of the Commons]]. Antibiotic effectiveness is a shared resource. Each prescriber and patient gains from using it, while the cost, resistance, is spread across everyone and the future; see [[Externality]]. The economics of new drugs make it worse. A new antibiotic is best held in reserve and used as little as possible, which means it barely sells, so most large drug companies left the field. Proposals pay developers a fixed subscription regardless of sales, as the UK began doing in 2022; see [[Free Rider Problem]].

Responses fall under **stewardship**: prescribing only when needed, choosing narrow drugs over broad ones, using the shortest effective course, and checking whether a recorded penicillin allergy is real; see [[Allergy]]. Vaccines reduce resistance indirectly by preventing infections that would have been treated; see [[Herd Immunity]]. Infection control in hospitals, including handwashing, slows spread.

Researchers are also reviving older ideas, such as phage therapy, which uses viruses that infect bacteria, and searching soil and other unusual environments for new compounds; see [[Fermentation]].
