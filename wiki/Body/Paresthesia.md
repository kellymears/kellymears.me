**Paresthesia** is an abnormal sensation of tingling, prickling, burning, or numbness without an outside cause. The everyday version is "pins and needles," felt when a leg or arm "falls asleep" after sitting or lying on it awkwardly. Most episodes are brief and harmless, but persistent paresthesia is a common early sign of nerve damage.

A limb falls asleep because of pressure on a nerve and the blood vessels that feed it. Nerve fibers need a steady oxygen supply to keep their electrical signals going. Compressed, they start to fail, roughly in order of size: the large fibers carrying touch and the sense of limb position go first, which is why a sleeping foot feels numb and clumsy and can give way when stood on; see [[Proprioception]]. When the pressure lifts and blood returns, the recovering fibers fire spontaneously and erratically for a minute or two, and the brain reads that disorganized activity as prickling.

Other everyday triggers:

- **Hitting the "funny bone."** The ulnar nerve runs exposed over the elbow, and a knock sends an electric jolt to the ring and little fingers; see [[Ulnar Neuropathy]].
- **Overbreathing.** Rapid breathing during anxiety or panic lowers carbon dioxide in the blood, changing its chemistry so that nerves become excitable, producing tingling around the mouth and in the fingers; see [[Diaphragmatic Breathing]].
- **Local anesthetic wearing off.** Numbness from a dental injection or a nerve block returns through a phase of tingling as the nerve recovers; see [[Regional Nerve Block]].
- **Cold.** Chilled fingers tingle as they warm, and frozen ones may stay numb; see [[Frostbite]].

Cyclists know a long-term version. Pressure on the palms from handlebars compresses the ulnar nerve at the wrist and numbs the outer fingers, and pressure from the saddle can numb the groin. Both usually respond to changing position, padding, or fit; see [[Bike Fit]] and [[Posture]]. Office workers get a similar pattern from the median nerve at the wrist, carpal tunnel syndrome; see [[Repetitive Strain Injury]].

Paresthesia that persists or keeps returning deserves attention. Diabetes damages small nerves in the feet first, so tingling in both feet is often an early sign; see [[Insulin Sensitivity]]. Vitamin B12 deficiency, some medications, and conditions like multiple sclerosis can cause it too, and a single numb patch can point to a pinched nerve root in the spine; see [[Low Back Pain]] and [[Referred Pain]].

Tingling shows that the nervous system builds sensation from patterns of activity, not from direct contact with the world. Nerves that fire without a stimulus produce real feelings, a principle seen at its extreme in sensations from a missing limb; see [[Phantom Limb]] and [[Nociception]].
