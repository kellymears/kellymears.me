**A blister** is a pocket of fluid between layers of skin. The most familiar kind, the friction blister, forms when skin is rubbed repeatedly, on heels in new shoes, on palms gripping oars or tools, or on toes during long walks. Blisters can also come from burns, including sunburn, from freezing, and from some infections and skin diseases; see [[Sunburn]] and [[Frostbite]].

Friction blisters don't come from the skin surface rubbing away. When something grips the skin and moves back and forth, the outer layer moves with it while deeper layers stay put. The resulting shear tears cells apart within the upper skin, and the gap fills with a clear fluid similar to blood plasma; see [[Friction]] and [[Fluid Balance]].

Several factors raise the risk:

- **Moisture.** Damp skin tends to grip socks and shoes more than either very dry or soaked skin, which is why sweat makes blisters more likely.
- **Heat**, which makes skin softer and more fragile.
- **Fit.** Shoes that are too loose let feet slide, and ones that are too tight press.
- **Repetition.** A long hike, marathon, or day of raking puts the same spot under thousands of cycles.

A **hot spot**, a red, tender, warm area, is the warning sign. Stopping to cover it with tape or padding can prevent a blister forming.

With gradual exposure, skin adapts by thickening its outer layer into a callus, a hardened patch that resists friction; see [[Hormesis]]. That's why hands toughen with regular climbing or rowing, and why feet break in to new shoes. Calluses can themselves cause trouble if they grow thick, since blisters may form beneath them. Building up gradually gives skin time to adapt, as with other tissues; see [[Training Load]].

Prevention focuses on reducing friction and moisture: well-fitting shoes, moisture-wicking socks, sometimes two layers of socks, lubricants or powders, and tape over vulnerable spots. Cyclists face a related problem with saddle sores, where pressure and friction combine; see [[Bike Fit]].

For treatment, most advice is to leave small blisters alone. The roof of skin is a natural dressing, keeping the wound clean and moist while new skin grows underneath; see [[Moist Wound Healing]]. Large, painful blisters can be drained with a sterile needle while leaving the roof in place. If the roof tears off, covering the raw area helps it heal.

Signs of infection include spreading redness, warmth, pus, and red streaks running from the blister, which are reasons to seek care; see [[Inflammation]] and [[Lymphatic System]]. People with diabetes or reduced sensation in the feet need to take blisters more seriously, since unnoticed injuries can become ulcers; see [[Paresthesia]].
