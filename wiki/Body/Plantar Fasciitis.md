**Plantar fasciitis** is pain at the bottom of the heel, caused by overload of the plantar fascia, the thick band of connective tissue running from the heel bone to the base of the toes. It is the most common cause of heel pain in adults, affecting roughly one in ten people at some point, most often between the ages of 40 and 60, and common among runners; see [[Fascia]].

The plantar fascia supports the arch of the foot. As the toes bend back at the end of each step, it tightens like a cable, stiffening the foot for push-off. Repeated load beyond what the tissue can handle leads to damage at its attachment on the heel.

The hallmark symptom is pain with the first steps in the morning or after sitting, which eases after a few minutes of walking, then returns after long periods on the feet.

Despite the name, it is mostly not an inflammation. Biopsies of long-standing cases show degeneration: disorganized collagen, thickening, and small tears, with few inflammatory cells. Many clinicians prefer the term plantar fasciopathy, in line with the way overuse tendon problems are now understood; see [[Tendinopathy]] and [[Inflammation]].

Risk factors:

- **Sudden increases** in walking, running, or standing; see [[Training Load]] and [[Repetitive Strain Injury]].
- **Body weight**, which raises load on each step.
- **Tight calves** and limited ankle flexibility.
- **Foot shape**, both very flat and very high arches.
- **Long hours standing** on hard floors.

A **heel spur**, a bony growth on the heel bone, often appears on X-rays of people with heel pain, but many people have spurs without pain, and the spur isn't the cause; see [[Bone Remodeling]].

Treatment is usually conservative, and most cases resolve within a year:

- **Load management.** Reduce aggravating activity without stopping movement entirely.
- **Strengthening.** A 2014 trial by Michael Rathleff and colleagues found that slow, heavy calf raises with the toes propped up on a towel, done every other day, reduced pain faster than stretching alone; see [[Strength Training]].
- **Stretching** of the calf and the fascia itself, especially before the first steps of the day; see [[Stretching]].
- **Supportive footwear** and insoles, taping, and night splints for some people.
- **Shockwave therapy** or injections for stubborn cases. Steroid injections relieve pain short term but may weaken the fascia and risk rupture.

Pain that persists for a long time can become partly a matter of a sensitized nervous system rather than tissue alone; see [[Central Sensitization]]. Heel pain that is worse with impact, more localized to the bone, or worsening rather than improving after the first steps may be a stress fracture instead; see [[Stress Fracture]].
