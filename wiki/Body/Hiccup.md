A **hiccup** is a sudden, involuntary contraction of the diaphragm and the muscles between the ribs, followed a fraction of a second later by the vocal cords snapping shut. The contraction pulls air in; the closing cuts it off, producing the familiar sound. Hiccups usually come in bouts, at a fairly steady rhythm, and stop on their own within minutes.

The reflex runs through a loop of nerves. Signals travel in along the [[Vagus Nerve]] and other nerves from the throat, chest, and stomach, are processed in the brainstem, and go out along the phrenic nerve to the diaphragm; see [[Autonomic Nervous System]]. Anything that irritates part of that loop can set it off: eating quickly, a stretched stomach, carbonated drinks, alcohol, a sudden temperature change, or excitement.

Hiccups appear before birth. Ultrasound shows them in fetuses by the end of the first trimester, and newborns hiccup far more than adults. That has made the reflex a puzzle, because it seems to do nothing useful. Two leading hypotheses:

- **A fossil of gill breathing.** Tadpoles pump water over their gills with a motion that draws water in and then closes the glottis to keep it out of the lungs, controlled by brainstem circuitry similar to what generates hiccups. On this view, the reflex is a leftover pattern generator from ancestors that breathed both ways.
- **A burping aid.** Infants swallow air while nursing. A hiccup may pull that air up out of the stomach, freeing room for milk, which would explain why the reflex is strongest in early life.

Neither has been settled.

Folk remedies are many: holding the breath, drinking from the far side of a glass, a fright, a spoonful of sugar, pulling on the tongue. Some have a plausible basis. Holding the breath raises carbon dioxide in the blood, which suppresses the reflex, and swallowing and gagging stimulate the same nerves that carry it; see [[Diaphragmatic Breathing]]. But hiccups stop on their own, so whatever was tried last gets the credit, and few remedies have been tested fairly; see [[Regression to the Mean]] and [[Placebo Effect]].

Hiccups that last more than two days are called persistent, and more than a month intractable. They can signal irritation along the nerve pathway, from a stomach problem to a tumor or stroke, and they can cause exhaustion, weight loss, and lost sleep. The record is usually given to Charles Osborne, an Iowa farmer who hiccupped for about 68 years, from 1922 to 1990.

The diaphragm has other ways of misbehaving during exercise. Cramping and stabbing pains under the ribs are related problems with different causes; see [[Side Stitch]] and [[Muscle Cramp]].
