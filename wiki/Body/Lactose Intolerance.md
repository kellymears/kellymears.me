**Lactose intolerance** is difficulty digesting lactose, the main sugar in milk. Digesting it requires an enzyme called **lactase**, made in the lining of the small intestine, which splits lactose into two simpler sugars that can be absorbed. Without enough lactase, lactose passes undigested into the large intestine, where it causes trouble.

Reduced lactase in adulthood is the normal mammalian pattern. Young mammals make lactase to digest their mother's milk, and production falls after weaning. Most humans follow the same course: roughly two-thirds of adults worldwide have reduced lactase activity, with large variation between populations. In parts of East Asia and West Africa nearly all adults do; in parts of northern Europe very few do. Calling it an intolerance reflects which populations wrote the textbooks.

The ability to keep making lactase into adulthood, called **lactase persistence**, is the evolutionary novelty. It arose through genetic changes that keep the lactase gene switched on, and different changes appeared separately in different herding populations, in Europe, East Africa, and the Middle East. Ancient DNA suggests the European variant became common only within the last several thousand years, after people had already begun keeping dairy animals. It's one of the clearest examples of culture shaping human genes: herding created an advantage for digesting milk, and that advantage spread the genes. Why the advantage was so large is debated; proposals include extra calories during famine and, in the north, help absorbing calcium where sunlight was scarce; see [[Vitamin D]].

Symptoms come from two processes in the colon. Undigested lactose draws water into the bowel, causing loose stools; see [[Osmosis]] and [[Fluid Balance]]. Gut bacteria ferment the lactose, producing gas, bloating, and cramps; see [[Gut Microbiome]]. The gas is the basis of the standard diagnostic test, which measures hydrogen in the breath after a dose of lactose.

Intolerance is a matter of degree. Many people with low lactase can handle a cup of milk with few symptoms, especially alongside other food. Fermented dairy contains less lactose because bacteria have already consumed much of it, so yogurt and aged cheese are often well tolerated; see [[Fermentation]]. Symptoms usually start between half an hour and two hours after eating. Lactase enzyme tablets and lactose-free milk, which has the enzyme added, sidestep the problem.

Lactose intolerance is not an allergy. A milk allergy is an immune reaction to milk proteins, which can be dangerous even in small amounts; see [[Allergy]] and [[Immune System]]. Intolerance causes discomfort but no immune response. A different reaction, to proteins in wheat, underlies celiac disease; see [[Gluten]].

Avoiding dairy entirely can reduce calcium intake, which matters for bone health over a lifetime; see [[Osteoporosis]].
