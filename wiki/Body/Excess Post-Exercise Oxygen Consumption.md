**Excess post-exercise oxygen consumption** (EPOC) is the extra oxygen the body uses after exercise ends, above its resting rate. Breathing and heart rate stay up for minutes to hours after a hard session because the body is still working: restoring energy stores, clearing byproducts, repairing tissue, and cooling down.

The idea began as the **oxygen debt**. In the 1920s the physiologist A. V. Hill, who won a Nobel Prize for work on muscle, proposed that hard exercise borrows energy without enough oxygen, building up lactic acid, and that the extra breathing afterward repays the loan by converting it back. The picture proved too simple, since lactate is used as a fuel rather than simply cleared as waste, and in 1984 Glenn Gaesser and George Brooks proposed the neutral term EPOC; see [[Lactate Threshold]] and [[Energy Systems]].

EPOC has two phases:

- **Fast component**, lasting a few minutes. The body rebuilds its phosphocreatine stores, the immediate energy reserve for sprints, and reloads oxygen onto myoglobin in muscle and hemoglobin in blood; see [[Creatine]].
- **Slow component**, lasting from under an hour to more than a day after very hard sessions. It reflects elevated body temperature, stress hormones, lactate being converted back to glucose, glycogen replenishment, and tissue repair; see [[Glycogen]] and [[Thermoregulation]].

Its size depends mainly on intensity. Doubling the duration of moderate exercise roughly doubles EPOC, but raising intensity increases it far more. Interval training and heavy resistance training produce the largest effects; see [[Interval Training]] and [[Strength Training]].

Fitness marketing calls EPOC the **afterburn**, promising that intense workouts keep burning fat for hours. The effect is real but usually modest: across studies, EPOC typically adds around 6–15% to the energy spent during the session itself. For a 400-calorie workout, that might be another 25 to 60 calories, most of it within the first hour. It isn't enough to make short, intense workouts superior for weight loss on that basis alone, though intensity has other benefits; see [[Body Composition]].

EPOC is also used for monitoring training. Some sports watches estimate training effect from heart rate data, modeling EPOC as a measure of how much a session disturbed the body; see [[Training Load]]. The disturbance and the recovery from it are what drive adaptation; see [[Supercompensation]].

Fitter people recover faster: their EPOC is often lower for the same absolute work and returns to baseline sooner, reflecting better aerobic capacity and more mitochondria; see [[VO2 Max]] and [[Mitochondria]].

Recovery from a session involves more than oxygen: fuel, fluids, sleep, and muscle repair all continue long after breathing settles; see [[Muscle Protein Synthesis]].
