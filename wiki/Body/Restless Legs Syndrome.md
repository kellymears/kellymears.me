**Restless legs syndrome**, also called Willis-Ekbom disease, is a neurological condition that produces an overwhelming urge to move the legs. The urge usually comes with uncomfortable sensations deep in the legs that people struggle to describe: crawling, pulling, itching, aching, or an electric fizz. It's different from numbness or pins and needles; see [[Paresthesia]] and [[Itch]].

Four features define it:

- an urge to move the legs, usually with unpleasant sensations;
- symptoms that begin or worsen at rest, sitting or lying down;
- relief, at least partly, from movement such as walking or stretching;
- symptoms that are worse in the evening and at night.

The evening pattern follows the body clock rather than simply tiredness; see [[Circadian Rhythm]]. Long car journeys, flights, and evenings in front of the television are typical problem times.

The English physician Thomas Willis described the condition in the 1600s, and the Swedish neurologist Karl-Axel Ekbom characterized it in detail in 1945. It's estimated to affect a few percent of adults to a clinically significant degree, more commonly women and older people, and often runs in families.

Most people with restless legs also have periodic limb movements during sleep: repetitive jerks or kicks every 20 to 40 seconds that may wake them or their partner. Together, the symptoms make falling asleep and staying asleep difficult; see [[Insomnia]] and [[Sleep Deprivation]].

The causes involve iron and dopamine. Brain imaging and autopsy studies have found low iron in parts of the brain, even in people whose blood iron looks normal; see [[Iron Deficiency]]. Iron is needed to make dopamine, a chemical messenger involved in movement; see [[Dopamine]]. Restless legs is more common in pregnancy, kidney failure, and iron deficiency anemia, and some drugs, including sedating antihistamines and some antidepressants, can worsen it. Caffeine and alcohol often make symptoms worse; see [[Sleep Pressure]].

Treatment starts with checking iron stores, ideally with a blood test for ferritin. Iron supplementation, taken by mouth or given intravenously, helps many people when stores are low. Drugs that mimic dopamine were long the standard treatment, but they can cause augmentation: over time, symptoms start earlier in the day, spread to the arms, and grow more severe. Recent guidelines have moved away from them as first choice toward other drugs and iron.

Non-drug measures include regular moderate exercise, leg massage, warm baths, and keeping the mind engaged during long periods of sitting; see [[Stretching]].

Restless legs differs from nighttime leg cramps, where a muscle suddenly locks painfully; see [[Muscle Cramp]]. Movements that soothe it superficially resemble the repetitive movements some people use to regulate their senses; see [[Stimming]].
