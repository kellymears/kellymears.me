**Heat stroke** is a medical emergency in which the body's core temperature rises above roughly 40°C (104°F) and the brain begins to fail, causing confusion, slurred speech, seizures, or collapse. Unlike a fever, where the brain deliberately raises its temperature set point, heat stroke is a failure of cooling: the body takes in or produces heat faster than it can shed it; see [[Fever]] and [[Thermoregulation]].

There are two forms:

- **Classic heat stroke** develops over days during heat waves, mostly in older adults, young children, and people with chronic illness or without air conditioning. The 2003 European heat wave was linked to tens of thousands of excess deaths.
- **Exertional heat stroke** strikes healthy, fit people during hard exercise, such as runners, soldiers, and football players, often in conditions that don't seem extreme. Exercise multiplies heat production, and motivation can override warning signs; see [[Central Governor Theory]].

Heat stroke sits at the end of a spectrum. **Heat exhaustion** brings heavy sweating, weakness, dizziness, nausea, and headache, with core temperature elevated but the mind clear. It resolves with rest, cooling, and fluids. The key difference is the brain: confusion or odd behavior in a hot, exercising person should be treated as heat stroke.

Treatment depends on speed. The guiding rule in sports medicine is "cool first, transport second." Immersion in cold or ice water is the most effective method, and when started within about 30 minutes it brings survival in exertional heat stroke close to 100%. Damage from heat stroke spreads to many organs, including breakdown of muscle, kidney injury, and clotting problems; see [[Rhabdomyolysis]] and [[Inflammation]].

Humidity matters as much as temperature. Sweat cools only by evaporating, and in humid air it can't. The **wet-bulb temperature** combines heat and humidity into one number; see [[Dew Point]]. Scientists long cited 35°C wet-bulb as the theoretical limit of human survival, but a 2022 study by Daniel Vecellio and colleagues found young, healthy volunteers lost control of core temperature at around 30–31°C in humid conditions. Sports bodies use a related index, the wet-bulb globe temperature, which also accounts for sun, to decide when to modify or cancel events.

Risk factors include lack of acclimatization, dehydration, recent illness, poor sleep, obesity, heavy clothing or equipment, and some medications; see [[Fluid Balance]]. Acclimatization over one to two weeks increases sweat rate and plasma volume and lowers heart rate in heat, substantially reducing risk; see [[Heat Acclimation]].

Prevention for athletes:

- **Acclimatize** gradually.
- **Adjust** pace, timing, and clothing to conditions; see [[Heart Rate Zones]].
- **Stop** at the first sign of confusion or unusual fatigue.
- **Have cold water ready** at events where heat is a risk.

Cooling strategies before and during exercise overlap with cold-water practices; see [[Cold Exposure]].
