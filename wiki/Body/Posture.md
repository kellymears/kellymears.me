**Posture** is the position the body holds while sitting, standing, lying, or moving. For more than a century, people have been told that slouching causes back pain, that there is one correct way to sit, and that good posture means a straight spine and pulled-back shoulders. Research has largely failed to support these claims.

The idea of correct posture has a long history. In the late 1800s and early 1900s, posture became linked to health and moral character, and schools and the military drilled children to stand straight. American colleges photographed incoming students nude to assess their posture as late as the 1960s.

Modern studies find only weak links between posture and pain. People with slouched postures are not consistently more likely to have back or neck pain than those who sit upright, and the natural curves of the spine vary widely between healthy people; see [[Low Back Pain]]. Concern about "text neck," the forward head tilt while using phones, has spread widely, but evidence that it causes lasting harm is thin.

What matters more seems to be:

- **Movement and variety.** Holding any position for long periods becomes uncomfortable, upright or slouched. Changing positions often, taking breaks, and moving around help more than finding a single correct posture; see [[Repetitive Strain Injury]].
- **Strength and capacity.** Muscles and tissues adapt to the loads they regularly handle, so building strength may matter more than avoiding positions; see [[Strength Training]] and [[Tendinopathy]].
- **Beliefs.** Telling people that their posture is dangerous can make them more fearful and guarded, which is associated with worse pain; see [[Nocebo Effect]] and [[Central Sensitization]].

Posture still matters in specific settings. Pressing on the elbow or keeping the wrists bent for hours can compress nerves; see [[Ulnar Neuropathy]]. Screen height and distance affect neck strain and eye fatigue; see [[Digital Eye Strain]]. Cyclists spend hours in one position and benefit from adjusting the bike to their body; see [[Bike Fit]]. Lifting very heavy loads with a good technique spreads strain, though everyday lifting with a rounded back is not as dangerous as often claimed.

Sitting itself has been the target of public health concern. Long periods of sitting are associated with heart disease and early death, leading to slogans like "sitting is the new smoking." The risk seems to come mostly from inactivity, and regular exercise offsets much of it. Standing desks became popular but don't solve the problem, since standing still for hours has its own discomforts.

Posture also affects how people are perceived and, some claimed, how they feel; see [[Embodied Cognition]] for the fate of research on power poses. The body's sense of its own position comes from [[Proprioception]].
