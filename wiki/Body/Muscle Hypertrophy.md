**Muscle hypertrophy** is the growth of muscle fibers in response to training. Individual fibers get thicker, mostly by adding more of the contractile proteins, actin and myosin, that generate force. Humans add few or no new fibers; growth comes from enlarging the ones already there.

Muscle is constantly being built and broken down. *Muscle protein synthesis* creates new protein, and breakdown removes old protein. Muscle grows when synthesis exceeds breakdown over time. A resistance training session raises synthesis for 24 to 48 hours, and eating protein raises it further.

What triggers growth? Researchers once proposed three main mechanisms: mechanical tension, metabolic stress (the "pump"), and muscle damage. The evidence now points most strongly to **mechanical tension**: high force on muscle fibers, especially while they are being stretched or working near their limit. Fibers sense tension through proteins in their membranes and structure, which switch on growth signaling pathways, notably one centered on a protein called mTOR. Muscle damage and soreness, once thought necessary, are not required; see [[Delayed Onset Muscle Soreness]].

Practical findings from training research:

- **Effort matters more than load.** Studies by Brad Schoenfeld and colleagues found that sets with light loads, as low as about 30 percent of maximum, build similar muscle to heavy sets when both are taken close to failure. Heavy loads build more strength, though.
- **Volume matters.** More hard sets per muscle per week generally means more growth, up to a point where recovery becomes the limit, the dynamic described under [[Supercompensation]] and [[Training Load]].
- **Protein.** A 2018 meta-analysis led by Robert Morton found benefits up to about 1.6 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day.
- **Sleep.** Recovery depends on it, and deep sleep coincides with growth hormone release; see [[Sleep Stages]].

Early strength gains, in the first weeks of training, come mostly from the nervous system learning to recruit muscle more effectively, a kind of [[Motor Learning]], before measurable growth appears. Fast-twitch fibers grow more than slow-twitch ones, as described under [[Muscle Fiber Types]]. *Satellite cells*, muscle stem cells, add nuclei to growing fibers, and those extra nuclei may persist after detraining, one proposed explanation for how muscle regrows quickly after a break.

Combining heavy endurance training with strength training can blunt hypertrophy, the *interference effect* first described by Robert Hickson in 1980, though the effect is modest for most people. Endurance work builds [[Mitochondria]] while resistance work builds size, and the signaling pathways partly compete.

Muscle matters beyond appearance. It is the body's largest site of glucose disposal, so more muscle supports [[Insulin Sensitivity]]. Loading also strengthens tendons and bone, through [[Tendon and Ligament]] adaptation and [[Bone Remodeling]].
