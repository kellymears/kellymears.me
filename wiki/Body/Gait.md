**Gait** is the pattern of movement that carries a body over the ground. Its basic unit is the **gait cycle**, from one foot touching down to the same foot touching down again. Each leg alternates between **stance**, when the foot is on the ground, and **swing**, when it moves forward through the air.

Walking and running differ in their mechanics, not just their speed:

- **Walking** always keeps at least one foot on the ground, with brief moments when both are down. Each leg acts like an upside-down pendulum. The body vaults over the stiff stance leg, rising and slowing, then falls forward and speeds up, swapping kinetic energy for height and back again; see [[Conservation of Energy]] and [[Center of Mass]]. That exchange recovers much of the energy of each step.
- **Running** has a flight phase with neither foot down. The leg behaves like a spring. On landing, tendons and ligaments, especially the Achilles tendon and the arch of the foot, stretch and store elastic energy, then return much of it at push-off; see [[Elasticity]] and [[Tendon and Ligament]].

People naturally pick the walking speed that costs the least energy per distance, typically around 1.3 to 1.4 meters per second for adults, and switch to running at about 2 meters per second, near the point where walking becomes more costly than running. How efficiently someone moves at a given speed is their economy; see [[Exercise Economy]]. Steps per minute, adjusted with stride length to change speed, is the [[Cadence]].

How the foot meets the ground in running has been argued over for decades. **Foot strike** can land on the heel, the midfoot or the forefoot. Daniel Lieberman's 2010 study of runners in Kenya and the United States found that habitually barefoot runners tended to land on the forefoot, producing a smaller initial impact force, and it fueled the minimalist shoe boom. Evidence that switching foot strike reduces injuries is weak, however; changing it shifts load from the knee to the calf and Achilles rather than removing it.

**Pronation**, the inward roll of the foot after landing, is a normal shock-absorbing motion. For years running shoes were prescribed to control "overpronation," but studies have found little evidence that matching shoes to foot type prevents injury. Comfort seems a better guide.

Gait analysis uses [[Motion Capture]], force plates and video to study athletes, prosthetics and neurological conditions. Balance during gait draws on the [[Vestibular System]] and [[Proprioception]].

In older adults, walking speed is a surprisingly strong health signal. A 2011 analysis of nearly 35,000 older adults found that gait speed predicted survival as well as age, sex and chronic conditions combined, which has earned it the nickname "the sixth vital sign." Slowing often reflects declining muscle; see [[Sarcopenia]].
