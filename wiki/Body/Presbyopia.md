**Presbyopia** is the gradual loss of the ability to focus on nearby objects that comes with age. Almost everyone experiences it, usually noticing in their early to mid-forties that they need to hold menus and phones farther away to read them. By about age 60, most people need help to read small print. The name comes from Greek words meaning "old eye."

The cause is in the lens inside the eye. To focus on something close, a ring of muscle inside the eye contracts, letting the flexible lens become rounder and bend light more strongly; see [[Refraction]]. This adjustment is called **accommodation**. Over a lifetime, the lens keeps adding layers of cells and becomes larger and stiffer, so it can no longer change shape as much; see [[Elasticity]]. The decline actually begins in childhood but only becomes a problem when the nearest point of clear focus moves past arm's length.

Presbyopia differs from other common vision problems. [[Myopia]], or nearsightedness, comes from the eye's shape and makes distant objects blurry. Nearsighted people can often read up close without glasses even as presbyopia sets in, by taking their distance glasses off. Farsighted people tend to notice presbyopia earlier.

Several solutions exist:

- **Reading glasses.** Simple magnifying lenses for close work, sold cheaply without prescription.
- **Bifocals and progressives.** Lenses with different strengths in different zones, invented in the 1700s and often credited to Benjamin Franklin. Progressive lenses blend smoothly from distance at the top to reading at the bottom.
- **Contact lenses.** Multifocal lenses, or monovision, where one eye is corrected for distance and the other for near.
- **Surgery and eye drops.** Lens replacement and some laser procedures can help, and drops that shrink the pupil, increasing depth of focus, were approved in the 2020s.

Presbyopia has large effects on daily life worldwide. Hundreds of millions of people lack the reading glasses they need, which limits their ability to work and earn, even though glasses are one of the cheapest interventions in medicine.

It also matters for design. Small type, low contrast, and dim lighting make reading harder for older eyes, which also scatter more light and need more of it; see [[Color Contrast]] and [[Typographic Scale]]. Interfaces that let users enlarge text and that avoid tiny touch targets serve people with presbyopia, who are a large share of users; see [[Accessibility]] and [[Fitts's Law]]. Screen work at fixed close distances adds strain for eyes that can no longer accommodate easily; see [[Digital Eye Strain]].

Presbyopia is a reminder that aging affects the body gradually and on a predictable schedule, like the loss of muscle mass and hearing; see [[Sarcopenia]] and [[Hearing Loss]].
