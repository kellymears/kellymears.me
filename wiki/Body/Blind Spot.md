Every eye has a **blind spot**, a small area of the retina with no light-sensitive cells. It sits where the optic nerve and blood vessels leave the back of the eye, a region called the **optic disc**. Light falling there isn't detected at all, leaving a gap in each eye's view about 15 degrees to the side of the center, large enough to hide a person's head at a few meters.

The French physicist Edme Mariotte discovered it around 1660 while dissecting eyes, and demonstrated it at the court of Charles II. The simple test still works: draw a dot and a cross a few inches apart, close the left eye, stare at the cross with the right, and move the page closer until the dot vanishes.

The blind spot exists because of how vertebrate eyes are built. The light-sensitive cells sit at the back of the retina, facing away from the light, with the nerve fibers running across the front surface. The fibers have to exit somewhere, so they pass through the retina, leaving the hole. Octopus eyes, which evolved separately, have the wiring behind the receptors and no blind spot.

People don't notice their blind spots, for several reasons:

- **Two eyes.** Each eye's blind spot falls on a part of the scene the other eye sees.
- **Constant movement.** The eyes shift several times a second, so no point stays hidden for long; see [[Eye Tracking]].
- **Filling-in.** Even with one eye closed, there is no visible hole. The brain fills the gap with colors and patterns from the surroundings, so a blind spot on a striped background looks striped.

Filling-in is a clear example of the brain constructing what we see rather than recording it. Perception supplies its best guess about what's probably there, based on context and expectation; see [[Predictive Processing]]. The same process makes people miss unexpected objects in plain view, and see faces where none exist; see [[Inattentional Blindness]] and [[Pareidolia]].

Damage to the retina or visual pathways can cause other blind areas, called **scotomas**. Glaucoma, which damages the optic nerve, often causes gradual loss of peripheral vision that goes unnoticed for years, because filling-in hides it until damage is advanced. This is why regular eye exams measure the visual field. Migraine auras can produce temporary shimmering scotomas.

People with large losses of vision sometimes experience vivid, complex hallucinations of faces, patterns, or figures, called Charles Bonnet syndrome; the brain generates imagery when input is missing; see [[Hallucination]] for the related term in language models.

The word "blind spot" has become a metaphor for anything people can't see about themselves, apt because the real one goes unnoticed; see [[Metacognition]].
