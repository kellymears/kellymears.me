**Cholesterol** is a waxy fat-like molecule that every animal cell uses. It stiffens cell membranes, and the body converts it into steroid hormones, bile acids for digesting fat, and vitamin D; see [[Vitamin D]] and [[Endocrine System]]. The liver makes most of the body's cholesterol, and diet supplies the rest.

Because cholesterol doesn't dissolve in blood, it travels inside particles called lipoproteins. **LDL**, low-density lipoprotein, carries cholesterol from the liver out to tissues. **HDL**, high-density lipoprotein, carries some back. They're nicknamed bad and good cholesterol, though the cholesterol inside each is the same molecule.

LDL particles that slip into an artery wall and get trapped there start the fatty plaques that narrow arteries and cause heart attacks and strokes; see [[Atherosclerosis]]. Several independent lines of evidence point to LDL as a cause rather than a bystander. People born with familial hypercholesterolemia, a genetic condition with very high LDL from birth, can have heart attacks in their thirties or younger. People born with gene variants that lower LDL have fewer heart attacks over their lifetimes. And drug trials that lower LDL by different mechanisms reduce heart disease roughly in proportion to how far they lower it; see [[Correlation and Causation]].

HDL is a warning tale. Higher HDL goes with fewer heart attacks, so drugs were developed to raise it. Several large trials succeeded in raising HDL substantially but didn't reduce heart attacks, and one increased deaths. A number on a lab report can be a marker without being a lever; see [[McNamara Fallacy]] and [[Confounding]].

**Statins** block an enzyme the liver uses to make cholesterol. The first was found by the Japanese biochemist Akira Endo in the 1970s in a mold, and the first statin approved in the United States, lovastatin, reached the market in 1987. They lower LDL substantially and reduce heart attacks and strokes in people at high risk. The benefit depends on starting risk, which is why the difference between relative and absolute risk reduction matters; see [[Relative Risk]].

Muscle aches are the most commonly reported side effect. In blinded trials where people took statin or placebo in alternating months, much of the reported muscle pain appeared during placebo months too; see [[Nocebo Effect]]. Some statins are broken down by a liver enzyme that grapefruit inhibits, raising their blood levels; see [[Xenobiotic Metabolism]].

Dietary cholesterol, as in eggs, affects blood cholesterol less than once thought, and US dietary guidelines dropped a specific daily limit in 2015. Saturated fat has a larger effect on LDL. Exercise and body weight affect it modestly, and blood fats also connect to how the body handles sugar; see [[Insulin Sensitivity]] and [[Blood Pressure]].
