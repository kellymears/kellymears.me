A **stroke** happens when part of the brain loses its blood supply. Brain tissue has almost no energy reserve, so neurons begin to die within minutes once blood stops arriving. The symptoms depend on which region is hit, and they arrive suddenly: weakness or numbness on one side of the body, a drooping face, slurred or garbled speech, loss of vision, or a severe headache.

There are two main kinds:

- **Ischemic stroke**, about 85 percent of cases, occurs when a clot blocks an artery. The clot may form on a ruptured plaque in a brain or neck artery, or travel from elsewhere. A common source is the heart in atrial fibrillation, an irregular rhythm that lets blood pool and clot; see [[Atherosclerosis]] and [[Blood Clotting]].
- **Hemorrhagic stroke** occurs when a vessel bursts and bleeds into or around the brain, often after years of high blood pressure weakening its walls.

A **transient ischemic attack** produces the same symptoms but resolves, usually within an hour, because the blockage clears. It is a warning: the risk of a full stroke is high in the following days, and urgent assessment can prevent one.

The slogan **time is brain** summarizes the treatment logic. A widely cited 2006 estimate by Jeffrey Saver put the loss in a typical large ischemic stroke at nearly two million neurons per minute. Public campaigns teach the FAST checklist, face drooping, arm weakness, speech difficulty, time to call emergency services. For ischemic stroke, clot-dissolving drugs work best within a few hours of onset. Since 2015, trials have shown that pulling large clots out mechanically through a catheter greatly improves outcomes, and later trials extended that window to as long as a day for selected patients whose brain scans show salvageable tissue. Because bleeding would be made worse by clot-busting drugs, a scan to tell the two types apart comes first.

Some strokes strike the border zones between arterial territories when blood pressure falls too low, the regions most vulnerable to reduced flow; see [[Watershed Blood Supply]]. Damage to the right side of the brain can leave people unaware of the left half of space, a condition called [[Hemispatial Neglect]].

The largest modifiable risk factor is high blood pressure; see [[Blood Pressure]]. Others include smoking, [[Diabetes]], high [[Cholesterol]] and physical inactivity.

Recovery relies on the brain rewiring around the damage; see [[Neuroplasticity]]. Most spontaneous improvement happens in the first three months, and intensive, task-specific practice in rehabilitation draws on the same principles as any [[Motor Learning]]. Many people regain substantial function, though stroke remains a leading cause of long-term disability worldwide.
