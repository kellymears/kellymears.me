**Basal metabolic rate (BMR)** is the energy the body uses at complete rest just to stay alive: pumping blood, breathing, keeping cells charged, maintaining temperature, and running the brain. For most sedentary adults it is the largest part of daily energy use, often 60 to 70 percent. The closely related *resting metabolic rate* is measured under less strict conditions and comes out slightly higher.

The work is unevenly spread. At rest, the liver, brain, and skeletal muscle each use roughly a fifth of the energy, and the heart and kidneys use much of the rest. The brain is the striking case, about 2 percent of body weight burning about 20 percent of resting energy. Inside cells, most of it is spent by [[Mitochondria]] supplying ATP for pumps and repairs.

**Total daily energy expenditure** adds three more parts:

- **Thermic effect of food.** Digesting and storing food costs about 10 percent of what is eaten, more for protein.
- **Exercise.** Deliberate training, the part athletes track with [[Training Load]] and [[Work and Power]].
- **NEAT.** Non-exercise activity thermogenesis covers fidgeting, standing, walking around. It varies by hundreds of calories a day between people. In a 1999 overfeeding study by James Levine, those who unconsciously moved more gained the least fat.

BMR depends mostly on lean mass, which is why prediction formulas use weight, height, age, and sex, and why losing muscle with age, as in [[Sarcopenia]], lowers it. A large 2021 analysis led by Herman Pontzer found that, adjusted for body size, metabolism is remarkably stable from about 20 to 60, then declines slowly. Middle-age weight gain is not mainly a slowing metabolism.

Pontzer's earlier work produced a more surprising result. The Hadza, hunter-gatherers in Tanzania who walk many miles a day, burn about as many calories per day as sedentary Westerners of similar size. His **constrained energy expenditure** model proposes that the body compensates for high activity by trimming other spending, such as inflammation and stress hormones. Exercise still matters greatly for health, but daily burn does not rise linearly with miles.

Dieting provokes a similar defense. Contestants from a televised weight-loss competition, studied in 2016, burned about 500 fewer calories a day at rest than predicted for their size six years later. Energy is still conserved, per [[Conservation of Energy]], but the body adjusts its spending as a form of [[Homeostasis]].

Temperature also costs energy. Cold exposure raises metabolic rate through shivering and brown fat, part of [[Thermoregulation]], and a [[Fever]] raises it by about 10 percent per degree Celsius. Athletes who chronically undereat relative to their training suppress BMR and hormones, which shows up as poor recovery and stress fractures, a failure of [[Supercompensation]].
