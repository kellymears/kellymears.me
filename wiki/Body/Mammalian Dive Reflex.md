The **mammalian dive reflex** is a set of automatic responses that conserve oxygen when an air-breathing animal goes underwater. It is triggered by holding the breath, and strongly reinforced by cold water on the face. It is most developed in diving mammals such as seals and whales, but it is present in all humans, including newborns.

Three changes happen together:

- **The heart slows.** The [[Vagus Nerve]] slows the heartbeat, often by 10 to 25 percent in people and far more in seals, cutting the heart's own oxygen use; see [[Cardiac Output]].
- **Blood vessels in the limbs narrow.** Blood is shunted away from the skin, fingers, and muscles, which can tolerate a shortage, toward the heart and brain, which cannot. [[Blood Pressure]] rises as a result.
- **The spleen contracts.** It holds a reserve of red blood cells, and squeezing them into circulation raises the blood's capacity to carry oxygen for the rest of the dive; see [[Hemoglobin]].

The face is the trigger because of receptors served by the trigeminal nerve, especially around the eyes and nose. Cold water works better than warm, and simply holding the breath without immersion produces a weaker version. The response is a clear exception to the usual rule that stress speeds the heart; see [[Autonomic Nervous System]] and [[Fight-or-Flight Response]].

Some human populations show the reflex unusually strongly. The Bajau, sea nomads of Southeast Asia who have hunted by freediving for generations, have larger spleens than neighboring groups who do not dive, and a 2018 study linked this to genetic variants, an example of human adaptation to a way of life. Trained freedivers can hold their breath for many minutes, partly by training the reflex itself; see [[Hypoxia]].

The reflex has practical uses. Doctors sometimes treat certain episodes of an abnormally fast heartbeat by having the patient plunge their face into ice water, and some therapy programs teach the same trick as a quick way to calm intense distress, since the vagal slowing is fast and reliable.

It can also clash with another reflex. Sudden immersion of the whole body in cold water triggers **cold shock**: a gasp, rapid breathing, and a racing heart; see [[Cold Exposure]]. When both fire at once, one speeding the heart and the other slowing it, the conflicting signals can disturb its rhythm, which may explain some sudden deaths in cold water among people who were good swimmers.

Stories of children surviving long submersion in icy water are sometimes credited to the dive reflex. The main protection there is more likely rapid cooling of the brain, which lowers its demand for oxygen; see [[Thermoregulation]] and [[Homeostasis]].
