**Influenza**, the flu, is a respiratory infection caused by influenza viruses. It is often lumped together with the common cold, but it usually arrives more abruptly and hits harder, with high [[Fever]], muscle aches, exhaustion and a dry cough that can linger for weeks. Most people recover in about a week. The World Health Organization estimates that seasonal flu nonetheless causes roughly 290,000 to 650,000 respiratory deaths worldwide each year, mostly among older adults, infants and people with chronic illness.

Two types, A and B, cause the seasonal epidemics. Influenza A strains are named for two proteins on the virus surface: hemagglutinin, which lets the virus latch onto cells in the airway, and neuraminidase, which lets new virus particles escape. The labels H1N1 and H3N2 refer to their variants.

Those same surface proteins are what the [[Immune System]] learns to recognize, and they keep changing. The flu's genetic copying is sloppy, so small mutations accumulate constantly, a process called **antigenic drift**. Each season's virus is different enough that antibodies from earlier infections or vaccines match it only partly. That is why flu vaccines are updated every year. Experts at the WHO choose the strains months in advance, about February for the northern winter, by forecasting which variants will dominate. In good years the vaccine reduces the risk of illness needing medical care by around 40 to 60 percent; in poorly matched years it does much worse.

Influenza A also infects birds and pigs. When two strains infect the same cell, their gene segments can reshuffle into a new combination, **antigenic shift**, producing a virus against which almost nobody has immunity. Shifts caused the pandemics of 1957, 1968 and 2009. The **1918 flu pandemic** was far worse, killing an estimated 50 million people, and unusually many of its victims were healthy young adults. One leading explanation is that their vigorous immune responses inflamed the lungs; see [[Inflammation]].

Flu spreads through droplets and smaller airborne particles from coughs, sneezes and breathing; see [[Sneeze]]. In temperate climates it peaks in winter, possibly because the virus survives better in cold, dry air and people crowd indoors. Each infected person passes it to only one or two others on average, yet its short incubation period lets it move through a population quickly; see [[Herd Immunity]].

Antibiotics do nothing against viruses, and prescribing them for flu contributes to [[Antibiotic Resistance]]. Antiviral drugs such as oseltamivir shorten illness modestly if started early. Severe flu can trigger bacterial pneumonia, heart complications and occasionally muscle breakdown; see [[Rhabdomyolysis]]. Fever itself raises metabolic rate, part of why the illness is so draining; see [[Basal Metabolic Rate]].
