**Hemispatial neglect** is a condition in which a person fails to notice, attend to, or respond to one side of space, usually the left. It most often follows a stroke or other damage to the right hemisphere of the brain, especially the parietal lobe. People with neglect may eat food only from the right side of a plate, shave or apply makeup to only half the face, bump into objects on the left, or ignore people speaking from that side.

Neglect isn't blindness. The eyes and visual pathways can be working, and the information reaches the brain, but it fails to command attention; see [[Selective Attention]]. Attention drawn directly to the neglected side often helps, and some studies suggest the ignored information is still processed without awareness; see [[Blindsight]].

Clinical tests reveal it plainly:

- **Line bisection.** Asked to mark the middle of a horizontal line, patients mark it well to the right.
- **Cancellation.** Asked to cross out every target on a page, they miss those on the left.
- **Drawing.** Copying a flower or a clock, they draw only the right half, or crowd all the numbers onto the right side.

Neglect can affect imagination as well as perception. In a well-known 1978 study, Edoardo Bisiach and Claudio Luzzatti asked patients in Milan to imagine standing in the city's cathedral square and describe what they saw. Patients described the buildings on the right of their imagined viewpoint. Asked to imagine standing at the opposite end, they described the buildings they had just left out and omitted the ones they had just mentioned. The mental map was intact, but attention to one side of it was not; see [[Cognitive Map]] and [[Mental imagery]].

Why the left side is usually affected seems to reflect an asymmetry: the right hemisphere attends to both sides of space, while the left attends mainly to the right. So left-hemisphere damage is often compensated, and right-hemisphere damage is not.

Neglect often comes with **anosognosia**, unawareness of one's own impairment. Some patients deny that anything is wrong, or that a paralyzed left arm belongs to them, sometimes offering elaborate explanations; see [[Confabulation]]. This makes rehabilitation harder, since patients may not understand why they need it.

Many patients improve over weeks and months as the brain reorganizes; see [[Neuroplasticity]]. Therapies include training to scan deliberately to the left, and prism adaptation, in which briefly wearing glasses that shift vision sideways can temporarily reduce neglect.

The condition shows that awareness of space depends on attention, not just intact senses, a point also made by healthy people's failures to see unexpected objects; see [[Inattentional Blindness]]. It also shows how the brain builds a sense of the body and its surroundings; see [[Rubber Hand Illusion]] and [[Phantom Limb]].
