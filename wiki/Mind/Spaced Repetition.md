**Spaced repetition** is a study method that schedules reviews of material at increasing intervals, each timed for when the memory is about to fade. It combines two of the most robust findings in learning research. The **spacing effect** says that study spread over time produces far better long-term retention than the same amount massed together. The **testing effect** says that actively recalling something strengthens memory more than rereading it.

The story starts with Hermann Ebbinghaus, who in the 1880s memorized lists of nonsense syllables and tested himself at intervals. He found the **forgetting curve**: memory drops steeply at first and then more slowly. He also found that each relearning flattened the curve, so the next review could wait longer. Spaced repetition exploits that pattern: review a card after a day, then three days, then a week, then a month. Each successful recall extends the next interval, and a failure shortens it again.

Retrieval is the active ingredient. A 2006 study by Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke had students either reread a passage or test themselves on it. Rereaders felt more confident and did better after five minutes. After a week, the self-testers remembered far more. Rereading creates *fluency*, a feeling of familiarity that is easily mistaken for knowing. Effortful recall is the "[[Desirable Difficulty|desirable difficulty]]" that actually builds memory. Cramming does the reverse, packing study into a single session that feels productive and fades fast.

The method spread through software. The Leitner system of the 1970s used flashcards moved between physical boxes. Piotr Woźniak's SuperMemo, from 1987, computed intervals algorithmically, and Anki, released in 2006, brought the approach to a large audience. Newer schedulers fit a statistical model of each learner's memory rather than using fixed multipliers. Medical students and language learners are the heaviest users, since both face large volumes of facts that must be retained for years.

Spaced repetition handles discrete facts best. Understanding comes from connecting ideas, which is where linked note systems such as a [[Zettelkasten]] or [[Wiki]] fit, and the two approaches complement each other. Its short daily review sessions also suit [[Habit Formation]]. It works with the limits of [[Working Memory]] and [[Cognitive Load]] by keeping each session small. Language models face a version of the same problem, and [[Agent Memory]] systems borrow ideas about what to keep and when to refresh it.
