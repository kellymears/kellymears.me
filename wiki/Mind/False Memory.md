A **false memory** is a recollection of something that didn't happen, or that happened differently than remembered. False memories feel just like true ones: they can be vivid, detailed, and held with full confidence. They arise because memory is not a recording but a reconstruction, assembled anew from fragments, expectations, and later information each time it is recalled.

The British psychologist Frederic Bartlett showed this in 1932. He had English participants retell a Native American folk tale, "The War of the Ghosts," over repeated sessions. Their versions drifted toward familiar English story conventions, dropping unfamiliar details and adding expected ones. Memory, he concluded, is shaped by *schemas*, mental frameworks about how things usually go.

Elizabeth Loftus made the modern case. In a 1974 study with John Palmer, people watched a film of a car crash and were asked how fast the cars were going when they "smashed" or "hit" each other. Those who heard "smashed" gave higher estimates and, a week later, were more likely to report seeing broken glass, which wasn't in the film. This is the **misinformation effect**: information encountered after an event blends into the memory of it. In later "lost in the mall" studies, Loftus showed that about a quarter of participants could be led to remember a childhood event that had never happened.

A simple lab version, the DRM paradigm, after James Deese, Henry Roediger, and Kathleen McDermott, presents lists of related words such as bed, rest, tired, dream, and pillow. Many people confidently "remember" hearing *sleep*, which was never on the list.

Neuroscience adds a mechanism. When a memory is recalled, it can become briefly unstable and must be stored again, a process called *reconsolidation*. Recall is therefore also an opportunity to modify.

False memory has serious consequences:

- **Eyewitness testimony.** Mistaken eyewitness identification is a leading factor in wrongful convictions later overturned by DNA evidence.
- **Recovered memories.** In the 1990s, some therapy techniques encouraged patients to "recover" memories of abuse through suggestion and imagery, producing memories that were later shown to be false and leading to bitter legal and scientific disputes.
- **Everyday life.** People confidently misremember where they heard a claim, which can make repeated falsehoods feel true, the [[Illusory Truth Effect]], and memories shift toward current beliefs, a cousin of [[Confirmation Bias]].

Protection comes from good practice: asking open rather than leading questions, recording accounts early, and using techniques like the cognitive interview, which leans on [[Context-Dependent Memory]] cues instead of suggestion. Memory's malleability fits [[Predictive Processing]] views of the mind as constantly filling gaps with expectations. It also shapes how people remember experiences overall, as in the [[Peak-End Rule]], and how [[Nostalgia]] edits the past into something warmer than it was.
