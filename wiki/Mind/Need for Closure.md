**Need for closure** is the desire for a definite answer, any answer, instead of continued uncertainty. Someone high in it finds ambiguity uncomfortable, makes up their mind quickly, and dislikes reopening a settled question. Someone low in it tolerates open questions, keeps considering alternatives, and may struggle to commit.

The social psychologist Arie Kruglanski developed the concept from the 1980s onward, and in 1994 Kruglanski and Donna Webster published a widely used questionnaire to measure it. The scale asks how much people like order and predictability, how decisive they are, and how uncomfortable ambiguity makes them.

Kruglanski described two tendencies:

- **Seizing.** The urge to reach a conclusion quickly, grabbing the first plausible answer.
- **Freezing.** The urge to hold onto that answer once reached, resisting new information that might reopen it.

The two often work in sequence. A person seizes on an early answer while the question is open, then freezes on it once it's settled, so the same trait can produce both haste and stubbornness.

The need is partly a stable trait and partly situational. Experiments raised it by putting people under time pressure, making them work in noisy rooms, or testing them when tired, and in each case judgment shifted toward quick, rigid conclusions; see [[Decision Fatigue]]. People who score high on the trait tend to prefer clear rules, familiar routines, and predictable surroundings.

High need for closure produces predictable effects:

- **First impressions dominate.** Early information gets more weight, and later information is discounted; see [[Anchoring Effect]].
- **Stereotypes fill gaps.** Ready-made categories supply quick answers about people, sparing the effort of judging each person individually.
- **Evidence gets filtered.** Information that would unsettle a conclusion is ignored or explained away; see [[Confirmation Bias]] and [[Motivated Reasoning]].
- **Groups close ranks.** Groups whose members need closure push toward consensus, favor strong leaders, and reject dissenters; see [[Groupthink]] and [[Conformity]].

The trait isn't simply a flaw. Many situations reward decisiveness, and endless deliberation has costs. Accepting a good-enough answer is often the rational choice; see [[Satisficing]]. Where decisions can be undone cheaply, committing early and adjusting later works well; see [[Reversible Decision]].

Trouble comes where the problem itself resists closure. Complex, changing situations punish premature conclusions; see [[Cynefin Framework]] and [[Wicked Problem]]. Under uncertainty about frightening events, a tidy explanation can be more comforting than an honest "we don't know," part of the appeal of conspiracy theories; see [[Conspiracy Theory]].

The opposite tendency is linked to curiosity and enjoyment of thinking, which keep questions open longer; see [[Curiosity]]. An unfinished task or open question also tends to nag at attention, which suggests why closure feels like relief; see [[Zeigarnik Effect]].
