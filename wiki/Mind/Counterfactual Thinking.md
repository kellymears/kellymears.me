**Counterfactual thinking** is imagining alternatives to what actually happened, "if only I had left five minutes earlier" or "at least nobody was hurt." It runs almost automatically after a surprising or bad outcome, and it shapes how people feel about events, whom they blame and what they plan to do next.

Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky drew attention to it in the early 1980s with what they called the simulation heuristic. People judge how upsetting an outcome is partly by how easily they can imagine it going otherwise. In one scenario, two travelers reach the airport 30 minutes late. One flight left on time; the other was delayed and left five minutes ago. Nearly everyone says the second traveler feels worse. Kahneman and Dale Miller's later norm theory added that unusual events generate more counterfactuals than routine ones. A crash on an unfamiliar route invites more "if only" than the same crash on the usual drive home.

Counterfactuals have a direction:

- **Upward counterfactuals** imagine a better outcome. They hurt, but they carry lessons and motivate change.
- **Downward counterfactuals** imagine a worse one. They console, at the cost of complacency.

A famous 1995 study by Victoria Medvec, Scott Madey and Thomas Gilovich coded the expressions of Olympic medalists and found bronze medalists looked happier than silver medalists on average. Silver invites the thought of gold, while bronze invites the thought of no medal at all. Later replications have been mixed.

**Regret** is the emotion counterfactuals produce when the better outcome was within one's own control. Gilovich and Medvec also reported a time pattern: in the short term people regret actions they took, but looking back over years they regret more the things they never tried. Anticipated regret then feeds back into decisions, pushing people toward defaults and away from choices they might later blame themselves for; see [[Loss Aversion]] and [[Affective Forecasting]].

The habit has costs. Replaying alternatives can harden into [[Rumination]], and vivid counterfactuals tilt blame toward whoever did something unusual, even when it was not causal. Victims of random harm are sometimes blamed for small departures from routine, which the [[Just-World Hypothesis]] makes more tempting. Once the outcome is known, the imagined alternatives also make it seem as if it should have been foreseen; see [[Hindsight Bias]].

Used deliberately, the same faculty is a planning tool. A [[Pre-mortem]] asks a team to imagine a project has already failed and explain why, turning counterfactual thinking forward in time. It also depends on a [[Sense of Agency]]: people generate the most counterfactuals about outcomes they believe they could have changed; see [[Locus of Control]].
