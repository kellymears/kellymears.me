**Misattribution of arousal** is the mistake of crediting physical arousal, a racing heart, quick breathing, a warm face, to the wrong cause. It follows from the **two-factor theory of emotion**, proposed by Stanley Schachter and Jerome Singer in 1962: an emotion combines bodily arousal with a cognitive label that explains it. If the true cause isn't obvious, people look around for one.

In their experiment, participants received injections of adrenaline, described as a vitamin. Some were told to expect a racing heart and trembling, and some weren't. They then waited with an actor who behaved either euphorically or angrily. Uninformed participants were more likely to take on the actor's mood, apparently reading their unexplained arousal as emotion. The results were weaker than often described, but the idea took hold.

The best-known demonstration is the **suspension bridge experiment** of Donald Dutton and Arthur Aron in 1974. An attractive woman approached men who had just crossed either the Capilano Suspension Bridge in Vancouver, swaying 70 meters above a river, or a low, sturdy bridge, and gave them her phone number. Men on the frightening bridge were more likely to call her and told more sexually themed stories in a projective test, as if some of their fear had been read as attraction. Later studies found the effect is real but smaller and less consistent than the original; see [[Replication Crisis]].

Related findings:

- **Excitation transfer.** Dolf Zillmann found that arousal left over from exercise or a thrilling film intensifies whatever emotion comes next, including anger. A frustrating email after a workout may provoke more than it would otherwise.
- **Date choices.** Horror films, roller coasters, and exciting activities are often recommended for dates for this reason.
- **Reappraising nerves.** Alison Wood Brooks found in 2014 that people told to say "I am excited" before singing or public speaking performed better than those told to calm down. Anxiety and excitement feel physically similar, and relabeling is easier than suppressing; see [[Emotional Regulation]] and [[Choking Under Pressure]].

The theory has a modern successor in accounts of emotion as the brain's prediction about the body's state, built from internal signals and context; see [[Predictive Processing]] and [[Interoception]]. People who can distinguish emotions finely tend to manage them better; see [[Emotional Granularity]]. Arousal is driven by the body's automatic stress response; see [[Autonomic Nervous System]].

Misattribution has practical edges. Caffeine jitters can be read as anxiety, and hunger as irritation. Moderate arousal sharpens performance, while too much harms it; see [[Yerkes-Dodson Law]]. Arousal also spills into judgments of people, coloring overall impressions; see [[Halo Effect]]. The feeling of standing before something vast mixes arousal and meaning in ways people struggle to label; see [[Awe]].
