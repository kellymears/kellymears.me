The **attentional blink** is a short window after noticing one thing when the mind is likely to miss the next. In the standard experiment, people watch a rapid stream of letters, about ten per second, and must report two targets, such as two digits among the letters. They usually catch the first. If the second appears within about 200 to 500 milliseconds after it, they often miss it entirely, though they'd have seen it easily on its own. It's as if attention briefly closes its eyes.

Jane Raymond, Kimron Shapiro, and Karen Arnell named the effect in 1992. The method, showing items one after another at the same spot, is called **rapid serial visual presentation**, or RSVP.

A puzzling detail is **lag-1 sparing**: if the second target comes immediately after the first, with no item between, people often catch it. The two seem to be swept into the same episode of attention. The blink starts after that.

The usual explanation is a bottleneck. Seeing something is fast, but turning it into a stable, reportable memory takes time and limited capacity; see [[Working Memory]]. While the first target is being consolidated, a second one can register briefly and then fade before it gets processed. Studies of brain activity show the missed target was processed at some level: its meaning can still prime later responses, even though people deny seeing it; see [[Priming]].

Some things break through the blink:

- **Emotional content.** Threatening or emotional words are less likely to be missed; see [[Negativity Bias]].
- **Personal relevance.** A person's own name tends to get through, as it does in a noisy room; see [[Selective Attention]].
- **Relaxed attention.** Strangely, people who are distracted by music or told to attend less intently show a smaller blink, suggesting overinvestment in the first target makes it worse.
- **Meditation training.** A 2007 study found that three months of intensive meditation reduced the blink, apparently by making people spend fewer resources on the first target; see [[Mindfulness]].

The attentional blink belongs to a family of effects showing that seeing isn't simply registering what's in front of the eyes. Others include missing unexpected objects or changes in a scene and the decline of alertness over long watches; see [[Inattentional Blindness]] and [[Vigilance Decrement]].

The practical implications concern fast information. Alerts that arrive just after another can go unseen, which matters for dashboards, cockpits, and monitoring tools; see [[Incident Response]]. Fast-changing subtitles and on-screen text can be missed after an attention-grabbing moment; see [[Subtitling]]. In games, prompts that appear right after a dramatic event leave players less time than the clock suggests; see [[Quick Time Event]]. Spacing important signals by at least half a second avoids the blink.
