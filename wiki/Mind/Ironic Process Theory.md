**Ironic process theory** explains why trying not to think about something tends to make it come to mind more. Social psychologist Daniel Wegner proposed it after a 1987 experiment in which participants were asked not to think about a white bear for five minutes and to ring a bell whenever they did. They rang it often. When later told they could think about white bears, they thought about them more than people who had never been told to suppress them, a **rebound** effect. Wegner had taken the idea from Dostoevsky, who wrote in 1863 that if you set yourself the task of not thinking of a polar bear, it will come to mind every minute.

Wegner's explanation involves two processes running together:

- **An operating process** that actively looks for thoughts other than the forbidden one. It is deliberate and effortful.
- **A monitoring process** that scans for the forbidden thought to check whether suppression is working. It runs automatically, and each check brings the target briefly to mind.

When people have spare mental capacity, the operating process wins and suppression mostly works. Under stress, fatigue, distraction, or high [[Cognitive Load]], the operating process falters while the monitor keeps running, so the unwanted thought shows up more than if no one had tried to suppress it. The effort meant to clear the thought keeps it active.

The pattern shows up in many places:

- **Sleep.** Trying hard to fall asleep keeps people awake.
- **Intrusive thoughts.** Pushing away worries or unwanted images can strengthen them, feeding [[Rumination]] and some anxiety disorders.
- **Food and habits.** Forbidding a food can increase preoccupation with it, and suppressing urges can make them louder.
- **Performance.** A golfer told "don't overshoot the hole" is more likely to overshoot under pressure. That is one route into [[Choking Under Pressure]].

The remedies follow from the mechanism. Instead of "don't think of X," give the mind a specific alternative to think about, so the operating process has a target. Instructions phrased as what to do work better than what to avoid, which is useful advice for [[Microcopy]] and coaching alike. Acceptance-based approaches go further: noticing a thought without fighting it, as practiced in [[Mindfulness]], removes the monitor's job. [[Cognitive Behavioral Therapy]] for intrusive thoughts often uses scheduled worry time or exposure, deliberately thinking the thought until it loses its charge, rather than suppression. [[Self-Compassion]] helps because self-criticism about having the thought adds pressure.

The research has some limits. Rebound effects vary in size across studies, and are stronger for emotional, personally meaningful thoughts than for white bears. The general lesson holds well: mental control is a limited resource, and [[Emotional Regulation]] works better through redirection and acceptance than through force.
