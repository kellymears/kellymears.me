**Naive realism** is the intuitive conviction that one sees the world objectively, as it really is. The social psychologists Lee Ross and Andrew Ward described it in 1996 as three linked beliefs:

1. I see things as they are.
2. Other reasonable people, given the same information, will see them the same way.
3. If they don't, they must be uninformed, irrational, or biased.

The name comes from philosophy, where naive realism is the view that perception shows the world directly. Psychology has shown how much perception and judgment are constructed, built from expectation and context; see [[Predictive Processing]]. But the construction is invisible, so its results feel like plain observation; see [[Map and Territory]].

Several well-known findings follow from it:

- **"They Saw a Game."** In 1954 Albert Hastorf and Hadley Cantril showed film of a rough Princeton–Dartmouth football game to students from each school. Each side saw the other team commit far more fouls, as if they had watched different games.
- **The false consensus effect.** In a 1977 study by Ross and colleagues, students asked to walk around campus wearing a sandwich board reading "Eat at Joe's" estimated that most others would make the same choice they did, whether they agreed or refused. People see their own choices as the normal ones.
- **The hostile media effect.** In 1985 Robert Vallone, Ross, and Mark Lepper showed pro-Israeli and pro-Arab viewers identical news coverage of a 1982 massacre in Lebanon. Each group judged it biased against their side.
- **The bias blind spot.** Emily Pronin and colleagues found in 2002 that people readily recognize biases in others while rating themselves as less biased than average. Introspection feels like it reveals one's reasoning, but it misses much of what drives judgment; see [[Introspection illusion]].

Naive realism connects to other errors in reading people. Attributing others' disagreement to their character rather than their information is close to [[Fundamental Attribution Error]]. Failing to imagine not knowing what one knows is the [[Curse of Knowledge]].

Opposing groups overestimate how extreme the other side's views are, and each sees itself as the reasonable party; negotiations stall when each side treats its own proposal as fair. Media environments that reinforce one view make disagreement seem more unreasonable; see [[Filter Bubble]] and [[Confirmation Bias]]. People often wrongly assume others privately disagree with a norm they themselves accept; see [[Pluralistic Ignorance]]. People also tend to think the media influences others more than it influences them; see [[Third-Person Effect]].

Remedies start from taking another's view seriously: restate an opponent's position until they agree it's fair before arguing with it; see [[Steelmanning]]. Recognizing that other minds hold different information is the foundation; see [[Theory of Mind]].
