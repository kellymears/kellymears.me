**Inner speech** is the experience of talking to oneself silently in words. Many people narrate plans, rehearse conversations, and argue with themselves in an inner voice. Others report rarely or never thinking in words, and the difference, like that of [[Aphantasia]], usually goes unnoticed until people compare notes.

The Soviet psychologist Lev Vygotsky argued in the 1930s that inner speech grows out of *private speech*: young children talk aloud to themselves while playing or solving problems, often repeating instructions adults gave them. Around school age this talk goes quiet and becomes internal, compressed into fragments that make sense only to the speaker. On this view, thinking in words is social conversation turned inward, and a tool for [[Executive Function]], used to plan, keep goals active, and steer behavior.

How common it is turns out to vary widely. Russell Hurlburt's *Descriptive Experience Sampling* gives people a beeper that sounds at random moments, then interviews them in detail about exactly what was in their experience at that instant. Inner speech appeared in roughly a quarter of samples on average, but some people had it in most samples and others almost never. In 2024 researchers proposed the term *anendophasia* for people with little or no inner speech; in experiments they found it harder to judge whether two words rhymed without saying them aloud, though everyday functioning was unaffected.

Inner speech overlaps with the "phonological loop" of [[Working Memory]], which holds words by silently rehearsing them, and is accompanied by tiny movements of the speech muscles, measurable with sensors, known as subvocalization.

Its tone matters as much as its content:

- **Rumination** is inner speech stuck in a loop, replaying the same worries without resolution, as described under [[Rumination]].
- **The inner critic** is a harsh voice narrating every mistake. Replacing it with a kinder one is a central aim of [[Self-Compassion]] and of the thought work in [[Cognitive Behavioral Therapy]].
- **Distanced self-talk** helps. Ethan Kross and colleagues found in 2014 that people who addressed themselves by their own name or as "you" before a stressful task, rather than as "I", felt less anxious and performed better, a simple tool for [[Emotional Regulation]].

Externalizing inner speech can make it clearer. Explaining a problem aloud, as in [[Rubber Duck Debugging]], or writing thoughts down forces vague fragments into full sentences, often exposing the gap in reasoning.

At the far end, voices that seem to come from outside, as in some psychiatric conditions, may arise when the brain fails to recognize inner speech as self-generated. Normally the brain predicts the sensory consequences of its own speech and dampens them, a mechanism described under [[Predictive Processing]]; when that prediction fails, one's own inner voice can feel alien.
