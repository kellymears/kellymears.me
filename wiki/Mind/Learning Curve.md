A **learning curve** plots how performance improves with practice or experience. Its characteristic shape is rapid improvement at the start, followed by gains that get progressively smaller. The first hours with a new instrument bring obvious progress. Years later, a further hour of practice yields only a small refinement.

The idea goes back to the German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus, whose 1885 studies of memorizing nonsense syllables measured how repetition improved recall and how quickly it decayed afterward; see [[Spaced Repetition]]. Industrial versions followed. In 1936, the aeronautical engineer Theodore Wright reported that each time the cumulative number of aircraft built doubled, the labor needed per plane fell by a roughly fixed percentage. In the 1960s, a consulting firm generalized this into the **experience curve**, arguing that costs fall predictably with accumulated production. The same pattern has since been observed in the falling costs of solar panels, batteries, and many manufactured goods.

In 1981, the cognitive scientists Allen Newell and Paul Rosenbloom surveyed many skills, from cigar rolling to mental arithmetic, and found that performance time typically falls as a power of the amount of practice. This **power law of practice** captures the diminishing returns: each doubling of practice buys about the same proportional improvement, so absolute gains shrink.

The shape has several consequences:

- **Early gains are cheap.** Reaching basic competence is fast, which is why beginners can feel they're progressing rapidly; see [[Pareto Principle]].
- **Late gains are expensive.** Approaching mastery takes disproportionately long, and the last stretch of a project often consumes as much effort as the rest; see [[Ninety-Ninety Rule]].
- **Plateaus happen.** Progress often stalls for a while before resuming, especially when a learner must reorganize a skill instead of refining it.

Simply repeating a skill tends to level off at a comfortable plateau. Continued improvement comes from practice that targets weaknesses, with feedback, just past current ability; see [[Deliberate Practice]] and [[Zone of Proximal Development]]. Practice that feels harder in the moment often produces more durable learning; see [[Desirable Difficulty]]. With enough repetition, skills become automatic, freeing attention for other things; see [[Habit Formation]] and [[Expert Intuition]].

Early in the curve, people often overestimate how much they've mastered, since they don't yet know what they're missing; see [[Dunning-Kruger Effect]]. Teams face their own curve when new members join, which is part of why adding people to a late project can slow it down; see [[Brooks's Law]] and [[Onboarding]].

"Steep learning curve" is usually used to mean hard to learn. On a graph of performance against time, though, a steep curve means fast improvement. The colloquial sense probably pictures a steep hill to climb. Games manage their curve deliberately, adding complexity as players master the basics; see [[Difficulty Curve]].
