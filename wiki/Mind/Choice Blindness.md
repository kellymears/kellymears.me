**Choice blindness** is the failure to notice that the outcome of a decision has been switched, combined with a willingness to justify the switched choice. The psychologists Petter Johansson and Lars Hall, at Lund University, demonstrated it in 2005, and it has become one of the clearest illustrations of how people explain their own decisions.

In the original experiment, participants were shown pairs of photographs of faces and asked which they found more attractive. The experimenter then handed over the chosen photo and asked why they preferred it. On some trials, using a magician's sleight of hand, the experimenter handed over the face they had rejected instead. Most participants didn't notice the swap, and they went on to explain their choice, sometimes citing features, such as earrings or a smile, that only the rejected face had.

Later studies extended the finding:

- **Taste and smell.** At a supermarket stall, shoppers sampled two jams or teas and picked a favorite. When given a second taste of their "chosen" one, which was actually the other, most failed to notice, even with flavors as different as cinnamon-apple and bitter grapefruit.
- **Political opinions.** Before a Swedish election, people filled out a survey on political issues. Some of their answers were secretly reversed, and many then argued for positions opposite to what they had marked.

The research builds on older work about the limits of introspection. In 1977, Richard Nisbett and Timothy Wilson reviewed studies in which people gave confident reasons for their behavior that couldn't have been the real causes. People can report what they did, but their explanations often come from general beliefs about why someone would act that way, not from access to their own mental processes. Emily Pronin later named the tendency to trust one's own introspection more than others' the **introspection illusion**; see [[Metacognition]].

The explanations people give for swapped choices are a form of [[Confabulation]]: sincere, fluent accounts constructed after the fact. Patients whose brain hemispheres have been surgically separated do something similar, inventing reasons for actions started by the hemisphere that can't speak. The process connects to [[Cognitive Dissonance]], since people reshape their reasons to fit what they believe they chose, and to [[False Memory]].

Choice blindness also relates to failures to notice visual changes; see [[Inattentional Blindness]]. People attend to what they expect and fill in the rest; see [[Predictive Processing]].

The findings have implications for surveys and research that rely on self-reports, and for persuasion, since preferences can shift once people have defended a position; see [[Framing Effect]] and [[Choice Architecture]]. They also inform debates about whether explanations produced by language models reflect how they reached an answer; see [[Chain-of-Thought Prompting]].
