**Eidetic memory** is the ability to keep a vivid image of something for a short time after it's gone. People with eidetic imagery describe "seeing" a picture after it's removed and scanning it as if it were still there, reporting details they couldn't otherwise recall. Studies from the 1960s and 1970s found it in a small minority of children and very rarely in adults, and the image usually fades within a few minutes.

The popular notion of a **photographic memory**, the ability to recall any page or scene in perfect detail long afterward, is different, and no case has held up under careful testing. A widely reported 1970 case described a woman who could reproduce random-dot patterns from memory well enough to fuse them into a three-dimensional image, but the result was never independently confirmed. Memory doesn't work like a camera. It reconstructs experiences from fragments each time they're recalled, which is why vivid memories can be wrong; see [[False Memory]] and [[Flashbulb Memory]].

Extraordinary memory does exist, but it takes other forms. The Soviet psychologist Alexander Luria studied Solomon Shereshevsky for decades and described the case in The Mind of a Mnemonist in 1968. Shereshevsky could recall long lists of numbers or nonsense words years later, helped by intense mixing of the senses, in which sounds produced colors and tastes; see [[Synesthesia]]. The gift came at a cost: forgetting was difficult, and abstract or figurative language was hard to follow.

**Hyperthymesia**, or highly superior autobiographical memory, was described in 2006. People with it can recall what happened on nearly any date of their adult lives, including the day of the week and trivial events. They're not usually better at memorizing lists or facts, and their memories turned out to be as vulnerable to distortion as anyone's in at least one study. Some describe it as burdensome, with past events replaying uninvited; see [[Rumination]] and [[Episodic Memory]].

Memory champions, who memorize decks of cards in seconds, mostly report ordinary memories. They use learned techniques such as placing images along an imagined route; see [[Method of Loci]].

The rarity of perfect memory may be by design. Forgetting removes detail that isn't needed and keeps the gist, a kind of compression that makes memories easier to generalize from; see [[Data Compression]] and [[Memory Consolidation]]. A brain that kept everything would struggle to find the patterns that make memory useful. Expectations fill in much of what we think we see and remember; see [[Predictive Processing]].

At the other extreme, some people have no voluntary visual imagery at all, and they can still remember well by other means; see [[Aphantasia]]. Holding and manipulating information over a few seconds is a separate, limited system; see [[Working Memory]].
