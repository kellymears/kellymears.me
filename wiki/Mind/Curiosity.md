**Curiosity** is the desire to know or learn, pursued for its own sake rather than for an external reward. Psychologists have long distinguished *perceptual curiosity*, the pull toward novel sights and sounds that animals share, from *epistemic curiosity*, the human desire for knowledge. Daniel Berlyne set out this distinction in the 1950s and 1960s, linking curiosity to novelty, complexity, and uncertainty.

The most influential modern account is George Loewenstein's **information gap theory**, published in 1994. Curiosity, he argued, arises when attention is drawn to a gap between what one knows and what one wants to know. The gap feels like a mild deprivation, an itch, and closing it is rewarding. Two consequences follow:

- **Some knowledge is needed first.** People are rarely curious about topics they know nothing about, since they can't perceive the gap. Curiosity grows as knowledge accumulates, then drops when the gap seems either trivially small or impossibly large.
- **Gaps can be engineered.** Mystery stories, cliffhangers, and puzzles create a specific question and withhold the answer. So do "curiosity gap" headlines, which promise a revelation and deliver a click, a tactic that exploits the itch without satisfying it.

Brain-imaging research supports the reward framing. In a 2009 study by Min Jeong Kang and colleagues, curiosity about trivia answers activated reward-related regions. A 2014 study by Matthias Gruber and colleagues found that when people were curious, they remembered not only the answers but also unrelated faces shown during the wait, suggesting curiosity puts the brain in a state primed for learning. Dopamine systems that signal [[Reward Prediction Error]] appear to respond to information much as they do to food or money.

Curiosity has clear uses:

- **Learning.** Posing a question before giving the answer, or asking learners to predict first, creates a gap worth closing, related to the generation effect in [[Desirable Difficulty]].
- **Design and storytelling.** Games reward exploration with hidden areas, and [[Environmental Storytelling]] leaves clues for players to piece together. Hiding complexity until it is wanted, through [[Progressive Disclosure]], leaves room for curiosity to lead.
- **Motivation.** Curiosity is a core form of intrinsic motivation in [[Self-Determination Theory]].

It also has a shadow. The pull to close one gap after another can lead down a [[Rabbit Hole]], and endless feeds supply an inexhaustible series of small gaps, a dynamic behind [[Doomscrolling]]. For some people, interests are intense and deep rather than broad, as described by [[Monotropism]], and following them can bring on [[Hyperfocus]].

Curiosity varies between people. *Need for cognition*, the enjoyment of effortful thinking, is a related, measurable trait. People also differ in how they feel about uncertainty: some find unanswered questions delightful, others aversive, and that tolerance shapes how freely they explore.
