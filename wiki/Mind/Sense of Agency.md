**The sense of agency** is the feeling of controlling one's own actions and, through them, events in the world. Reaching for a cup, flicking a switch, or pressing a key, a person normally feels without thinking about it that they did it. Psychologists and neuroscientists study how the brain produces this feeling, and when it goes wrong.

One influential account is the comparator model. When the brain sends a command to move, it also predicts the sensory consequences. If what happens matches the prediction, the action feels self-caused; if not, something else seems responsible; see [[Predictive Processing]]. The same mechanism explains why people can't tickle themselves: the predicted touch is dampened, so it feels weaker than an unexpected one.

Timing is part of the feeling. In experiments by Patrick Haggard and colleagues in the early 2000s, people judged a voluntary action and a tone that followed it as closer together in time than they really were, an effect called **intentional binding**. Delays and inconsistencies weaken the sense of control, which is why interfaces feel sluggish and games feel unresponsive when feedback lags; see [[Input Latency]] and [[Response Time Limits]]. Game designers bend rules slightly so players feel in control even when their timing was a little off; see [[Coyote Time]].

The feeling isn't infallible:

- **Ideomotor movements.** People moving a Ouija planchette or dowsing rod often deny moving it, because tiny unconscious movements don't register as their own; see [[Ideomotor Effect]].
- **Illusions of control.** People can feel they caused outcomes they had nothing to do with, as in experiments the psychologist Daniel Wegner described in the 2002 book The Illusion of Conscious Will; see [[Locus of Control]].
- **Clinical disturbances.** Some people with schizophrenia experience their own thoughts or movements as controlled by outside forces, and rare brain injuries produce a hand that seems to act on its own.

The **Libet experiment** brought agency into debates about free will. In the early 1980s, Benjamin Libet recorded a buildup of brain activity, the readiness potential, that began a few hundred milliseconds before people reported deciding to move. Some concluded that the brain decides before awareness catches up. Later work questioned that interpretation, suggesting the buildup may partly reflect random fluctuations that happen to cross a threshold, rather than a decision already made. The debate continues.

Agency is distinct from body ownership, the feeling that a body part is one's own, though the two usually go together; see [[Rubber Hand Illusion]]. It also matters for motivation: experiences of making things happen support persistence and well-being, while repeated loss of control can produce passivity; see [[Self-Efficacy]], [[Self-Determination Theory]], and [[Learned Helplessness]]. Clear feedback in tools and interfaces helps people feel in charge; see [[Haptic Feedback]].
