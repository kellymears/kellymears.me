The **window of tolerance** is the range of arousal within which a person can function well: think clearly, feel emotions without being swept away by them, and respond to what is happening rather than react. The psychiatrist Dan Siegel introduced the term in his 1999 book *The Developing Mind*, and trauma therapists such as Pat Ogden built it into their practice. It is a clinical model for describing states, not something measured in a lab.

Outside the window lie two failure states:

- **Hyperarousal**, above it. Racing heart, spinning thoughts, irritability, panic, anger: the fight-or-flight end. Small things feel like emergencies, and careful reasoning goes offline.
- **Hypoarousal**, below it. Numbness, fog, flatness, collapse, dissociation: the shutdown or freeze end. It can look calm from outside, which is why it is often missed.

The width of the window varies between people and from day to day. Poor sleep, hunger, illness, chronic stress, and accumulated [[Allostatic Load]] narrow it, so a minor frustration that would be fine on a good day tips someone over. Trauma can narrow it lastingly, and so can years of [[Masking]]. Support, rest, and safety widen it.

The model connects several Mind ideas:

- **[[Yerkes-Dodson Law]]** describes the same curve from the performance side: too little arousal and too much both impair.
- **[[Sensory Overload]]** pushes an autistic or sensitive person above the window, and a meltdown or shutdown is what leaving it looks like. [[Autistic Burnout]] can feel like a window that has shrunk to almost nothing.
- **[[Emotional Regulation]]** is largely the work of staying in, or getting back into, the window.
- **[[Spoon Theory]]** offers a related budgeting picture of the same limits.

Getting back in depends on the direction. Above the window, the goal is to bring arousal down: slow exhalation, cold water, movement, a quieter room. Long exhales engage the [[Vagus Nerve]] and slow the heart, visible as rising [[Heart Rate Variability]]. Below it, the goal is to bring arousal up gently: movement, temperature, strong tastes, orienting to the room. [[Stimming]] often does both jobs, regulating sensation in either direction. Noticing the shift early, through [[Interoception]], leaves more room to act before the window is left entirely.

Talking or reasoning rarely helps once someone is far outside the window, because the faculties needed for it are what has gone offline. Helping someone return first and discussing later is the practical rule for parents, partners, and teams in the middle of an incident alike.

Siegel's model is often paired with polyvagal theory, which proposes specific nerve pathways for each state. That theory's physiology is disputed, but the window itself does not depend on it.
