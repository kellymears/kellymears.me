**Monotropism** is a theory of autistic cognition proposed by Dinah Murray, Mike Lesser, and Wenn Lawson in 2005. It holds that the defining feature is how **attention is distributed**: a monotropic mind concentrates its available attention on a small number of interests at a time and engages with them very deeply, while a *polytropic* mind spreads attention thinly across many things at once. Murray and Lawson were autistic, and the theory was built from the inside rather than inferred from outside observation.

The useful thing about the theory is how much it explains with one mechanism:

- **Intense interests** (often called *special interests*) are where attention naturally pools. Within them, learning is fast, detailed, and rewarding.
- **Transitions are expensive.** Pulling attention out of a deep channel and building a new one takes real effort, so unexpected changes of plan are disproportionately disruptive. This is [[Attention Residue]] at a larger scale, and it accounts for what is often called *autistic inertia*: difficulty starting, and also difficulty stopping.
- **Sensory and social overload.** Things outside the current channel either go unnoticed or arrive all at once, unfiltered, and can overwhelm.
- **Missed peripheral cues.** Social information that polytropic people pick up in the background, such as a shift in tone or a glance at the clock, falls outside the tunnel. That is a difference in where attention goes, not a lack of caring.
- **[[Hyperfocus]]** is the same mechanism at full depth.

The theory is useful in practice because it turns judgments into mechanisms. "Rigid" becomes "transitions are expensive, so give warning". "Obsessive" becomes "attention pools, so connect the work to the interest". "Oblivious" becomes "that cue was outside the channel, so say it out loud". Those accommodations are cheap. Warnings before changes, written agendas, and uninterrupted blocks help nearly everyone, which is the general pattern in [[Neurodiversity]] accommodations.

It also connects to design. A [[Focus Trap]] and careful [[Focus Management]] in interfaces exist because sudden, unannounced shifts of context are disorienting. A monotropic mind experiences unannounced context shifts in the world the same way.
