**Emotional regulation** covers everything people do, consciously or not, to influence which emotions they have, when they have them, and how they experience and express them. It doesn't mean suppressing feelings. It means having some say over their course. James Gross's *process model*, the most widely used framework, sorts strategies by when they act on an emotion as it unfolds:

- **Situation selection.** Avoiding or seeking situations, such as skipping a party or calling a friend.
- **Situation modification.** Changing the situation, such as asking for the music to be turned down.
- **Attentional deployment.** Shifting focus, through distraction or concentrating on something else.
- **Cognitive change.** Reinterpreting what the situation means, called *reappraisal*: "they're not ignoring me, they're busy."
- **Response modulation.** Acting on the emotion once it's there, by suppressing expression, breathing slowly, or moving.

Studies consistently find that strategies acting early, such as reappraisal, work better than suppression, which leaves the feeling intact, costs effort, raises physiological arousal, and tends to impair memory and relationships. Suppression is still useful in short bursts, such as staying calm during an emergency. The problem is relying on it habitually.

**Dysregulation** describes emotions that arrive faster and stronger than usual and take longer to settle. It is common in ADHD, where it is increasingly seen as a core feature rather than a side effect, and it appears in [[Rejection Sensitivity]]. In autism, regulation can be harder because of [[Interoception]] differences: alexithymia, difficulty identifying one's own feelings, makes it hard to manage an emotion one can't yet name. [[Sensory Overload]] shrinks the capacity for regulation, and so do exhaustion, hunger, and [[Autistic Burnout]].

Regulation isn't purely individual. **Co-regulation**, calming through another person's steady presence, is how infants learn to regulate at all, and adults still use it. [[Body Doubling]] partly works this way. [[Stimming]] is a regulation strategy, which is why suppressing it can make things worse. Many productivity problems are regulation problems in disguise. [[Procrastination]] in particular is often avoidance of a bad feeling rather than of the task, which makes regulation skills a more direct fix than any time-management system. Regulation also takes resources from [[Executive Function]], and running low on them is one way to read [[Spoon Theory]].
