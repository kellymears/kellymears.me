**Impostor syndrome** is the persistent feeling of being a fraud: believing your success is undeserved, owed to luck or to fooling people, and fearing that others will eventually find you out. It persists in the face of evidence to the contrary. Promotions, praise, and finished work do not settle it. Psychologists Pauline Clance and Suzanne Imes described it in 1978 as the *impostor phenomenon*, studying high-achieving women. Later work found it across genders and fields. It is not a clinical diagnosis.

The mechanism is attribution. Successes are explained externally ("the interviewer was easy," "anyone could have done it") and failures internally ("this proves I'm not good enough"). Since success never counts as evidence, [[Self-Efficacy]] fails to grow from the mastery experiences that should build it. A common cycle follows: anxiety before a task, then either heavy overpreparation or [[Procrastination]] and a frantic finish, then success, then relief that gets credited to the overwork or to luck. The cycle repeats with the next task.

Some features of skilled work make it worse:

- **Knowing what you don't know.** Expertise reveals how big a field is, so the more you learn, the less you feel you know. This is the mirror image of the [[Dunning-Kruger Effect]], where the less skilled overrate themselves.
- **Comparing inside to outside.** You see your own struggles and others' finished results, a sampling problem close to [[Survivorship Bias]]. Others' competence looks effortless because the effort is hidden.
- **Ambiguous standards.** In fields like software, where nobody knows everything, there is always something to be ignorant of. [[Code Review]] and [[Pair Programming]] can expose gaps, which feels threatening, or normalize them, which helps.

It overlaps with perfectionism, [[Rumination]] over mistakes, and [[Rejection Sensitivity]]. For neurodivergent people it often merges with [[Masking]]: if you spend effort appearing to function like others, feeling like a fraud is partly accurate about the performance, though not about the competence.

There is a strong critique of the concept. Calling it a syndrome locates the problem inside the individual. People who are the only one like them in a room, or who are questioned more than their peers, may feel like outsiders because they are being treated as outsiders. A 2021 *Harvard Business Review* essay argued that telling women they have impostor syndrome mislabels a reasonable reaction to bias as a personal flaw.

What helps is making evidence count: keeping a record of finished work and feedback, talking openly about doubt, which reveals how common it is, and working where mistakes are discussed without blame, as in a [[Blameless Postmortem]].
