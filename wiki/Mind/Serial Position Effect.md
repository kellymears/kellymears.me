The **serial position effect** is the tendency to recall the first and last items of a sequence better than the ones in the middle. Plot how often each position in a list is remembered and the result is a U-shaped curve: high at the start, high at the end, sagging in between. Hermann Ebbinghaus noticed it in his nineteenth-century memory experiments, and Bennet Murdock mapped the curve carefully in 1962.

The curve has two parts with different causes:

- **The primacy effect.** Early items are remembered well because they get more rehearsal. When the first word arrives, it has your full attention; by the tenth, attention is split across everything so far. Early items are therefore more likely to make it into long-term memory.
- **The recency effect.** The last few items are remembered well because they are still held in [[Working Memory]] at the moment of recall, so they can simply be read off.

Experiments separated the two. When people are distracted for about 30 seconds after the list, for instance by counting backward, the recency effect disappears while primacy stays, showing that recency depends on short-term storage. Speeding up the list, which leaves less time to rehearse, weakens primacy but leaves recency intact. Results like these supported the idea that short- and long-term memory are distinct systems, though later research found recency effects over much longer spans too, such as remembering recent holidays better than earlier ones.

The middle loses out because it gets neither advantage. Items there arrive when attention is already divided and are pushed out of working memory by what follows, which adds to [[Cognitive Load]] for anyone trying to hold a long list.

The effect shows up well beyond lab lists:

- **Interfaces.** Users notice and remember the first and last items of a menu or navigation bar most, so important options belong at the ends. The [[Visual Hierarchy]] of a page interacts with this, and very long menus also slow choice under [[Hick's Law]].
- **Persuasion and evaluation.** Opening and closing arguments carry extra weight, and in sequences of candidates, auditions, or wine tastings, position can affect judgments independently of quality. First impressions tie primacy to the [[Halo Effect]].
- **Experiences.** How an experience ends shapes memory of the whole, a related idea captured by the [[Peak-End Rule]].
- **Learning.** Study sessions favor what comes first and last, one reason breaking material into shorter sessions helps, alongside [[Spaced Repetition]].

A striking item in the middle can escape the slump, which is the [[Von Restorff Effect]]. Together these effects describe memory as selective by design: attention, rehearsal, and distinctiveness decide what gets kept, guided by [[Selective Attention]] rather than by an even record of everything encountered.
