**Mental rotation** is imagining an object turning in order to compare it with another or to see how it would look from a different angle. Deciding whether a shape will fit a gap, whether a map matches the street, or whether two photos show the same chair can all involve it.

The landmark experiment came from Roger Shepard and Jacqueline Metzler in 1971. They showed people pairs of drawings of three-dimensional objects built from cubes and asked whether the two were the same object rotated, or mirror images. The time to answer rose in a straight line with the angle between them: the farther one object had to turn to match the other, the longer people took, about a second for every 60 degrees in their study. People behaved as if they were rotating an internal image at a steady speed, passing through each intermediate position, as a physical object would.

The result was a landmark for cognitive psychology because it seemed to show that mental images are real, measurable things with spatial properties. It fed a long debate. The psychologist Stephen Kosslyn argued that the brain holds picture-like representations; Zenon Pylyshyn argued that images are an illusion and the mind uses descriptions, with the timing reflecting people's tacit knowledge of how rotation works. Brain imaging later showed that visual and motor planning areas are active during mental rotation, consistent with simulation; see [[Embodied Cognition]].

Interestingly, people who report no visual imagery can usually still solve rotation problems, often accurately but with different strategies, such as reasoning about features piece by piece; see [[Aphantasia]].

Mental rotation draws heavily on limited attention and memory capacity; see [[Working Memory]]. It is part of a broader spatial ability that predicts success in engineering, architecture, and other technical fields. Average performance differs between men and women on standard tests, one of the larger sex differences in cognitive tests, but the gap varies by test and culture, and practice reduces it substantially.

Practice improves performance. Playing action video games and *Tetris* improves rotation skill, though studies disagree about how far gains transfer beyond the trained shapes; see [[Tetris]] and [[Neuroplasticity]]. Drawing, building, and spatial play in childhood are associated with stronger spatial skills, though these associations are not proof of cause.

Computers rotate objects precisely and instantly, using matrices or quaternions rather than step-by-step imagined turning; see [[Transformation Matrix]] and [[Quaternion]]. Technical drawing standards exist partly because translating between flat views and solid objects is hard for people; see [[Isometric Projection]].

Mental rotation also figures in navigation, where people must align a map with their surroundings. Many find it easier to turn the map than to rotate it mentally; see [[Cognitive Map]].
