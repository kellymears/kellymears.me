**Sensory overload** happens when the input reaching the senses (sound, light, touch, smell, movement) exceeds what the brain can process at that moment. Everyone experiences it occasionally, in a loud bar, a crowded station, or a bright and chaotic store. For many neurodivergent people the threshold is much lower, the experience more intense, and the recovery slower. Differences in sensory reactivity were added to the diagnostic criteria for autism in 2013, and they are common in ADHD as well.

Sensory processing differences run in both directions, often in the same person:

- **Hyper-reactivity**: sounds that others filter out, such as a ticking clock, a humming fridge, or a nearby conversation, remain in the foreground. Fluorescent hum, [[LED Flicker]], clothing tags, and particular textures can be intolerable.
- **Hypo-reactivity**: some signals barely register, such as temperature, pain, or the body's own state, a pattern related to low [[Interoception]].
- **Sensory seeking**: actively looking for strong input, such as deep pressure, motion, or loud music, often for regulation. [[Stimming]] is partly this.

[[Monotropism]] offers one explanation of why overload hits so hard. If attention concentrates into a narrow channel, input outside it is not filtered gradually but either ignored or let in all at once.

Overload also accumulates. Input that is tolerable for ten minutes becomes unbearable after two hours, especially when combined with social demands and [[Masking]], which consume the same capacity. At the extreme, people describe a **meltdown**, an involuntary loss of control that looks like a tantrum but is not chosen, or a **shutdown**, a withdrawal in which speech and responsiveness drop. Both are overload responses, not behavior to be disciplined.

The most effective remedies change the environment rather than the person. Noise-canceling headphones, sunglasses, warm dim lighting rather than harsh overhead light (see [[Color Temperature]]), a quiet room to retreat to, and advance notice of loud events all help. Some stores now run *sensory-friendly hours* with lowered music and lights. In interfaces, the same principle drives [[Reduced Motion]] settings, muted notification defaults, and avoiding autoplaying media. These are [[Accessibility]] features that many users who would never call themselves sensitive also prefer.
