The **framing effect** is the finding that people make different choices depending on how the same information is presented. Describing an outcome as a gain or a loss, a success rate or a failure rate, changes decisions even when the facts are identical. It is one of the clearest demonstrations that preferences aren't fixed values people look up, but are partly built at the moment of choice.

The best-known study is Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman's 1981 "Asian disease problem." Participants imagined an outbreak expected to kill 600 people and chose between two programs:

- **Gain frame.** Program A saves 200 people. Program B has a one-third chance of saving all 600 and a two-thirds chance of saving no one. Most chose A, the sure thing.
- **Loss frame.** Under Program C, 400 people die. Under Program D, there's a one-third chance nobody dies and a two-thirds chance all 600 die. Most chose D, the gamble.

A and C are the same program, as are B and D. Only the words changed. The reversal follows from [[Loss Aversion]] and prospect theory: people avoid risk when choices are framed as gains and seek it when they are framed as losses.

Framing comes in several forms:

- **Attribute framing.** Ground beef labeled "75 percent lean" is rated better than beef labeled "25 percent fat." A surgery with "90 percent survival" is chosen more often than one with "10 percent mortality," even among doctors.
- **Goal framing.** Messages stressing what people lose by not acting, such as not getting screened, can persuade more than messages stressing what they gain by acting.
- **Number formats.** Gerd Gigerenzer showed that people reason about risk far better with natural frequencies, "8 out of 1,000," than with percentages and conditional probabilities, which helps avoid the [[Base Rate Fallacy]].

Framing is unavoidable. Any description must pick words, an order, and a reference point, so the responsible question is which frame is fair, not whether to frame. That is the same point [[Choice Architecture]] makes about defaults. Clear [[Microcopy]] and [[Plain Language]] choose frames that help people understand, while a [[Dark Pattern]] picks them to mislead.

The idea extends well beyond individual choices. Political and media framing decides which aspects of an issue seem relevant, shifting what feels reasonable to discuss, as the [[Overton Window]] describes. Whether language shapes thought more deeply is the question behind [[Linguistic Relativity]].

Framing also works as a thinking tool. The [[Pre-mortem]] reframes a plan as having already failed so people can say what went wrong. Reframing a situation is a core technique in [[Cognitive Behavioral Therapy]] and in [[Emotional Regulation]]. Deliberately describing a decision in both frames, and checking whether your answer changes, is a simple guard against the effect.
