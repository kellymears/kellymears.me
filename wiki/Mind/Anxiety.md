**Anxiety** is the state of preparing for a threat that may come, as opposed to fear, which answers a threat that is already here. Fear sees the bear. Anxiety listens for one in the dark. Both run on the same machinery: a racing heart, quickened breathing, tense muscles and a narrowing of attention toward danger, the package described in the [[Fight-or-Flight Response]].

In moderate amounts anxiety is useful. It sharpens preparation before an exam or a performance, and the [[Yerkes-Dodson Law]] describes the familiar pattern in which a little arousal helps and a lot hurts. The trouble starts when the alarm fires too often, too loudly, or for threats that are unlikely or impossible to resolve. Anxiety disorders are among the most common mental health conditions, and surveys in the United States put the lifetime prevalence somewhere near a third of adults.

Several forms are recognized. **Generalized anxiety disorder** is persistent, hard-to-control worry about many things at once, a close relative of [[Rumination]]. Social anxiety centers on being judged, and it inflates the sense of being watched that the [[Spotlight Effect]] describes. A **panic attack** is a sudden surge of intense fear that peaks within minutes, often with chest pain, dizziness and the conviction that something catastrophic is happening. Panic disorder develops when people start to fear the attacks themselves and to scan their own bodies for warning signs; see [[Interoception]]. Fast breathing during an attack can produce tingling and lightheadedness that feel like confirmation; see [[Hyperventilation]].

A recurring ingredient is **intolerance of uncertainty**, the sense that not knowing is itself unbearable. It overlaps with [[Ambiguity Aversion]] and [[Need for Closure]], and it explains why reassurance helps only briefly. Each answer quiets the question until the next one arrives.

The mechanism that keeps anxiety going is usually avoidance. Steering clear of a feared situation brings immediate relief, and that relief rewards the avoidance, a textbook case of negative reinforcement in [[Operant Conditioning]]. The person never learns that the situation was survivable. Subtler **safety behaviors** do the same: always sitting near the exit, carrying medication that is never taken, rehearsing every sentence before a phone call.

That is why the best-supported treatments involve approaching rather than escaping. [[Exposure Therapy]] has people meet feared situations gradually until the alarm recalibrates, and [[Cognitive Behavioral Therapy]] adds work on the predictions that drive it. Slow breathing can calm the body directly, which is part of why paced breathing appears in many programs; see [[Heart Rate Variability]]. Medication helps many people too. The goal is rarely to eliminate anxiety, only to return it to a size that fits the actual risk, inside a person's [[Window of Tolerance]].
