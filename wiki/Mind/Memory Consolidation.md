**Memory consolidation** is the process that turns a fragile new memory into a stable, long-lasting one. A freshly formed memory is easily disrupted by a blow to the head, a drug, or simply learning something similar right afterward. Given time, it becomes resistant. Georg Müller and Alfons Pilzecker named the process in 1900 after noticing that new learning interfered with material studied just before it.

It happens on two timescales:

- **Synaptic consolidation** takes minutes to hours. The connections that encoded the experience are strengthened by protein synthesis, a form of [[Neuroplasticity]]. Block that synthesis in an animal shortly after training and the memory never stabilizes.
- **Systems consolidation** takes weeks to years. Memories of events depend at first on the hippocampus, which binds together the scattered cortical pieces of an experience. Over time the cortex comes to hold them on its own. The patient Henry Molaison, whose hippocampi were removed in 1953, could not form new lasting memories but kept many older ones, the pattern this model predicts.

**Sleep** does much of the work. During slow-wave sleep, the hippocampus replays recent experience at high speed, a pattern first recorded in rats running mazes in 1994, and the cortex gradually absorbs it. REM sleep seems to help with emotional memory and with linking new knowledge to old. Pulling an all-nighter before an exam skips the very step that would have kept the material. [[Sleep Stages]] covers the cycle, and [[Motor Learning]] shows the same benefit for physical skills.

Spacing study over days gives consolidation repeated chances to work, part of why [[Spaced Repetition]] beats cramming, and part of why [[Desirable Difficulty]] pays off later rather than in the session. New material similar to old competes for the same traces, and distinct [[Context-Dependent Memory]] cues help keep them apart.

In 2000 Karim Nader showed that **reconsolidation** reopens the process. Recalling a consolidated memory makes it briefly unstable again, and it must be stored anew. During that window it can be updated, strengthened, or distorted. This helps explain [[False Memory]]: each retelling is a rewrite, not a playback. It also suggests therapies that weaken traumatic memories by disrupting their restorage.

Machine learning ran into the same problem. A neural network trained on a new task tends to overwrite what it knew, called *catastrophic forgetting*, a known hazard of [[Fine-Tuning]]. The complementary learning systems theory of 1995 argued that the brain avoids this by learning fast in the hippocampus and slowly in the cortex, interleaving old memories with new during replay. Reinforcement learning borrowed the idea as *experience replay*, revisiting stored past episodes while training.
