The **ideomotor effect** is the tendency to make small physical movements in response to a thought or expectation, without being aware of making them. The physiologist William Carpenter named it in 1852. Thinking of a direction, an answer, or an outcome can be enough to move the hands toward it, and because the movement isn't felt as chosen, it seems to come from outside.

The effect explains several practices once taken as evidence of hidden forces:

- **Table-turning.** In the 1850s, groups at séances rested their hands on tables that then moved and spun. In 1853 Michael Faraday placed a device between the sitters' hands and the tabletop that showed whether the hands moved before the table did. They did, and once sitters could see the indicator, the table stopped moving.
- **The Ouija board.** Sold commercially from 1890, it has users rest fingers on a pointer that drifts toward letters. Studies in which participants were blindfolded, sometimes with the board secretly rotated, found that the answers became gibberish.
- **Dowsing.** Rods or pendulums that seem to dip over water or metal respond to the dowser's expectations. Controlled tests, where the dowser doesn't know where the target is, consistently find results no better than chance; see [[Blinding]].
- **Pendulums** held over a pregnant belly or a map.

The effect is a reminder that people have only partial access to the causes of their own actions; see [[Introspection illusion]] and [[Dual Process Theory]]. Thoughts prime related movements automatically; see [[Priming]] and [[Embodied Cognition]]. Seeing meaning in random input completes the loop; see [[Pareidolia]].

Its most serious consequence was **facilitated communication**, popular from the early 1990s. Facilitators supported the hands of nonspeaking autistic people and others with disabilities as they typed, producing fluent messages. Controlled tests in which the facilitator and the speaker were shown different pictures found that the typed answers matched what the facilitator saw. Some messages included accusations of abuse that led to families being separated. Professional bodies now reject the technique. The episode is often cited in debates about respecting the communication of disabled people without imposing others' words on them; see [[Neurodiversity]].

Related effects occur without touch. The horse Clever Hans appeared to do arithmetic in early-1900s Berlin, until the psychologist Oskar Pfungst showed that it was reading unintentional cues in its questioners' posture. Animals and people alike respond to cues the cue-giver doesn't know they're giving.

Some products have exploited the effect outright. Swivel-antenna "detectors" sold to security forces in the 2000s had no working electronics; one seller was convicted of fraud in Britain in 2013; see [[Confidence Trick]].

The same mechanism operates in [[Tarot]] readings and many alternative therapies, where expectations shape outcomes; see [[Placebo Effect]] and [[Confirmation Bias]].
