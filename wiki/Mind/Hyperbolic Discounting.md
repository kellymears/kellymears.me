**Hyperbolic discounting** is the pattern in which people value a reward less the further in the future it lies, but not at a steady rate. The drop is steep over the first hours and days and flattens out over months and years. A week's delay matters enormously when it means waiting until next week, and hardly at all when it means a year from now versus a year and a week.

Classical economics assumed *exponential* discounting: a constant rate per unit of time, like interest. Under that model, preferences stay consistent. If someone prefers $110 in 31 days to $100 in 30 days, they should also prefer $110 tomorrow to $100 today. Real people often don't. From a distance they choose the larger, later reward; as the smaller one becomes immediate, they switch. This is **time inconsistency**, and the economist David Laibson and others modeled it as a special premium on *now*, often called present bias; see [[Procrastination]].

The pattern explains a familiar list of failures:

- **Planning versus doing.** On Sunday night, Monday's workout is obviously worth it. On Monday morning, the bed wins.
- **Saving.** Retirement is distant and abstract; this month's purchase is concrete.
- **Deferred maintenance.** Code shortcuts pay off today and cost later, the same shape as [[Technical Debt]].
- **Underestimated time.** Plans are made with the distant self in mind; see [[Planning Fallacy]].

The **marshmallow test**, run by Walter Mischel at Stanford from the late 1960s, offered preschoolers one treat now or two if they waited. Children who waited longer later showed better outcomes in some follow-ups, and the study became a parable about willpower. A larger 2018 replication found a much weaker link once family background and early ability were accounted for; see [[Confounding]]. Waiting also depends on trust: children who had just seen an adult break a promise waited far less, a rational response when the later reward may never come.

Steep discounting is more common under stress, scarcity, and in [[ADHD]], where the future can feel unreal until it's close; see [[Time Blindness]]. That reframes it as a problem of how the future is represented, not just a lack of character.

Since the bias is predictable, people can plan around it:

- **Bind the future self** while the choice is still distant; see [[Commitment Device]].
- **Make the future concrete** with specific plans; see [[Implementation Intention]].
- **Change defaults** so the patient option requires no action, as with automatic enrollment in savings plans; see [[Choice Architecture]].
- **Bring rewards closer** by pairing a dull task with something enjoyable now.

Designers exploit the same pattern: daily login rewards, timed offers, and instant gratification loops are built to win the present-biased moment; see [[Gacha Monetization]].
