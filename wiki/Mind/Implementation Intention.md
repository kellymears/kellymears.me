An **implementation intention** is a plan written as an if-then rule tying a specific action to a specific situation: "When I finish my morning coffee, I will open the draft and write one sentence." The psychologist Peter Gollwitzer introduced the concept in the 1990s to contrast it with a *goal intention* ("I will write more"), which states what someone wants but not when, where, or how they will act.

The evidence is unusually solid for a self-help idea. A 2006 meta-analysis by Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran, covering 94 studies, found a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment across domains: exercise, screenings, medication, studying, eating habits. It is cheap, takes a minute, and works on its own.

The mechanism is delegation. Forming the if-then link makes the situation mentally salient, so the cue gets noticed, and it makes the response more automatic once the cue appears. The decision has already been made in advance, so it doesn't have to be made again in the moment, when [[Executive Function]] is weakest. That is particularly useful for task *initiation*, where many intentions die, and for people with [[Time Blindness]], since the trigger is an event rather than a clock time that has to be felt approaching.

Several refinements make plans work better:

- **Concrete cues.** "After I sit down at my desk" beats "in the morning". The cue has to be something that will actually be noticed.
- **Obstacle plans.** "If I want to check messages, then I will write them on a list first" handles the predictable derailment. Combined with first picturing the goal and what stands in its way, this is known as mental contrasting with implementation intentions (MCII, or WOOP).
- **Anchoring to existing routines,** sometimes called habit stacking. Attaching a new action to an established one borrows the old habit as the cue.

For programmers the structure will feel familiar. An implementation intention is an event handler for behavior: it registers a callback on a situation instead of polling willpower. It is closer to a [[Webhook]] than to a [[Cron]] job, and close to a [[Finite State Machine]] transition defined in advance. It also closes the open loop described by the [[Zeigarnik Effect]], since a credible plan quiets intrusive thoughts about an unfinished goal even before it is done.
