The **fresh start effect** is the boost in motivation people feel at the start of a new period. Gym visits rise at the beginning of the week, the month, and the year, and after birthdays and holidays. Searches for "diet" spike on New Year's Day and on Mondays. People commit to goals at these points far more than at other times.

The psychologists Hengchen Dai, Katherine Milkman, and Jason Riis named and measured the effect in 2014. Their explanation is that **temporal landmarks** divide the stream of time into chapters. A new chapter lets people set past failures aside as belonging to an old self, and look at the bigger picture rather than the day-to-day grind. The effect works best at landmarks that feel meaningful: a birthday more than a random Tuesday, the first of the month more than the eleventh.

Landmarks aren't only calendar dates. Moving to a new home, starting a new job, recovering from an illness, or returning from a trip can each mark a fresh start. Habits are tied to context, so a new setting also breaks the cues behind old patterns; see [[Habit Formation]].

The effect has limits. Fresh-start resolutions are often abandoned within weeks, because the spike in motivation fades while the underlying difficulty doesn't. Motivation is a good reason to start, but not a plan:

- **Make it concrete.** Decide exactly when and where the new behavior will happen; see [[Implementation Intention]].
- **Lock it in.** Use the motivated moment to set up structure that survives later dips; see [[Commitment Device]].
- **Plan for lapses.** A missed day isn't a reason to wait for the next landmark. Treating a slip as total failure, the "what-the-hell effect," is what usually ends a streak; see [[Self-Compassion]].

People can also create landmarks deliberately. Framing a date as "the first day of spring" or "the start of the new quarter" works better than an unmarked date. Sending reminders at landmarks increases take-up, which is why organizations time campaigns for January; see [[Choice Architecture]].

Teams use the same structure. Sprints, quarters, and retrospectives create regular chapter breaks, where it's natural to look back and choose a new focus; see [[Agile Software Development]]. New major versions let projects drop old baggage; see [[Semantic Versioning]]. The risk mirrors the personal one: a rewrite or reorganization timed to a landmark can feel like a clean slate while carrying the old problems forward.

The fresh start effect is the opposite of what happens with an unfinished task, which keeps pulling at attention until closed; see [[Zeigarnik Effect]]. A landmark can serve as a closing point, letting someone deliberately put old intentions down.

For those who find time hard to track, external landmarks give structure the calendar otherwise lacks; see [[Time Blindness]].
