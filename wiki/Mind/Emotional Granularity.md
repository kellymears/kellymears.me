**Emotional granularity** is how precisely a person distinguishes and names their feelings. Someone with low granularity feels "bad" or "stressed"; someone with high granularity can tell apprehension from dread, irritation from resentment, or loneliness from boredom. The psychologist Lisa Feldman Barrett introduced the concept in the 1990s, noticing that some study participants used emotion words almost interchangeably while others drew fine distinctions.

Barrett's broader *theory of constructed emotion*, set out in her 2017 book *How Emotions Are Made*, explains why this matters. On her account emotions are not fixed circuits that fire in response to events. The brain constructs them, interpreting bodily sensations in context using learned concepts, an instance of [[Predictive Processing]]. A racing heart and tight chest might become fear before a talk, excitement before a race, or anger in an argument. The concepts available shape what is felt. More concepts mean more precise construction, and more precise feelings point to more specific responses.

The research supports the idea that granularity helps:

- **Better regulation.** People who differentiate negative emotions use more varied and effective strategies under stress, as described under [[Emotional Regulation]], and are less likely to binge drink, lash out, or self-harm when upset.
- **Less rumination.** A vague, global sense of distress invites circling, while a named, specific feeling suggests what to do next, an escape from [[Rumination]].
- **Affect labeling.** Simply putting feelings into words reduces their intensity. A 2007 brain-imaging study by Matthew Lieberman found that labeling the emotion in a face dampened amygdala response, popularized as "name it to tame it".

Granularity depends partly on noticing bodily signals in the first place, the sense of [[Interoception]]. *Alexithymia*, difficulty identifying and describing one's own emotions, is the far end of low granularity. It is more common in autistic people, though not universal, and is part of why emotions can arrive late or unannounced, sometimes only as physical symptoms or as sudden overwhelm past the [[Window of Tolerance]].

Granularity can be developed:

- **Learning words.** Emotion wheels and lists offer distinctions to try on. Other languages contribute concepts English lacks, such as German *Schadenfreude* or Portuguese *saudade*, an everyday case of [[Linguistic Relativity]].
- **Checking in.** Periodically pausing to ask "what exactly am I feeling, and where?", a practice shared with [[Mindfulness]].
- **Writing.** Describing an experience in detail forces finer distinctions than a single word allows.
- **Body first.** For people with weaker interoception, starting from physical sensations, such as tight jaw or hot face, and then guessing the emotion can be easier than introspecting directly.

The goal is not more feelings or better ones, but more precise ones. A well-named feeling is easier to act on, and easier to let pass.
