**Burnout** is a state of exhaustion, detachment, and reduced effectiveness caused by long-term stress at work that hasn't been successfully managed. The psychologist Herbert Freudenberger described it in 1974 among volunteers at a free clinic, and Christina Maslach's research over the following decades turned it into a measurable syndrome. The World Health Organization added it to its disease classification in 2019 as an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition.

Maslach identified three dimensions, measured by the widely used **Maslach Burnout Inventory**:

- **Exhaustion.** Feeling drained, without the energy to face another day; this is the central symptom.
- **Cynicism.** Distancing from the work and the people it serves, becoming callous or indifferent.
- **Reduced efficacy.** Feeling incompetent and that nothing you do makes a difference; see [[Self-Efficacy]].

Burnout differs from ordinary tiredness in that rest doesn't fix it: a weekend or even a vacation brings brief relief, then it returns. It overlaps heavily with depression, and researchers debate how distinct the two really are, though burnout is defined as tied to work while depression spreads across all of life.

Maslach and Michael Leiter found that burnout arises from mismatches between people and their jobs in six areas:

- **Workload.** Too much to do, too little time, and no recovery between demands.
- **Control.** Little say over how the work is done; see [[Locus of Control]].
- **Reward.** Effort that goes unrecognized, financially or otherwise.
- **Community.** Isolation or conflict instead of support; see [[Psychological Safety]].
- **Fairness.** Decisions perceived as unjust or arbitrary.
- **Values.** Being asked to do work that conflicts with one's own ethics.

This framing places most causes in the conditions of work rather than in individual weakness. Wellness programs, meditation apps, and resilience training help some individuals, but on their own they can imply that burnout is a personal failing to be fixed by better coping, while the workload stays unchanged; see [[Fundamental Attribution Error]].

Caring professions are especially affected. Doctors, nurses, teachers, and social workers report high rates, and **compassion fatigue** describes the particular exhaustion of absorbing others' suffering. Software developers report it too, often tied to on-call duties, constant interruptions, and deadlines that keep slipping; see [[Attention Residue]].

Chronic stress also affects the body, through disrupted sleep and the wear of prolonged stress hormones; see [[Allostatic Load]] and [[Sleep Deprivation]]. Recovery usually requires real change: less work, more control, or a different job, plus time.

Autistic people describe a related but distinct state brought on by the effort of masking and sensory strain rather than by work alone; see [[Autistic Burnout]] and [[Spoon Theory]]. Paying attention to early signs, such as dread on Sunday evenings, lost interest, and irritability, allows changes before exhaustion sets in; see [[Emotional Regulation]].
