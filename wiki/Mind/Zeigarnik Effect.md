The **Zeigarnik effect** is the finding that people remember interrupted or unfinished tasks better than completed ones. The story goes that Kurt Lewin noticed a Berlin waiter who remembered every detail of unpaid orders and forgot them the moment the bill was settled. His student Bluma Zeigarnik tested the idea in 1927. Participants interrupted partway through simple tasks recalled them better than tasks they had completed. Lewin's explanation was that an unfinished goal leaves a kind of *tension* that keeps it active until resolved.

The effect is more fragile than its fame. Replications have been mixed, and a 2023 meta-analysis found no reliable general memory advantage for unfinished tasks. Its sibling, the **Ovsiankina effect** (Maria Ovsiankina, 1928), held up better: people strongly tend to *resume* interrupted tasks, even trivial ones, when given the chance. The robust core is motivational rather than mnemonic. Unfinished things pull.

That pull shows up everywhere:

- **Cliffhangers** and serial fiction end mid-action because an open question keeps the audience coming back. Streaming autoplay is the same pull, engineered toward [[Doomscrolling]].
- **Progress bars and streaks** in apps and games show a task as nearly complete, which makes leaving uncomfortable, part of a [[Compulsion Loop]].
- **Intrusive to-dos.** Unfinished obligations surface at odd moments, such as in the shower or at 3 a.m., and contribute to [[Attention Residue]] when switching.

The useful finding for daily life came from E. J. Masicampo and Roy Baumeister in 2011. Intrusive thoughts about an unfinished goal dropped substantially once participants made a specific plan for completing it, even though the task itself was still not done. The mind seems to need not completion but a credible commitment. This is the logic behind productivity systems built on *capturing* every open loop into a trusted list, and behind [[Implementation Intention|implementation intentions]]. A written note of where to resume is enough to close the loop for now.

For people whose [[Working Memory]] is already loaded, offloading open loops is a large share of the work of staying organized. A [[Zettelkasten]] or task inbox gives the tension somewhere to go.
