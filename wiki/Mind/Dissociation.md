**Dissociation** is a disruption in the usual integration of experience: thoughts, feelings, memories, perceptions and the sense of self stop fitting together as they normally do. It ranges from the ordinary to the severe. Arriving home with no memory of the drive, or being so absorbed in a book that someone has to say your name three times, are mild forms. At the other end are conditions in which whole stretches of time go missing or the sense of being one continuous person breaks down.

Two experiences are especially common. **Depersonalization** is feeling detached from oneself, as if watching one's own actions from outside, or as if one's body, voice or emotions belong to someone else. **Derealization** is feeling that the surroundings are unreal, foggy or dreamlike, as if seen through glass. Brief episodes are common during extreme fatigue or panic. When they persist and cause distress, the diagnosis is depersonalization-derealization disorder, estimated to affect around one to two percent of people.

The French psychologist Pierre Janet studied dissociation in the late nineteenth century and proposed that overwhelming experiences could be split off from ordinary awareness. A modern view treats it as a protective response. When fighting or fleeing isn't possible, the nervous system can shift toward shutdown, numbing feeling and distancing awareness from what is happening; see [[Fight-or-Flight Response]]. People describe moving outside a tolerable range of arousal into a flattened, disconnected state; see [[Window of Tolerance]].

Dissociation that happens during a traumatic event predicts later difficulties, and some trauma-related conditions involve recurring episodes. Dissociative amnesia involves gaps in memory for important personal information beyond ordinary forgetting. Dissociative identity disorder, the most severe and most debated form, involves a disrupted sense of identity, and its causes and diagnosis remain contested among clinicians; see [[False Memory]].

Dissociation also illuminates how the sense of self normally works. Feeling that one's body belongs to oneself and that one's actions are one's own are constructed experiences that can be disturbed in the lab; see [[Sense of Agency]] and [[Rubber Hand Illusion]]. Reduced awareness of bodily signals is common in people who dissociate; see [[Interoception]]. Sleep loss, certain anesthetics and some recreational drugs can trigger similar states; see [[Sleep Deprivation]] and [[Sleep Paralysis]].

Not all disconnection is pathological. Deep absorption in a task resembles mild dissociation, though it usually feels engaged rather than numb; see [[Flow]] and [[Mind-Wandering]]. Some autistic people describe dissociation during sensory overload or long periods of masking; see [[Autistic Burnout]].

Treatment focuses on what drives it. Grounding techniques, such as naming things in the room or attending to physical sensations, bring attention back to the present; see [[Mindfulness]]. Therapy addresses the anxiety or trauma underneath and the fearful interpretations that keep episodes going; see [[Cognitive Behavioral Therapy]].
