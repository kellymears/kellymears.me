**Prosopagnosia**, or face blindness, is a difficulty recognizing familiar faces. A person with it may see a face perfectly well, describing the eyes, nose, and expression, but be unable to tell whose face it is. In severe cases people fail to recognize close friends, family, or even themselves in a photograph.

There are two forms:

- **Acquired prosopagnosia** follows brain damage from stroke, injury, or disease, typically to regions on the underside of the temporal lobes.
- **Developmental prosopagnosia** is lifelong, with no brain injury. It is far more common than once thought: studies estimate around 2 percent of people have it to a significant degree, and it often runs in families. Many don't realize they see faces differently from others until adulthood.

Faces seem to get special treatment in the brain. Imaging research in the 1990s, notably by Nancy Kanwisher, identified the **fusiform face area**, a region that responds much more strongly to faces than to other objects. Typical face recognition is holistic: people perceive a face as a whole configuration rather than a list of features. That is why faces turned upside down are disproportionately hard to recognize, the face inversion effect, and why most people can spot a familiar face in a crowd at once.

The brain's eagerness to detect faces is the flip side. [[Pareidolia]], seeing faces in clouds or electrical outlets, shows how readily face-detection fires, and the [[Uncanny Valley]] shows how sensitive people are to faces that are nearly but not quite right. Detecting that something is a face and recognizing whose it is turn out to be separable abilities.

People with prosopagnosia develop workarounds:

- **Other cues.** Voice, gait, hairstyle, glasses, clothing, and distinctive features.
- **Context.** Expecting to meet a colleague at work makes recognition easier, but meeting them in a supermarket fails, an extreme case of [[Context-Dependent Memory]].
- **Strategies.** Waiting for others to speak first, or asking open questions until a clue appears.

The social cost is real. Failing to greet people can read as rudeness or coldness, and some people hide the difficulty with tiring compensation, a form of [[Masking]]. Face blindness appears to be more common among autistic people, and it sits comfortably within the idea of [[Neurodiversity]]: a variation in perception, like [[Aphantasia]] or [[Synesthesia]], that often goes unnoticed because people assume everyone perceives the same way.

At the other end of the range are **super-recognizers**, perhaps 1 to 2 percent of people, who can recognize faces seen once, years earlier. Some police forces have recruited them to identify suspects from footage. Face recognition software built on the [[Convolutional neural network]] now rivals people in some benchmarks, raising its own questions about surveillance.
