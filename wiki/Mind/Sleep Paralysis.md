**Sleep paralysis** is a brief state, usually when falling asleep or waking up, in which a person is conscious but can't move or speak. It often comes with vivid hallucinations: a sense that someone is in the room, a dark figure at the bedside, or a weight pressing on the chest. Episodes last from a few seconds to a couple of minutes and can be terrifying, though they are harmless.

The cause lies in REM sleep, the stage when most vivid dreaming happens. During REM, the brain blocks signals to most muscles, which keeps sleepers from acting out their dreams; see [[Sleep Stages]]. In sleep paralysis, the person wakes into awareness while the paralysis and some dream activity continue. The eyes and breathing still work, but breathing may feel restricted because the chest muscles are relaxed, which may explain the common sense of pressure; see [[Interoception]].

Estimates suggest around 8 percent of people experience it at least once, with higher rates among students and people with anxiety, trauma, or the sleep disorder narcolepsy. Triggers include:

- **Sleep loss and irregular schedules.** Shift work, jet lag, and all-nighters raise the risk; see [[Sleep Deprivation]] and [[Circadian Rhythm]].
- **Sleeping on the back.** Many episodes occur in this position.
- **Interrupted sleep.** Waking in the night and falling back asleep can lead straight into REM; see [[Segmented Sleep]].

The hallucinations follow consistent patterns across cultures, which researchers explain through the brain's threat detection. Lying paralyzed in the dark with a racing heart, the mind searches for a cause and readily perceives an intruder; see [[Predictive Processing]] and [[Pareidolia]].

Cultures have explained the experience in their own terms. In Newfoundland it is called the **Old Hag**, a witch who sits on sleepers' chests. In Japan it is *kanashibari*, being bound by metal. The word "nightmare" originally referred to a *mare*, a spirit that sat on sleepers and smothered them, an image painted by Henry Fuseli in *The Nightmare* (1781). Psychologists have argued that many reports of alien abduction describe sleep paralysis, interpreted through the cultural ideas available to the sleeper.

**Hypnagogia** is the broader set of experiences at sleep's edge: images, voices, falling sensations, and sudden jerks while drifting off. People who played the video game *Tetris* for hours, in a 2000 study, reported seeing falling blocks as they fell asleep, even those with amnesia who couldn't remember playing; see [[Memory Consolidation]] and [[Default Mode Network]].

Treatment usually means better sleep habits. Knowing what the experience is reduces its terror, and some people learn to break an episode by focusing on moving a single finger or toe. Approaches adapted from [[Cognitive Behavioral Therapy]] help frequent sufferers, and reassurance matters, since many people fear they are going mad; see [[Mindfulness]].
