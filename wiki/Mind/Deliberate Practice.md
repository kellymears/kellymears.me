**Deliberate practice** is training designed to improve specific weaknesses, at the edge of current ability, with immediate feedback and full concentration. The psychologist K. Anders Ericsson introduced the term in a 1993 study of violinists at a Berlin music academy, which found that the best students had accumulated more hours of solitary, targeted practice by age twenty than their less accomplished peers.

Ericsson distinguished it from other activities that look like practice:

- **Naive practice** is repeating an activity, like playing pieces you already know or casual games, which builds comfort but plateaus.
- **Purposeful practice** has goals and focus but no expert guidance on what to work on.
- **Deliberate practice** adds a teacher or established training methods that identify what to improve and how, drawn from a field where expert performance is well understood.

Its marks are specific goals, effort at the edge of ability, immediate feedback, and repetition with refinement. It is usually not enjoyable, which is part of why few people do much of it. Musicians practice a hard passage slowly, over and over, rather than playing through; chess players study positions from master games and predict the next move before checking it.

Why it works: experts have built detailed mental representations of their domain, patterns that let them see structure novices miss, and those take years of feedback to form; see [[Expert Intuition]]. Without feedback, repetition just automates what you already do, including your mistakes. Joshua Foer called this the **OK plateau**: once a skill is good enough, it runs automatically and stops improving. Breaking through means bringing it back under conscious control; see [[Motor Learning]].

Malcolm Gladwell's *Outliers* (2008) turned the violin study into the **10,000-hour rule**, the idea that ten thousand hours of practice makes anyone an expert. Ericsson objected: the number was an average, varied widely across fields and individuals, and referred to deliberate practice specifically, not time spent. Later meta-analyses found practice explained a substantial share of performance differences in games and music, but much less in education and professions, leaving room for starting age, aptitude, and other factors.

The idea still offers a useful lesson. Hours alone don't make someone better; what matters is whether practice targets weaknesses with feedback. That applies to writing, programming, and sports as much as to violin, and it explains why experience without feedback sometimes produces confident non-experts; see [[Dunning-Kruger Effect]].

Related findings support it. Spacing and mixing practice feels harder but produces more durable learning; see [[Desirable Difficulty]] and [[Spaced Repetition]]. Believing ability can grow makes people more willing to work at the edge of their skill; see [[Growth Mindset]]. And focused effort is tiring, so even top performers rarely manage more than about four hours a day; see [[Flow]].
