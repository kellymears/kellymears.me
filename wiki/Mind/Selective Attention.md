**Selective attention** is the process of focusing on some of what the senses deliver while suppressing the rest. The senses take in far more than can be processed fully, so the brain is always choosing. In 1953 the cognitive scientist Colin Cherry described the **cocktail party effect**: in a room full of conversations, a listener can follow one and tune the others out. Played different messages in each ear and asked to repeat one, his subjects could report almost nothing about the other, not even when it switched to a different language or was played backward.

Almost nothing, though. In 1959 Neville Moray found that people often noticed their own name in the ignored ear. That raised the question that organized decades of research: where does the filter sit?

- **Early selection.** Donald Broadbent's 1958 filter model proposed that unattended input is blocked before its meaning is processed.
- **Attenuation.** Anne Treisman proposed that unattended input is turned down rather than blocked, so highly relevant signals such as your name still get through.
- **Late selection.** Other models argued that everything is processed for meaning and selection happens only at the point of response or memory.
- **Load theory.** Nilli Lavie's resolution, from the 1990s, is that it depends. When a task fills perceptual capacity, distractors aren't processed. When the task is easy, spare capacity spills over onto them, which is why simple tasks are often harder to stay focused on.

Attention is steered from two directions. *Top-down* control follows goals, and *bottom-up* capture is pulled by salience: motion, sudden sound, bright color, faces. Bottom-up capture is hard to resist, which notifications, autoplay, and [[Dark Pattern]] designs exploit, and why [[Motion Design]] needs restraint and [[Reduced Motion]] settings exist. Design can also cooperate with it. [[Gestalt Principles]] group related elements so the eye finds structure without effort.

When the filter is narrow, the rest of the scene can vanish, as [[Inattentional Blindness]] shows. When it is leaky, everything competes. Many autistic and ADHD people describe weaker filtering of background input, contributing to [[Sensory Overload]], alongside an ability to lock onto a single interest, as [[Monotropism]] describes and [[Hyperfocus]] shows. Holding a focus also draws on [[Working Memory]] and [[Executive Function]], and switching it leaves [[Attention Residue]].
