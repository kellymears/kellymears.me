**Transactive memory** is a group's shared system for remembering, in which members know who holds which knowledge and rely on each other instead of storing everything themselves. The psychologist Daniel Wegner proposed it in 1985, starting from couples: one partner remembers birthdays and the other knows how the car works, and each knows to ask the other.

A transactive memory system has three parts:

- **Specialization.** Different members hold different knowledge.
- **Credibility.** Members trust each other's knowledge enough to rely on it without checking.
- **Coordination.** The group knows how to route questions and combine what members know.

The crucial piece is the directory: knowing *who* knows, even without knowing *what* they know. Wegner's experiments showed that couples given lists of items to remember naturally split them by expertise, and recalled more together than pairs of strangers did. When experimenters assigned couples topics against their usual division, their performance dropped below that of strangers.

Teams show the same pattern. Groups trained together on a task, who learned each other's strengths, outperformed groups whose members were trained separately, largely because they knew whom to ask. Research on work teams links strong transactive memory to better coordination and fewer duplicated efforts. Members also need to feel safe admitting what they don't know, or the directory fills with bluffs; see [[Psychological Safety]].

The systems have characteristic weaknesses:

- **Loss.** When a member leaves, the knowledge leaves with them, and often nobody notices until a question has no one to answer it. Widowed partners describe losing part of their memory, not just a companion. Software teams track this as the risk that knowledge is held by too few people; see [[Bus Factor]].
- **Stale directories.** People change roles and forget, but others keep routing questions to them.
- **Uneven load.** A recognized expert receives every question, which interrupts their own work; see [[Attention Residue]].
- **False confidence.** Knowing that someone knows can feel like knowing it yourself; see [[Curse of Knowledge]].

Wegner's idea extended naturally to technology. People who expect information to be saved on a computer remember it less well but remember where it is stored, treating the computer as a member of their memory system; see [[Extended Mind]]. Search engines, wikis, and chat histories now serve as the shared directory for many groups, and documentation is partly an attempt to make transactive memory survive turnover; see [[Documentation Rot]].

Organizations try to strengthen it deliberately, through pairing, rotations, and explicit maps of who owns what, so that expertise is both findable and shared enough to survive any one person leaving. The aim is not for everyone to know everything, which defeats the point, but for the directory to be accurate and the important knowledge held by more than one person.
