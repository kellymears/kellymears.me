**Demand avoidance** is resistance to doing what is asked or expected. Everyone has some: few people enjoy being told what to do. In its extreme form, often called **pathological demand avoidance (PDA)**, the resistance is intense, pervasive, and driven by anxiety, and it extends to demands the person would otherwise want to meet, including their own plans and even pleasant things like a favorite meal at a set time. Being asked can make a wanted task impossible.

The British psychologist Elizabeth Newson described PDA in the 1980s in children who resembled autistic children in some ways but not others. It is now usually discussed as a profile within autism, though it is not a formal diagnosis in DSM-5 or ICD-11, and researchers disagree about whether it is a distinct condition, a set of traits that shows up across several diagnoses, or an anxiety response that could follow from many causes. Many people with the profile prefer the reframing **persistent drive for autonomy**, which describes the need rather than the pathology.

The demands that trigger avoidance can be obvious or very subtle:

- **Direct requests** and instructions.
- **Implied demands**: expectations, routines, praise that creates pressure to repeat something, a question that requires an answer.
- **Internal demands**: the person's own intention, which can turn into a demand the moment it is formed.

Avoidance often looks strategic, such as distraction, negotiation, excuses, or delays, and when pressure keeps rising, it can become panic or meltdown. It is a threat response more than defiance, and it sits close to [[Rejection Sensitivity]] in that the reaction is out of proportion to the situation and driven by anxiety rather than intent.

The supportive approach is the opposite of firm boundaries and consequences, which usually escalate it. Reducing demands, offering choices, phrasing requests indirectly or as collaboration, and building trust lower the anxiety that drives avoidance. It shares a mechanism with [[Autistic Inertia]] in that wanting and doing come apart, and with [[Procrastination]], which can be understood as avoiding the feelings attached to a task rather than the task itself. [[Learned Helplessness]] is a different failure mode, a loss of attempts rather than resistance, though years of escalating conflict over demands can lead there too.
