**The rubber hand illusion** is an experiment that makes people feel a fake hand as their own. A participant's real hand rests out of sight behind a screen, while a realistic rubber hand lies in view where the real one might plausibly be. An experimenter strokes both hands with brushes at the same moments. Within a minute or two, many people report feeling the touch on the rubber hand, as if it had become part of their body.

The researchers Matthew Botvinick and Jonathan Cohen described the illusion in a short paper in Nature in 1998. Later research found signs that the brain treats the fake hand as real, beyond what people report:

- **Drift.** Asked to point to their hidden hand, participants point toward the rubber one; see [[Proprioception]].
- **Threat responses.** When the rubber hand is suddenly bent backward or threatened with a needle, many participants flinch and show a spike in skin conductance, a sign of stress.
- **Timing dependence.** If the strokes are out of sync, the illusion weakens or disappears.

The illusion shows that body ownership, the feeling that this body is mine, isn't fixed. The brain constructs it continually by combining vision, touch, and the sense of body position, and when the senses agree on a fake hand, vision can win; see [[Predictive Processing]] and [[Bayesian Inference]]. The same weighting of sight over other senses appears in hearing, where seeing lips can change the sound a person perceives; see [[McGurk Effect]].

Later experiments extended the effect. In the 2000s, researchers including Henrik Ehrsson used head-mounted cameras and displays to create full-body illusions, making people feel located in a mannequin's body or behind their own; see [[Virtual Reality]]. Virtual reality designers use the same principles to make avatars feel embodied, and synchrony between movement and display is critical; see [[Input Latency]].

The illusion connects to clinical phenomena. Amputees often feel a missing limb, and seeing a reflection of the remaining limb in a mirror can sometimes ease pain in the missing one; see [[Phantom Limb]]. Some brain injuries produce the opposite problem, where people fail to recognize a limb as their own or ignore one side of space; see [[Hemispatial Neglect]]. Embodiment research has also informed prosthetics, where sensory feedback may help an artificial limb feel like one's own.

Owning a body is related to but different from feeling in control of it. Moving a hand and seeing it move as expected builds a sense of agency, and agency and ownership can come apart; see [[Sense of Agency]]. People also differ in how strongly they feel the illusion, which some researchers link to differences in sensing the body's internal state; see [[Interoception]]. The body the mind experiences isn't simply given; see [[Embodied Cognition]].
