**Perfectionism** is the tendency to set extremely high standards and to treat falling short of them as failure, often as a judgment on one's worth. It is easy to mistake for conscientiousness or high ambition, but the research suggests what matters is less the height of the bar than the relationship to it.

Psychologists Paul Hewitt and Gordon Flett proposed in 1991 that perfectionism has three directions:

- **Self-oriented** perfectionism demands flawlessness from oneself.
- **Other-oriented** perfectionism demands it from other people, which strains teams and relationships.
- **Socially prescribed** perfectionism is the belief that others demand it of you and will reject you for falling short.

Later work split the trait another way. **Perfectionistic strivings**, pursuing high standards, are sometimes linked to better performance and motivation. **Perfectionistic concerns**, the fear of mistakes, the harsh self-criticism and the doubt that anything is ever good enough, are consistently linked to anxiety, depression, eating disorders and [[Burnout]]. The socially prescribed form shows the strongest ties to distress.

A widely cited 2019 meta-analysis by Thomas Curran and Andrew Hill found that all three Hewitt and Flett dimensions had risen among college students in the United States, Canada and Britain between 1989 and 2016, with socially prescribed perfectionism rising the most. The authors pointed to more competitive schooling and constant [[Social Comparison]], though causes are hard to pin down.

The paradox is that perfectionism often lowers output. If anything less than excellent counts as failure, starting is risky, so tasks get postponed; see [[Procrastination]]. Some people protect themselves in advance by not trying fully, so a poor result can be blamed on lack of effort; see [[Self-Handicapping]]. Success brings little relief, because it is redefined as the minimum, and high achievers who cannot credit their own work often describe [[Impostor Syndrome]]. Rigid standards also invite all-or-nothing thinking, in which a single error ruins the whole.

Treatment tends to target the concerns rather than the standards. [[Cognitive Behavioral Therapy]] programs for perfectionism use behavioral experiments, such as deliberately submitting work with a small flaw and observing that nothing terrible happens, a form of [[Exposure Therapy]]. [[Self-Compassion]] is consistently associated with lower perfectionistic concerns, and a [[Growth Mindset]] reframes mistakes as information. The decision-making counterpart is [[Satisficing]], choosing the option that is good enough instead of hunting for the best.

Researchers sometimes use the term **excellencism** for pursuing high standards without the demand for flawlessness, and early studies suggest it captures most of the benefit with fewer of the costs. Being good at something, in other words, does not require believing that anything less than perfect is worthless; see [[Deliberate Practice]].
