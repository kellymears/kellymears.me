**The Barnum effect** is the tendency to accept vague, general personality descriptions as specifically and accurately describing oneself. Statements like "you have a great need for other people to like you," "at times you are extroverted and sociable, while at other times you are introverted and reserved," or "you have a great deal of unused capacity" feel personal, but they apply to almost anyone.

The psychologist Bertram Forer demonstrated it in a classroom experiment published in 1949. Forer gave students a personality test, then handed each of them what seemed to be an individual assessment. All received the same text, assembled largely from a newsstand astrology book. Asked to rate how well it described them on a scale from 0 to 5, the students gave an average of about 4.3. The psychologist Paul Meehl later named the effect after the showman P. T. Barnum, whose entertainments were said to offer a little something for everyone.

Statements that work this way share features:

- **Vagueness.** They can be interpreted to fit many situations.
- **Two-sidedness.** They describe both a trait and its opposite, so one half always seems to apply.
- **Flattery.** People accept positive descriptions more readily than negative ones; see [[Self-Serving Bias]].
- **Apparent personalization.** People rate a description as more accurate when they believe it was prepared for them specifically, or based on their own birth date or answers.

The effect underlies much of the appeal of horoscopes, fortune telling, and palm reading; see [[Tarot]]. Practitioners of **cold reading** combine Barnum statements with close observation and feedback: they make broad guesses, watch reactions, and narrow toward what lands, while clients remember the hits and forget the misses; see [[Confirmation Bias]]. Mentalists and con artists use the same techniques; see [[Confidence Trick]].

Some personality tests used in workplaces have been criticized along similar lines, since descriptions of types can be general and flattering enough that most people feel recognized. Validated psychological measures are checked against independent evidence of whether they predict anything, rather than whether people find them accurate. A description that can't be wrong about anyone also tells nobody anything; see [[Falsifiability]].

The effect reflects broader tendencies. People find meaning in ambiguous material, whether faces in clouds or patterns in personal descriptions; see [[Pareidolia]]. They prefer statements that feel easy and familiar; see [[Processing Fluency]]. And believing a reading was personal can make it feel helpful, much as believing in a treatment can produce real relief; see [[Placebo Effect]].

Recommendation systems and personalized feeds can trigger something similar. A suggestion presented as "picked for you" can feel insightful even when it's popular with nearly everyone; see [[Recommender System]].

A useful defense: before accepting that a description fits, ask whether it would fit most other people too.
