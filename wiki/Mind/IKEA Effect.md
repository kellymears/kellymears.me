**The IKEA effect** is the tendency to value things more highly when we've put effort into making them. A wobbly bookshelf assembled from a flat-pack box feels worth more than an identical one delivered built. The psychologists Michael Norton, Daniel Mochon, and Dan Ariely named it in a 2012 paper, after the Swedish furniture company whose customers assemble their own purchases.

In their experiments, people who assembled IKEA storage boxes were willing to pay more for them than people who were handed the same boxes already assembled. Participants who folded origami cranes and frogs valued their own amateur creations nearly as highly as models made by experts, while other people saw the amateur versions as near worthless. The effect depended on finishing: when people built something and then took it apart, or failed to complete it, the extra value largely disappeared.

A popular story connects it to cake mixes. Instant mixes in the 1950s supposedly sold better once manufacturers made home bakers add a fresh egg, giving them a sense of having really baked. The story is widely repeated, but the evidence for it is thin, and other explanations, such as better flavor from fresh eggs, are just as plausible.

Several mechanisms probably contribute:

- **Competence.** Successfully making something signals skill and satisfies the need to feel capable; see [[Self-Determination Theory]] and [[Self-Efficacy]].
- **Justifying effort.** Having worked hard for something, people resolve the tension by deciding it was worth it; see [[Effort justification]].
- **Ownership.** Things we own, and especially things we've shaped, take on part of our identity; see [[Endowment effect]].

The bias appears wherever people make their own tools. Programmers often prefer code they wrote to better libraries written elsewhere, and organizations resist adopting outside solutions; see [[Not Invented Here]]. A team that built a system may defend it long after it should be replaced, a pattern that overlaps with refusing to abandon past investment; see [[Sunk Cost Fallacy]] and [[Big Rewrite]]. Hobbyists who customize their computer desktops in detail grow attached to setups no one else would choose; see [[Ricing]].

The effect has constructive uses. Products that let customers configure or assemble something can earn more attachment than polished finished goods. Involving people in designing a process makes them more committed to it. Teams that build an internal tool and use it daily care more about its quality; see [[Dogfooding]].

It can also mislead. People overestimate how much others will value what they made, which skews pricing, hiring decisions, and judgments of a project's success; see [[Naive Realism]]. The same pull toward familiar things appears even without effort, simply through repeated exposure; see [[Mere-Exposure Effect]]. When judging one's own work, it helps to remember that effort and quality aren't the same; see [[Motivated Reasoning]].
