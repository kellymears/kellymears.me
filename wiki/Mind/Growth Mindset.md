A **growth mindset** is the belief that abilities like intelligence or skill can be developed through effort, good strategies, and help from others. A **fixed mindset** is the belief that they're innate and largely unchangeable. The psychologist Carol Dweck developed the idea from research on how students respond to failure, and popularized it in her 2006 book *Mindset*.

Dweck's argument is that these beliefs shape behavior. If ability is fixed, every challenge is a test of worth: failure proves a lack of talent, effort suggests a lack of ability, and the safe choice is to avoid hard tasks. If ability can grow, challenges are chances to learn, effort is how to improve, and mistakes carry useful information. The belief changes what struggle means.

A widely cited 1998 study by Claudia Mueller and Dweck praised children either for being smart or for working hard. Children praised for intelligence later chose easier problems, enjoyed the work less after failing, and some exaggerated their scores. Children praised for effort chose harder problems and persisted longer. The practical advice that followed, praise process rather than talent, spread through schools.

The evidence has since been tested at scale:

- **Small average effects.** Meta-analyses published in 2018 found that mindset beliefs were only weakly related to academic achievement, and that mindset interventions had small effects overall; see [[Effect Size]].
- **Targeted benefits.** A large national study in the United States, led by David Yeager and published in 2019, found that a short online intervention modestly raised grades among lower-achieving students, especially in schools whose culture supported it.
- **Replication questions.** Some well-known findings, including aspects of the praise studies, have replicated weakly or not at all; see [[Replication Crisis]].

Dweck herself warned against a **false growth mindset**: praising effort alone, even when it isn't working, or treating the idea as a slogan. Effort without changing strategy doesn't produce learning; neither does telling struggling people to try harder. The useful version pairs effort with feedback and new approaches; see [[Desirable Difficulty]].

The concept overlaps with several others. Believing one can succeed at a specific task is [[Self-Efficacy]]; believing outcomes depend on one's own actions is an internal [[Locus of Control]]. Its opposite resembles [[Learned Helplessness]]. Fear that success will reveal one as a fraud often comes with fixed beliefs about ability; see [[Impostor Syndrome]]. And the brain's real capacity to change provides the biological backdrop; see [[Neuroplasticity]].

Teams show a similar dynamic. When mistakes are treated as evidence of incompetence, people hide them; when treated as learning, they surface early. Blameless reviews of incidents apply a growth mindset to organizations; see [[Blameless Postmortem]].
