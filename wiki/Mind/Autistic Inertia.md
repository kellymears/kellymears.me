**Autistic inertia** is the term autistic people use for a pronounced difficulty with transitions: starting an activity, stopping one, or switching between them. The name borrows from physics. An object at rest tends to stay at rest, and an object in motion tends to stay in motion. Someone may sit for an hour fully intending to get up and cook, unable to begin. Later, once absorbed in a task, they may be unable to stop to eat or sleep. Both are the same difficulty seen from different sides.

It is not laziness or lack of desire, which is the misreading it attracts most. People describe wanting very much to do the thing and still being stuck, often with growing distress. Research on it is recent. A 2021 study by Buckle and colleagues collected autistic adults' descriptions and found inertia affected daily life widely, from self-care to work, and was poorly understood by clinicians.

Several overlapping explanations fit:

- **Executive function.** Initiating and shifting are core [[Executive Function]] skills, and both are often affected in autism and ADHD.
- **Monotropism.** [[Monotropism]] holds that autistic attention tends to gather into a few deep channels. Moving attention out of one channel and into another is costly, much as [[Attention Residue]] describes but stronger.
- **Load and depletion.** Inertia worsens with stress, sensory demands, and [[Autistic Burnout]], when every transition costs more.
- **Uncertainty.** A task with unclear first steps is harder to start than one with a known sequence.

The stopping side links to [[Hyperfocus]] and [[Flow]]. The same channel that is hard to enter is hard to leave, and being interrupted mid-task can be genuinely painful rather than mildly annoying.

What helps works with the inertia rather than against it. External cues such as alarms, a visual schedule, or a timer, warnings before a transition, and keeping the first step small and concrete all lower the cost of starting. [[Body Doubling]] gives momentum through another person's presence. [[Implementation Intention]] plans pre-decide the trigger. Building transitions into routines turns them into [[Habit Formation]], so they no longer have to be willed each time. Others can help most by giving notice before interruptions and accepting that "in a minute" may be an honest estimate of what switching costs.
