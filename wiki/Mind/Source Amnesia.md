**Source amnesia** is remembering information while forgetting where it came from. Someone recalls that a certain food causes headaches but not whether they read it in a medical journal, heard it from a friend, or saw it in an advertisement. The fact survives, and the label that would let them judge it is gone.

Memory researchers, notably Marcia Johnson and colleagues in the 1980s and 1990s, describe the underlying process as **source monitoring**. Memories don't arrive tagged with their origin. The brain infers the source from features of the memory itself, such as its vividness, sensory detail, and context. When those cues are weak or misleading, it attributes memories to the wrong source: a dream remembered as an event, something imagined remembered as something seen, or one person's remark remembered as another's; see [[Episodic Memory]] and [[False Memory]].

Several familiar effects follow:

- **The sleeper effect.** In studies of wartime propaganda films in the 1940s, Carl Hovland and colleagues found that a message from a low-credibility source can become more persuasive over time. People remember the message after forgetting that they had reason to doubt it. Later research found the effect real but inconsistent.
- **Misinformation that outlasts corrections.** A false claim and its correction are both remembered, but the link between them fades, and the claim can come to feel simply true; see [[Illusory Truth Effect]] and [[Brandolini's Law]].
- **Cryptomnesia.** People mistake a remembered idea for an original one, producing inadvertent plagiarism. In 1976, a US court found that George Harrison's song "My Sweet Lord" had subconsciously copied the 1960s hit "He's So Fine," a case often cited as an example.
- **Eyewitness errors.** Witnesses may identify a face they saw in a mug shot or elsewhere, not at the crime; see [[Rashomon Effect]].

Familiarity plays a central role. A claim that feels familiar feels true and seems to come from somewhere credible, even when it doesn't; see [[Processing Fluency]] and [[Mere-Exposure Effect]]. The feeling of familiarity without its origin is also part of what makes déjà vu disorienting; see [[Déjà Vu]].

Source memory is fragile in particular groups. Young children and older adults tend to have more difficulty remembering sources than recalling content, and certain kinds of damage to the frontal lobes make source errors common, sometimes producing confident false accounts; see [[Confabulation]].

Practical defenses rely on recording sources rather than trusting memory to keep them. Researchers cite as they write, note-taking systems record where each idea came from, and wikis require references; see [[Zettelkasten]] and [[Wiki]]. Rumors and urban legends spread partly because each retelling sheds the original source until only "I heard somewhere" remains; see [[Urban Legend]]. Knowing that memory loses provenance is a reason to ask where a belief came from; see [[Metacognition]].
