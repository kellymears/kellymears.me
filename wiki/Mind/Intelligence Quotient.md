An **intelligence quotient**, or IQ, is a score from a standardized test of reasoning, knowledge, memory and problem solving. Modern tests are scaled so that the population average is 100 and about two-thirds of people score between 85 and 115; see [[Normal Distribution]]. The score describes where someone falls relative to others of the same age, not an absolute amount of anything.

The first practical test came from Alfred Binet and Théodore Simon in France in 1905, designed to identify children who needed extra help in school. The German psychologist William Stern proposed dividing a child's "mental age" by actual age, giving a quotient. Lewis Terman's American revision, the Stanford-Binet, appeared in 1916, and adult tests followed. Today's scores use the age-normed scale instead of the original ratio.

A striking regularity underlies the tests. In 1904, Charles Spearman noticed that people who do well on one kind of mental test tend to do well on others, from vocabulary to pattern puzzles. Spearman proposed a general factor, **g**, behind that overlap. Later theorists split abilities further. Raymond Cattell distinguished **fluid intelligence**, reasoning about new problems, which peaks in early adulthood, from **crystallized intelligence**, accumulated knowledge, which tends to hold or grow into later life. Working memory and executive control correlate strongly with fluid ability; see [[Working Memory]] and [[Executive Function]].

IQ predicts real outcomes moderately well. It correlates with school grades, years of education, and job performance, more strongly in complex jobs. It also misses a great deal. Expertise in a field depends heavily on knowledge and practice; see [[Deliberate Practice]] and [[Expert Intuition]]. A single score can hide uneven profiles, such as strong reasoning alongside a specific reading difficulty; see [[Dyslexia]] and [[Neurodiversity]].

Scores also change over time. James Flynn documented that average raw scores rose by roughly three points per decade across much of the twentieth century, too fast to be genetic, and probably driven by schooling, nutrition and more abstract everyday thinking. This **Flynn effect** has stalled or reversed in some countries since the 1990s. Test conditions matter too, as anxiety about confirming a stereotype can lower performance; see [[Stereotype Threat]].

The history is troubled. Early American testers used IQ to argue for restricting immigration and for forced sterilization, which the Supreme Court upheld in 1927. Debates over group differences remain heated, and most researchers agree that test scores reflect environment as well as heredity.

Turning a broad idea like intelligence into a test score is an act of operationalization, with the risk of treating the measure as the thing itself; see [[Operationalization]] and [[Goodhart's Law]]. Beliefs about whether ability is fixed or can grow affect how people respond to setbacks; see [[Growth Mindset]]. Early advantages in schooling compound, widening gaps over time; see [[Matthew Effect]].
