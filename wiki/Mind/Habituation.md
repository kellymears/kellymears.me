**Habituation** is the gradual decline in response to a stimulus that repeats without consequence. A new refrigerator hum is noticed, then ignored within days. A dog stops startling at the doorbell of a busy house. It is the simplest form of learning, found in animals without brains and, by some accounts, in single-celled organisms.

Habituation is not tiredness or a dulled sense. The test is *dishabituation*: after a response has faded, a new or stronger stimulus brings it back, and sometimes the old one regains its effect too. The ears still register the hum; the brain has learned it predicts nothing. Richard Thompson and William Spencer set out the defining features in 1966, including that it recovers with time and proceeds faster with frequent repetition.

Eric Kandel worked out the cellular basis in the sea slug *Aplysia*, whose gill-withdrawal reflex weakens when its siphon is touched again and again. The connection between sensory and motor neurons releases less transmitter with each touch, a small instance of [[Neuroplasticity]]. Kandel shared the 2000 Nobel Prize partly for this work. Its counterpart is **sensitization**, in which a strong or harmful stimulus makes responses grow, as in [[Central Sensitization]] of pain.

Why it matters:

- **Attention budget.** The first exposure to anything triggers an *orienting response*, a turn of the head and a surge of alertness. Habituation lets that fade so [[Selective Attention]] can go to what is new. Under [[Predictive Processing]], it is a prediction that stopped producing error.
- **When it lags.** Some autistic people and people with sensory processing differences habituate more slowly, so the hum stays loud all day, a route to [[Sensory Overload]].
- **Alarm fatigue.** Hospital monitors that alarm constantly, mostly for nothing, teach staff to tune them out, and real alarms then go unanswered. Software alerting suffers the same way, where noisy pages slow [[Incident Response]].
- **Design.** Banners, badges, and notifications all lose force with repetition. The [[Von Restorff Effect]] works only while the distinctive item stays rare.

Habituation explains some broader patterns too. The [[Hedonic Treadmill]] is habituation to good fortune: a raise or a new car stops registering. [[Semantic Satiation]], a word losing meaning after many repetitions, is a quick laboratory version. And [[Boredom]] partly arises when habituation drains the novelty from a task.

Infant researchers rely on it. Babies cannot answer questions, but they look longer at something new, so showing an image until looking drops and then changing one feature reveals what differences they can perceive. The same method works with animals.
