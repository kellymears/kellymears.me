**Dreaming** is the experience of images, events, and feelings during sleep, often in a story that seems real while it lasts. Nearly everyone dreams, even people who rarely remember it. Why the brain does it is still one of the open questions of psychology.

Dreams happen throughout the night but are most vivid, strange, and story-like during REM sleep, the stage in which the eyes move rapidly and the body's muscles are paralyzed, which keeps sleepers from acting dreams out; see [[Sleep Stages]]. When that paralysis lingers on waking, the result is the frightening state of [[Sleep Paralysis]]. REM periods lengthen toward morning, which is why the dreams people remember usually come just before waking.

The brain in REM looks partly awake. Visual and emotional areas are active, while regions behind the forehead that handle logic, self-monitoring, and time are quieter. That combination may explain dreams' character: intense emotion and imagery, with impossible events accepted without question.

The main theories:

- **Memory processing.** Sleep strengthens and reorganizes memories, and dreams often mix fragments of recent experience with older ones, suggesting dreaming reflects that work; see [[Memory Consolidation]].
- **Emotional regulation.** Dreams may replay emotional experiences in a safer setting, softening their sting. People deprived of REM sleep react more strongly to upsetting images; see [[Emotional Regulation]].
- **Threat simulation.** Antti Revonsuo proposed that dreams evolved to rehearse responses to danger, which would explain why chases and attacks are so common.
- **Activation-synthesis.** J. Allan Hobson and Robert McCarley argued in 1977 that dreams are the brain's attempt to make a story out of random activity from the brainstem, a side effect with no purpose.
- **Prediction.** Recent views treat dreams as the brain's model of the world running without sensory input, testing and refining its expectations; see [[Predictive Processing]].

Sigmund Freud's *The Interpretation of Dreams* (1899) treated dreams as disguised wishes needing decoding. Few researchers accept that now, though dreams often reflect waking worries.

**Lucid dreaming** is knowing you're dreaming while it happens, sometimes with the ability to steer the dream. Its reality was established in the late 1970s and 1980s, when Stephen LaBerge and others had lucid dreamers signal with agreed eye movements, the one movement REM paralysis leaves free. Techniques such as reality checks and waking briefly before returning to sleep make it more likely.

**Nightmares** are dreams intense enough to wake the sleeper with fear. Frequent ones are common after trauma, and a therapy called imagery rehearsal, in which the person rewrites a recurring nightmare while awake and rehearses the new version, reduces them substantially; see [[Cognitive Behavioral Therapy]].

Daydreams share some features with night dreams, drawing on the same brain networks that run when attention turns inward; see [[Default Mode Network]] and [[Mind-Wandering]].
