An **earworm** is a short fragment of music that repeats in the mind without being invited, often for minutes or hours. The word is a translation of the German *Ohrwurm*. Researchers call it **involuntary musical imagery**. Surveys find that over 90% of people experience one at least weekly, and most find them neutral or pleasant rather than annoying.

The fragment is usually short, often a chorus or hook of a few seconds, looping rather than playing through. Common triggers:

- **Recent and repeated exposure.** A song heard that morning or many times lately; see [[Mere-Exposure Effect]].
- **Memory cues.** A word, a place, or a person associated with a song.
- **Idle attention.** Earworms arise most during low-demand activities such as walking, showering, or chores, when the mind wanders; see [[Mind-Wandering]] and [[Default Mode Network]].
- **Stress and fatigue**, which loosen control over thoughts.

Some songs are stickier. A 2016 study by Kelly Jakubowski and colleagues analyzed songs people reported as earworms and found they tended to be faster, with a common overall melodic shape but some unusual intervals or repetitions. Lady Gaga's "Bad Romance" topped the list.

Earworms are part of a broader capacity for imagining sound. Most people can "hear" music in their heads, along with their own inner voice; see [[Inner Speech]]. People with little or no voluntary imagery report fewer earworms; see [[Aphantasia]]. Some musicians experience them as a creative resource.

Why they loop is less clear. One theory links them to unfinished tasks: a fragment without its ending keeps nagging, much as interrupted work stays active in memory; see [[Zeigarnik Effect]]. They also share features with intrusive thoughts and repetitive worry, and for a small number of people with obsessive-compulsive disorder, they can be distressing; see [[Rumination]].

What helps:

- **Finish the song.** Listening to it all the way through sometimes resolves the loop.
- **Give working memory a job.** Moderately demanding tasks, such as anagrams, a puzzle, or a good book, crowd out the music. Too easy a task leaves room for it; too hard a task lets the mind drift back; see [[Working Memory]].
- **Chew gum.** A 2015 study found that chewing gum reduced earworms, perhaps by occupying the muscles used to subvocalize.
- **Don't fight it.** Trying hard to suppress a song tends to bring it back; see [[Ironic Process Theory]].

Mostly earworms fade on their own as the brain adjusts to the loop; see [[Habituation]].

They interact with how songs are heard in the first place. Misheard lyrics often get stuck in their misheard form; see [[Mondegreen]]. Games build musical motifs that return in new arrangements, which can make soundtracks especially sticky; see [[Adaptive Music]]. Repeated phrases also lose their sense when looped, as with words repeated aloud; see [[Semantic Satiation]].
