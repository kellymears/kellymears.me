The **peak-end rule** describes how people remember experiences. Rather than averaging how they felt across the whole thing, they judge it mostly by two moments: the most intense point, positive or negative, and the end. How long the experience lasted barely matters, a pattern called *duration neglect*. Daniel Kahneman and colleagues developed the idea in the early 1990s.

The best-known evidence came from a 1993 experiment in which participants held a hand in painfully cold water. In one trial, they kept it in 14°C water for 60 seconds. In another, they did the same 60 seconds and then kept it in for 30 more seconds while the water was warmed slightly, still uncomfortable but less so. Asked which trial to repeat, most chose the longer one, even though it contained strictly more pain. Its ending was milder, so it was remembered as better.

A study of colonoscopy patients, published by Donald Redelmeier and Kahneman in 1996, found the same thing. Patients' ratings of the procedure tracked the worst moment and the last few minutes, not the total duration.

Kahneman described the split as two selves. The *experiencing self* lives through each moment. The *remembering self* keeps score afterward and makes decisions about the future. Because we choose based on memories, the remembering self tends to win, even when its story misrepresents what actually happened.

The rule has practical uses:

- **Endings deserve design.** A service that ends well, a smooth checkout, a kind goodbye, a final dessert, is remembered better than one that was excellent until a frustrating last step. Software onboarding, support calls, and holidays all benefit from attention to the last moment.
- **Peaks matter more than consistency.** A few memorable highs can outweigh many neutral moments, an idea games use with boss fights and finales shaped by the [[Difficulty Curve]].
- **Fixing the worst moment.** Reducing the peak of pain does more for memory than shortening the whole.

It also explains some distortions. Vacations are remembered by their best day and their last, not their average. Memory of waiting is shaped by how the wait ended, so a slow page that resolves with a satisfying result feels better than a fast one that ends in an error, a point that complements [[Response Time Limits]].

The rule interacts with other memory effects. The [[Serial Position Effect]] also gives endings extra weight in lists. Distinctive peaks are remembered for the same reason as the [[Von Restorff Effect]]. And the gap between experience and memory relates to the [[Hedonic Treadmill]], since people adapt quickly to steady states while remembering changes. Reconstructed memories of experiences can drift further over time, as described under [[False Memory]].
