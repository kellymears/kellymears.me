The **Stroop effect** is the delay people show when naming the ink color of a word that spells a different color. Shown the word RED printed in blue ink, people are slower and make more errors saying "blue" than when naming the color of a row of X's or of the word BLUE printed in blue. The psychologist J. Ridley Stroop published the effect in 1935, and it has become one of the most reliably reproduced findings in psychology.

The effect arises because reading is **automatic** for fluent readers. Seeing a word triggers its meaning whether or not the reader wants it, while naming an ink color takes deliberate effort. When the two conflict, the brain has to suppress the faster, habitual response to give the slower, intended one; see [[Dual Process Theory]] and [[Habit Formation]]. Children who are just learning to read show a weaker effect, because reading hasn't yet become automatic.

The task became a standard tool for studying attention and self-control:

- **Clinical assessment.** Stroop tests measure inhibition, one of the core [[Executive Function]]s, and are used in assessing brain injury, dementia, and [[ADHD]].
- **Selective attention.** The effect shows the limits of ignoring irrelevant information even when told to; see [[Selective Attention]] and [[Cognitive Load]].
- **Emotional Stroop.** People with anxiety disorders are slower to name the color of words related to their fears, suggesting that threatening words grab their attention; see [[Negativity Bias]].
- **Synesthesia.** People who see letters or numbers in color are slowed when a letter is printed in a color that clashes with the one they experience, which helps researchers verify that the experience is genuine; see [[Synesthesia]].

The Stroop task was also central to research on **ego depletion**, the idea that self-control draws on a limited resource that can run out. Many studies found that people did worse on a Stroop test after an earlier task requiring willpower. Large preregistered replications in the 2010s found the effect close to zero, though the Stroop effect itself remains as solid as ever; see [[Decision Fatigue]] and [[Replication Crisis]].

Brain imaging links the task to the anterior cingulate cortex, which seems to detect conflict between competing responses, and the prefrontal cortex, which resolves it. Practice reduces the effect, and so does anything that makes the word harder to read, such as unusual fonts; see [[Processing Fluency]].

Interface designers meet the same conflict. A red button labeled "Go," or a green icon that means "stop," forces users to override one signal with another, slowing them down and inviting mistakes; see [[Preattentive Processing]] and [[Signifier]]. Consistent mappings of color to meaning reduce errors, especially for users under pressure.
