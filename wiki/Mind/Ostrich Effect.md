The **ostrich effect** is the tendency to avoid information one expects to be bad. People check their bank balance less when they suspect it's low, put off opening a letter from the tax office, and skip medical checkups when worried about the results. The name comes from the old belief that ostriches bury their heads in the sand when threatened. They don't, but the image stuck.

The term was introduced in a 2006 study of financial markets by the economists Dan Galai and Orly Sade. A few years later, the economists Niklas Karlsson, George Loewenstein, and Duane Seppi showed that investors in Sweden and the United States logged into their accounts less often after market declines than after rises. Knowing the bad news would hurt, so they postponed learning it. When markets rose, they checked more often, savoring the good news.

The pattern appears wherever information carries emotional weight:

- **Health.** People at risk of serious genetic diseases often decline testing even when it's available, and people delay seeing a doctor about worrying symptoms.
- **Money.** Debts, credit scores, and retirement savings go unexamined.
- **Relationships and work.** People avoid feedback, performance reviews, and conversations they expect to go badly.

The effect follows from how people value information. Information is worth seeking when it might change a decision, but it also changes how people feel, and the expected pain can outweigh its usefulness; see [[Loss Aversion]]. Not knowing allows hope and avoids the discomfort of confronting a problem; see [[Cognitive Dissonance]] and [[Emotional Regulation]]. When people do encounter information, they tend to weigh what they want to believe more heavily; see [[Motivated Reasoning]] and [[Confirmation Bias]].

Avoidance usually backfires. Problems left unexamined tend to grow: a symptom becomes harder to treat, a debt accrues interest. The relief is immediate, while the cost arrives later, a familiar trade-off; see [[Procrastination]] and [[Hyperbolic Discounting]]. People also tend to overestimate how bad bad news will feel, which makes avoiding it seem more worthwhile than it is; see [[Affective Forecasting]].

Organizations show the same pattern. Teams that don't want to hear about problems stop measuring them, and failures accumulate unseen until something breaks; see [[Silent Failure]] and [[Normalization of Deviance]]. Countermeasures include making bad news safe to deliver and deliberately imagining failure in advance; see [[Psychological Safety]] and [[Pre-mortem]]. Automated monitoring helps for a similar reason, since it reports bad news without waiting for anyone to go looking, and nobody has to decide to face it.

The opposite pattern exists too. Compulsively seeking threatening news, refreshing the feed for one more alarming headline, is another way that anxiety distorts information-seeking; see [[Doomscrolling]].
