**Shame** is the painful feeling that one's whole self is defective or exposed, usually in front of others, real or imagined. It belongs to a family called the **self-conscious emotions**, along with guilt, embarrassment and pride. Unlike fear or joy, these depend on a sense of self and of how that self looks to other people, which is why they emerge in toddlerhood, after children start to recognize themselves in a mirror and to grasp that others hold views of them; see [[Theory of Mind]].

The most useful distinction in the research is between shame and **guilt**. Psychologist June Tangney and colleagues describe guilt as focused on a behavior, "I did a bad thing," and shame as focused on the self, "I am bad." The difference changes what people do next. Guilt tends to produce remorse, apology and attempts to repair the harm. Shame tends to produce hiding, withdrawal, denial, or anger directed outward, since the problem feels like an unfixable identity rather than a fixable act. In Tangney's studies, a tendency toward guilt predicted better relationships and more empathy, while a tendency toward shame was linked to depression, anxiety and aggression; see [[Empathy]].

**Embarrassment** is the lighter cousin. It follows a minor social slip, passes quickly, and is often met with laughter. Charles Darwin called blushing "the most peculiar and the most human of all expressions," and later research suggests the blush works as an apology, signaling that the person knows the rule and cares about it.

Shame pulls attention toward how one is seen. It intensifies the feeling of being watched, the [[Spotlight Effect]], and it feeds habits built to avoid exposure. People who feel like frauds often describe shame at the core of [[Impostor Syndrome]]. Hiding traits that might bring judgment, such as autistic [[Masking]], can be sustained by it. Fear of the experience can shape a life, as in [[Rejection Sensitivity]] and in the standards of [[Perfectionism]]. The same concern with how one is seen runs through [[Politeness Theory]], which treats everyday courtesy as protecting each other's public self-image.

The anthropologist Ruth Benedict famously contrasted "shame cultures" with "guilt cultures" in The Chrysanthemum and the Sword (1946), a distinction that later scholars have criticized as too sharp. All societies use both, in different proportions and for different offenses.

The most studied antidote is [[Self-Compassion]]. Treating one's own failures as part of common human experience, rather than as proof of a defective self, tends to convert shame into something closer to guilt, which can then be acted on. Shame may also be easier to bear once it is spoken about; secrecy tends to deepen it, and [[Rumination]] keeps the self-image under constant review.
