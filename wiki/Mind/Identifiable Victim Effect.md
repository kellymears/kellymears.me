summary: The tendency to help a single, named person in need far more readily than a large number of anonymous people facing the same harm, so that compassion shrinks rather than grows as the numbers rise.
---
The **identifiable victim effect** is the tendency to give more help to a single, specific person in need than to many anonymous people facing the same danger. A child trapped down a well draws national attention and donations; thousands of deaths from a preventable disease draw far less. The economist Thomas Schelling described the asymmetry in 1968, distinguishing an identified life from a statistical one and noting that people will spend enormous sums to save the first while neglecting the second.

Experiments have measured it. In a 2007 study by Deborah Small, George Loewenstein and Paul Slovic, people asked to donate to help a seven-year-old girl named Rokia from Mali gave about twice as much as people shown statistics about millions of hungry children in Africa. Adding the statistics to Rokia's story reduced giving. Other research found that people gave more to help one sick child than to help a group of eight, even when the group was described in identical terms.

Several mechanisms seem to be at work. A single person with a face and a name invites an emotional response, while a number invites calculation; see [[Emotional Contagion]] and [[Theory of Mind]]. Vivid individual stories are also easier to imagine and recall; see [[Availability Heuristic]]. A statistical victim is psychologically distant, a member of a category rather than a person; see [[Psychological Distance]].

Slovic called the larger pattern **psychic numbing**: as the number of victims grows, the feeling doesn't grow with it, and it can even shrink. Like perceived brightness or loudness, concern responds to proportional rather than absolute change; see [[Weber-Fechner Law]]. Studies of **compassion fade** have found that willingness to help can drop when the number of victims goes from one to two. Related work on **scope insensitivity** found that people asked how much they would pay to save migrating birds from oil ponds named similar amounts whether 2,000, 20,000 or 200,000 birds were at stake.

The effect complicates moral reasoning. Charity appeals almost always feature one person rather than a statistic, because it works. Policy decisions that weigh lives against costs can seem callous when the lives are anonymous and cruel when a single case reaches the news; see [[Trolley Problem]]. Approaches that try to direct giving by expected impact are partly an attempt to correct for the bias; see [[Expected Value]].

Large numbers of victims can also make people feel their help would be a drop in the ocean, a sense of futility that adds to the numbing. Responsibility spread across a crowd of potential helpers weakens the pull further; see [[Bystander Effect]]. Constant exposure to suffering in news feeds may wear compassion down instead of building it; see [[Doomscrolling]] and [[Burnout]].
