An **optical illusion** is an image perceived differently from how it physically is. Two lines of equal length look unequal, two identical patches of gray look like different shades, or a still picture seems to move. Illusions aren't failures of eyesight. They're the visual system doing its usual job, inferring a three-dimensional world from flat and ambiguous input, in a situation where its assumptions don't fit.

The **Müller-Lyer illusion**, published by Franz Carl Müller-Lyer in 1889, shows two equal lines, one ending in outward-pointing arrowheads and one in inward-pointing ones. The first looks longer. In Edward Adelson's checker shadow illusion, from 1995, a square in shadow and a square in light are printed in exactly the same gray, yet look like dark and light squares of a checkerboard. The visual system discounts the shadow, as it should for real objects, and reports the color the surface probably is; see [[Color Theory]].

The nineteenth-century physicist Hermann von Helmholtz called this unconscious inference: perception is a best guess about the causes of sensory input, built on assumptions such as light coming from above and objects having stable colors. Modern accounts frame it as prediction, with the brain combining expectations and evidence; see [[Predictive Processing]]. Illusions are useful to researchers because they expose those expectations.

Some images support more than one interpretation, and perception flips between them, as with the Necker cube; see [[Multistable Perception]]. Some objects are impossible, like the Penrose triangle, whose parts each look consistent but can't form a whole. Others exploit grouping rules that link nearby or similar elements; see [[Gestalt Principles]]. In 2015, a photo of a dress split viewers between seeing it as white and gold or blue and black, apparently depending on unconscious assumptions about the lighting.

Knowing the trick usually doesn't dispel it. The lines still look unequal after being measured, a sign that early vision runs separately from conscious belief; see [[Naive Realism]]. Similar illusions occur in other senses. Sight can change what is heard, and touch can be relocated onto a fake limb; see [[McGurk Effect]] and [[Rubber Hand Illusion]]. Faces are seen in clouds and electrical outlets; see [[Pareidolia]].

Cross-cultural studies in the 1960s reported that people raised in environments with fewer rectangular buildings were less susceptible to the Müller-Lyer illusion, suggesting experience tunes the assumptions, though the interpretation is debated.

Displays and graphics depend on the same properties. Film and video create motion from still frames; see [[Apparent Motion]]. Patterns of a few colored dots can be blended by the eye into intermediate shades; see [[Dithering]]. Fine patterns sampled too coarsely produce moiré and other artifacts; see [[Aliasing]].
