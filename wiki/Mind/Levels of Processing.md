The **levels of processing** framework holds that memory depends less on how long information is held or repeated than on how deeply it is processed. Fergus Craik and Robert Lockhart proposed it in 1972 as an alternative to models that treated memory as a series of storage boxes; see [[Working Memory]].

The classic experiments, by Craik and Endel Tulving in 1975, asked people questions about words without telling them a memory test was coming:

- **Shallow**: "Is the word in capital letters?" Attention to appearance.
- **Intermediate**: "Does it rhyme with *weight*?" Attention to sound.
- **Deep**: "Would it fit in the sentence 'He met a ___ in the street'?" Attention to meaning.

On a surprise test, words processed for meaning were remembered far better, often two or three times as well as those judged by appearance. Intending to remember mattered less than what people did with the words.

The deepest processing found was relating material to oneself. In a 1977 study, people asked whether a word described them remembered it better than people asked whether it meant the same as another word. This **self-reference effect** is one reason personal examples help learning.

Practical consequences:

- **Elaborate.** Connect new information to what you already know: ask why it is true, how it relates to other ideas, where you've seen it before. Writing notes in your own words and linking them to older notes does this by design; see [[Zettelkasten]].
- **Generate.** Producing an answer or explanation yourself beats reading one; see [[Desirable Difficulty]]. Explaining a problem aloud forces the same depth; see [[Rubber Duck Debugging]].
- **Don't trust ease.** Rereading and highlighting feel productive because familiar material is easy to process, but ease is a poor guide to learning; see [[Processing Fluency]] and [[Metacognition]].
- **Imagery helps.** Vivid images and locations add another layer of encoding; see [[Method of Loci]].

Skimming fast mostly produces shallow processing, which is why comprehension drops at high reading speeds; see [[Speed Reading]].

The framework drew criticism. Depth was hard to define independently: if deep processing is whatever produces good memory, the theory is circular. And depth isn't always best. In 1977 Donald Morris, John Bransford, and Jeffery Franks showed that people who processed words for sound did better on a rhyming test than people who processed meaning. Memory works best when processing at learning matches what retrieval will demand, an idea close to encoding specificity; see [[Context-Dependent Memory]].

Depth also doesn't replace repetition and retrieval over time, which keep memories accessible; see [[Spaced Repetition]] and [[Memory Consolidation]]. Deeply processed material is rich in connections, which is why personal, meaningful events are remembered best; see [[Episodic Memory]].
