**Psychological safety** is a shared belief among members of a team that they won't be punished or humiliated for speaking up with ideas, questions, concerns, or mistakes. The organizational researcher Amy Edmondson defined it in a 1999 study, building on earlier work by Edgar Schein and Warren Bennis on how people learn in organizations.

Edmondson came to the idea by accident. Studying hospital nursing teams, she expected that better teams would make fewer medication errors. Instead, the best-led teams reported more errors. Further work suggested they didn't make more mistakes; they were more willing to talk about them, while people on other teams kept quiet for fear of blame. Teams that discuss errors can learn from them, so silence is costly.

The idea gained wide attention in 2016, when Google published results of its Project Aristotle, a study of what made some of its teams more effective than others. The composition of the team, such as members' skills or personalities, mattered less than how the team worked together, and psychological safety was the strongest factor.

The concept is often misunderstood. Psychological safety is not niceness, comfort, or lowered standards. Edmondson describes it as compatible with, and necessary for, high performance: a team with high standards but low safety produces anxiety and hidden problems, while a team with both enters a "learning zone." Safety also differs from trust between two people; it describes a team's norms.

Signs of low psychological safety are familiar:

- **Silence in meetings.** Concerns stay unspoken, especially to people with more power; see [[Spiral of Silence]] and [[Groupthink]].
- **Hidden mistakes.** People cover errors rather than reporting them, so problems grow; see [[Normalization of Deviance]].
- **Agreement in public, disagreement in private.** Each person assumes their doubts are unusual; see [[Pluralistic Ignorance]].

Fields with high stakes have built practices to protect speaking up. Aviation's crew resource management trains junior pilots to challenge captains, after accident investigations found that copilots had noticed problems but stayed quiet; see [[Checklist]]. Software teams hold reviews of outages that focus on causes in systems rather than individual fault; see [[Blameless Postmortem]] and [[Incident Response]]. Exercises that imagine failure in advance make it acceptable to voice doubts; see [[Pre-mortem]]. Practices such as reviewing each other's work go better when criticism doesn't feel like a threat; see [[Code Review]].

Leaders shape safety most, by admitting their own uncertainty, asking for input, and responding to bad news with curiosity rather than anger. People who already feel like outsiders are often the least likely to speak up, so the effects of low safety fall unevenly; see [[Impostor Syndrome]] and [[Rejection Sensitivity]].
