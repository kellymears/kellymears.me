**Misophonia** is a strong negative reaction to particular everyday sounds. The most common triggers are sounds other people make: chewing, slurping, breathing, sniffing, throat clearing, pen clicking, or keyboard tapping. The reaction goes beyond ordinary annoyance. People describe sudden anger, disgust, or panic, a rush of physical arousal, and an urgent need to escape or stop the sound.

The name, meaning "hatred of sound," was proposed in 2001 by the audiology researchers Pawel and Margaret Jastreboff, who worked with tinnitus and sound sensitivity; see [[Tinnitus]]. For years, people with the condition had no word for it and were often told they were being oversensitive. In 2022, an international panel of researchers published a consensus definition. Misophonia isn't listed as a distinct diagnosis in the major psychiatric manuals, and researchers still debate whether it's best seen as a disorder of its own or a feature of other conditions.

Typical features:

- **Specific triggers.** Reactions usually focus on a few particular sounds, often from specific people, such as family members, rather than loudness in general.
- **Visual triggers.** Seeing someone chew or fidget can provoke the same response, sometimes called misokinesia.
- **Early onset.** Symptoms often begin in late childhood or adolescence.
- **Avoidance.** People may avoid shared meals, offices, or classes, or wear headphones constantly.

Estimates of how common it is vary widely depending on how it's defined, from a few percent for severe cases to much higher figures for milder symptoms.

The causes are uncertain. Brain imaging studies have found stronger responses to trigger sounds in regions involved in processing emotion and internal bodily states; see [[Interoception]]. One hypothesis suggests heightened activity in motor regions, as if the brain were mirroring the movements of the person making the sound; see [[Mirror Neurons]]. Learned associations may also contribute, since repeated irritation in particular settings can strengthen the response; see [[Classical Conditioning]].

Misophonia overlaps with other conditions, including anxiety, obsessive-compulsive symptoms, and autism, though many people with it have none of these; see [[Sensory Processing]] and [[Neurodiversity]]. It differs from hyperacusis, where ordinary sounds feel uncomfortably loud or painful, and from general overstimulation in noisy environments; see [[Sensory Overload]].

There's no established cure. Approaches with some evidence include cognitive behavioral therapy, which targets the thoughts and reactions surrounding triggers, and gradual work on tolerating sounds; see [[Cognitive Behavioral Therapy]] and [[Exposure Therapy]]. Practical strategies include background noise, earplugs, or noise-cancelling headphones, and agreeing on accommodations with family or coworkers. Tools for staying calm when arousal spikes can also help; see [[Emotional Regulation]] and [[Window of Tolerance]].

For many people, simply learning that the experience has a name, and is shared by others, brings relief.
