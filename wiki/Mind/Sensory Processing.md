**Sensory processing** is how the nervous system receives sensory information, filters it, and turns it into perception and response. People differ enormously in how they do this. One person barely notices the hum of a refrigerator; another cannot think until it stops. One seeks spicy food, loud music, and constant movement; another finds clothing tags unbearable. These are not preferences in the usual sense but differences in how sensation is registered.

Beyond the familiar five senses, sensory processing includes several others:

- **[[Proprioception]].** The sense of where the body's parts are, from receptors in muscles and joints.
- **Vestibular.** Balance and movement, from the inner ear.
- **Interoception.** Signals from inside the body, such as hunger, heartbeat, and the need for the toilet, described under [[Interoception]].

The occupational therapist Jean Ayres developed sensory integration theory in the 1970s. Winnie Dunn's model, from 1997, is widely used to describe individual profiles. It crosses two dimensions: a person's *neurological threshold*, whether they notice input readily or need a lot of it, and their *response strategy*, whether they act on it or let it be. That yields four patterns:

- **Seeking.** High threshold, active: craving movement, noise, texture, or strong flavors.
- **Avoiding.** Low threshold, active: limiting input by leaving, covering ears, or controlling routines.
- **Sensitivity.** Low threshold, passive: noticing and being bothered by input without withdrawing from it.
- **Low registration.** High threshold, passive: missing cues others notice, such as a name being called.

Most people show a mix, and the same person can avoid sound while seeking pressure or movement. Differences are especially common in autism; since 2013 the DSM-5 has listed sensory hyper- or hypo-reactivity among autism's diagnostic features. They are also common in ADHD and anxiety. Under [[Predictive Processing]], some of this may reflect how much weight the brain gives incoming signals relative to its predictions, so that more input stays surprising rather than fading through [[Habituation]].

When input exceeds what can be processed, the result is [[Sensory Overload]], and repeated overload contributes to [[Autistic Burnout]]. Seeking behaviors, such as rocking, fidgeting, or pacing, are often regulation strategies rather than problems, as described under [[Stimming]].

Practical supports work on both the person and the environment:

- **A sensory diet.** A planned routine of input that helps regulation, such as heavy work, movement breaks, or deep pressure from a weighted blanket.
- **Environmental changes.** Dimmer or warmer lighting, quiet spaces, noise-cancelling headphones, and fewer simultaneous demands.
- **Design.** Interfaces can reduce load with options such as [[Reduced Motion]], and spaces with natural features can restore attention, as [[Attention Restoration Theory]] suggests.

Recognizing one's own profile, and building a life around it rather than [[Masking]] through discomfort, keeps people within their [[Window of Tolerance]].
