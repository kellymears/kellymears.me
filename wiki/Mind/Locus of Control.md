**Locus of control** is the degree to which a person believes outcomes depend on their own actions rather than outside forces. Someone with an **internal** locus tends to think success comes from effort and decisions. Someone with an **external** locus tends to credit luck, fate, other people, or the system. The psychologist Julian Rotter introduced the idea in 1954 and published a widely used scale for measuring it in 1966.

Rotter's insight came from learning theory. If people don't believe their actions affect outcomes, rewards and punishments shouldn't change their behavior much, because they don't see the connection. Belief about control, not just control itself, shapes how people learn from experience.

An internal locus is associated with many good outcomes: more effort in school and work, better health habits, and lower stress. People who see outcomes as controllable tend to try harder and persist; see [[Self-Efficacy]] and [[Growth Mindset]]. A strongly external locus resembles [[Learned Helplessness]], where repeated uncontrollable failures teach people to stop trying even when control returns.

The picture is less simple than "internal is good":

- **Accuracy matters.** Some outcomes really aren't controllable. Blaming oneself for illness, layoffs, or discrimination can add guilt to misfortune.
- **Too much internality.** People who believe everything is up to them can become anxious and self-critical when things go wrong; see [[Rumination]].
- **Structural blind spots.** Treating success as purely personal can lead people to blame others for circumstances beyond their control; see [[Fundamental Attribution Error]].

The opposite error is the **illusion of control**, described by Ellen Langer in 1975. People act as if they can influence chance: they value a lottery ticket they picked more than one handed to them, throw dice harder for high numbers, and feel more confident when they make choices in a game of luck; see [[Gambler's Fallacy]]. Games exploit this with buttons to press and choices to make before a random outcome.

The most useful stance separates what can be controlled from what can't, an idea as old as Stoic philosophy and as current as therapy. [[Cognitive Behavioral Therapy]] helps people test beliefs about control, while acceptance-based approaches help with what can't be changed. The question "what part of this is mine to act on?" applies to both.

Control matters physiologically, too. Uncontrollable stressors produce larger stress responses than controllable ones of equal size, even when the control isn't used; see [[Allostatic Load]]. Having a sense of autonomy is one of the basic needs in [[Self-Determination Theory]].

Organizations shape locus of control. Blaming individuals for system failures teaches people that outcomes depend on luck and politics, while reviews that focus on causes teach them what they can actually change; see [[Blameless Postmortem]].
