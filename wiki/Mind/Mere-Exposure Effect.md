The **mere-exposure effect** is the tendency to prefer things simply because they've been encountered before. Repeated exposure to a word, face, melody, shape, or brand makes people rate it more positively, even when nothing else about it changes. The psychologist Robert Zajonc demonstrated it in 1968 with nonsense words and Chinese characters shown to people who couldn't read them: the more often a character had been shown, the more people guessed it meant something good.

The effect works even without awareness. In later studies, shapes flashed too briefly to be consciously seen were still preferred afterward over new ones. People liked them without being able to say they'd seen them.

The leading explanation is that familiar things are easier to process, and that ease feels good. The mind misattributes its own fluency to the object: this is easy to think about, so it must be pleasant, true, or safe; see [[Processing Fluency]]. An evolutionary reading adds that something encountered repeatedly without harm is probably safe. Novelty triggers caution, and caution fades with repetition; see [[Habituation]].

The effect is everywhere:

- **Music.** Songs usually grow on listeners with repeated play, and radio rotation exploits this.
- **Faces.** People prefer their mirror image, the version they see daily, while friends prefer their true image.
- **Interfaces.** Users often prefer a familiar design to an objectively better new one, and redesigns face backlash that fades with exposure; see [[Mental Model]].
- **Advertising.** Repeated brand exposure builds liking with little information.
- **Beliefs.** Repeated statements feel truer, regardless of accuracy; see [[Illusory Truth Effect]].

The effect has limits. It's strongest for neutral or mildly positive things; repetition of something disliked can deepen the dislike. Too much exposure brings boredom, and the gains level off; see [[Boredom]]. It's also weaker for complex things, which reveal more over repeated encounters, than for simple ones that wear out quickly.

Mere exposure helps explain several broader patterns. It contributes to status quo bias, since the current option is the familiar one; see [[Loss Aversion]]. It supports [[Nostalgia]], since older music and media have had years to become familiar. It reinforces in-group preferences and can make the unfamiliar seem threatening, one reason contact between groups reduces prejudice. And in technology choices, teams often prefer what they already know over a better alternative they haven't tried; see [[Not Invented Here]].

In design, the effect argues for following conventions, since users arrive already familiar with standard patterns, and for introducing changes gradually. A new design may need time before it can be fairly judged. Testing a change on users for only a few days can mistake unfamiliarity for worse usability; see [[AB Testing]].
