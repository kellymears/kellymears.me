**Aphantasia** is the inability to voluntarily form mental images. Asked to picture an apple, most people report some inner image, faint or vivid. People with aphantasia know what an apple looks like and can describe it in detail, but see nothing. The opposite extreme, **hyperphantasia**, is imagery so vivid it can rival perception. Most people sit somewhere between.

Francis Galton noticed the variation in 1880, when he asked people to imagine their breakfast table and rate how clearly they saw it. Some scientists among his respondents insisted mental imagery was a figure of speech. The neurologist Adam Zeman named aphantasia in 2015, after studying a man who lost his imagery following heart surgery and then hearing from thousands who had never had any. Estimates suggest roughly 1 to 4 percent of people have little or no visual imagery. The *Vividness of Visual Imagery Questionnaire*, from 1973, is the common self-report measure.

Its most striking feature is how long it goes unnoticed. Inner experience is private, so people assume others' minds work like their own. Aphantasic people often interpret "picture a beach" or "count sheep" as metaphors, and only discover in adulthood, often from an article, that others mean it literally. The discovery runs both ways: people with vivid imagery are equally surprised that others lack it. It is a vivid case of the limits of [[Theory of Mind]] and of the [[Curse of Knowledge]].

Aphantasia is a difference in experience, not a deficit in thinking, and fits naturally within [[Neurodiversity]]. Aphantasic people perform normally on most spatial reasoning tasks, solving them through spatial or verbal strategies rather than images. Some differences do show up:

- **Autobiographical memory.** Many report weaker re-living of personal memories, recalling facts about events rather than seeing them again.
- **Dreams.** Many still dream in images, suggesting voluntary and involuntary imagery differ.
- **Other senses.** Imagery can be absent for sounds, smells, or touch as well, or only for vision.
- **Memory techniques.** Methods that depend on imagery, such as the [[Method of Loci]], need adapting, though spatial versions can still work.

Objective evidence has accumulated. A 2022 study found that imagining a bright scene makes most people's pupils constrict slightly, as they would for real light, while aphantasic people's pupils did not respond. Brain-imaging studies show differing connectivity between visual and frontal regions. Under [[Predictive Processing]], imagery can be understood as the brain running its visual predictions without input, and aphantasia as a difference in how strongly those predictions reach conscious experience.

Inner experience varies along many such dimensions. Some people have little [[Inner Speech]], some experience [[Synesthesia]], and some form imagery only in [[Working Memory]] for an instant. Asking how someone thinks, rather than assuming, usually turns up surprises.
