**Loss aversion** is the tendency for losing something to feel worse than gaining the same thing feels good. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky made it a pillar of **prospect theory**, their 1979 account of how people actually decide under risk, as opposed to how classical economics said they should.

Prospect theory made three claims that standard expected-value reasoning lacked:

- **Reference points.** People evaluate outcomes as gains or losses relative to a reference point, usually the status quo or an expectation, not as final states of wealth.
- **Loss aversion.** The value curve is steeper for losses than for gains. Early estimates put losses at roughly twice the weight of gains.
- **Diminishing sensitivity.** The difference between $10 and $20 feels larger than between $1,010 and $1,020, for both gains and losses. That makes people risk-averse for gains and risk-seeking for losses: they take a sure gain but gamble to avoid a sure loss.

Several familiar effects follow:

- **Endowment effect.** People value things more once they own them. In a famous 1990 experiment by Kahneman, Jack Knetsch, and Richard Thaler, people given a coffee mug asked about twice as much to sell it as others would pay to buy it.
- **Status quo bias.** Change involves giving something up, and the losses loom larger than the gains, so people stick with what they have. It is one reason defaults are so powerful in [[Choice Architecture]].
- **Sunk costs.** Walking away means accepting a loss, which helps explain the [[Sunk Cost Fallacy]].
- **Gambling to break even.** People who are down take bigger risks to get back to zero, the reference point.

How a choice is described can change whether it registers as a gain or a loss, which is the [[Framing Effect]]. Loss aversion is also one form of a broader [[Negativity Bias]], where bad events affect people more than good ones.

Products use it deliberately. Free trials create an endowment that feels like a loss to give up. Streaks in apps and games make a missed day feel like losing something, not just failing to gain. Countdown timers and "only 2 left" banners frame inaction as loss, and when they are fake they become a [[Dark Pattern]].

Research has refined the picture. The two-to-one ratio varies widely across studies and contexts, and some researchers argue small, routine losses show little aversion at all. The effect is real but less universal than the early figures implied.

It fits with other findings about judgment: [[Anchoring Effect]] shows that reference points are easy to move, and [[Dual Process Theory]] describes the fast, intuitive system where loss aversion operates. The [[Hedonic Treadmill]] explains why gains fade quickly while losses linger.
