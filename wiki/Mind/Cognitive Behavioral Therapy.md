**Cognitive behavioral therapy**, or CBT, is a short-term, structured form of psychotherapy based on a simple model: situations do not cause feelings directly. The interpretation of a situation does, and feelings then drive behavior, which in turn shapes the next situation. Changing the thoughts or the behaviors can break a cycle that keeps someone anxious or depressed.

It grew from two sources. Albert Ellis developed rational emotive behavior therapy in the 1950s, and Aaron Beck, working with depressed patients in the 1960s, noticed recurring automatic negative thoughts about the self, the world, and the future. Beck's *cognitive therapy* merged with behavioral techniques drawn from learning theory, and the combination became CBT. It is now one of the most studied psychotherapies, with the strongest evidence for anxiety disorders and depression.

A central idea is the **cognitive distortion**, a habitual thinking error that makes things feel worse than the evidence supports. David Burns's 1980 book *Feeling Good* popularized a list:

- **Catastrophizing.** Jumping to the worst outcome: one mistake means being fired.
- **All-or-nothing thinking.** Anything short of perfect is a failure.
- **Mind reading.** Assuming others are thinking badly of you, a pattern close to [[Rejection Sensitivity]].
- **Overgeneralization.** One bad event becomes "this always happens".
- **Discounting the positive.** Successes don't count, a feature of [[Impostor Syndrome]].

The core techniques are practical:

- **Thought records.** Writing down a situation, the automatic thought, the emotion, and then the evidence for and against the thought, to arrive at a more balanced view. This is *cognitive restructuring*, a structured form of [[Metacognition]].
- **Behavioral activation.** Scheduling small, rewarding activities, since depression shrinks activity and inactivity deepens depression. Action often has to come before motivation, a lesson shared with [[Procrastination]] research.
- **Exposure.** Gradually facing feared situations without escape, so anxiety falls through [[Habituation]] and predictions of disaster are tested against reality.
- **Behavioral experiments.** Treating a belief as a hypothesis and designing a small test of it.

So-called third-wave therapies grew from CBT: acceptance and commitment therapy, which aims to change one's relationship to thoughts rather than their content; dialectical behavior therapy, built around [[Emotional Regulation]] skills; and mindfulness-based cognitive therapy, which combines CBT with [[Mindfulness]] to prevent relapse into depression and break [[Rumination]].

CBT has limits. It works less well when problems stem from circumstances rather than interpretation, and labeling accurate perceptions as distortions can be invalidating. That matters for autistic and other neurodivergent people, whose negative expectations may reflect real experiences of exclusion. Adapted CBT for autistic clients uses more concrete and visual material, attends to [[Sensory Processing]] needs, and avoids treating [[Masking]] as a goal. Done well, CBT is less about thinking positively than thinking accurately, and acting on that.
