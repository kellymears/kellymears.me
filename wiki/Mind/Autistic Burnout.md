**Autistic burnout** is a state of pervasive, long-term exhaustion that autistic adults have described for decades in their own communities, well before researchers studied it. A 2020 study by Dora Raymaker and colleagues, built on autistic people's own accounts, defined it by three features: chronic exhaustion, a loss of skills the person previously had, and reduced tolerance for stimulation. It typically lasts months, sometimes years, and it can look from the outside like a sudden decline in someone who "was managing fine."

Its distinguishing feature is **skill regression**. People lose access, temporarily, to abilities that used to be reliable: speech under pressure, cooking, keeping up with messages, tolerating a noisy shop, holding down a job. [[Executive Function]] narrows sharply. [[Sensory Overload]] arrives at lower thresholds, and meltdowns and shutdowns become more frequent. In [[Spoon Theory]] terms, the daily allowance of spoons shrinks while the cost of each task rises.

The model behind it is a **load exceeding capacity over time**. Sources of load include:

- **Masking.** The constant effort of [[Masking]] autistic traits to pass in social settings, often cited as the single biggest cause.
- **Sensory and social environments** that are never designed for the person and never let up.
- **Life transitions.** A new job, parenthood, moving home, or the end of school's external structure.
- **Lack of accommodation and recovery time,** including the pressure to keep up appearances while struggling.

Autistic burnout differs from occupational burnout, which centers on work and cynicism toward it, and from depression, though the two can co-occur and be mistaken for each other. People in burnout often still want to do things. They can't, and that loss of capacity is the defining feature, not loss of interest. Treating it as depression alone can miss the cause.

Recovery is mostly about reducing load: dropping masking where safe, cutting demands, withdrawing to low-stimulation environments, and long periods of rest. People describe recovery as slow and incomplete if load returns to its old level. It shares a shape with overtraining in sport, where [[Training Load]] outruns recovery long enough that performance declines rather than improves, and rest is the only fix. Recognizing the warning signs early, before skills begin slipping, is the most practical protection. [[Interoception]] differences can make those signals hard to read in time.
