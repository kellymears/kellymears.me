**Moral licensing** is the tendency to behave less ethically after doing something good, as if the earlier act earned credit that can be spent. A person who has just donated, eaten a salad, or rejected a prejudiced statement may feel they've proved what kind of person they are, and so feel freer to cut a corner next. The same pattern is sometimes called **self-licensing** when it applies to self-control rather than ethics, such as treating a workout as permission for dessert.

The psychologists Benoît Monin and Dale Miller reported an early demonstration in 2001. Participants who had been given a chance to disagree with blatantly sexist statements were later more willing to say that a man was better suited to a stereotypically male job. Having established their credentials as unprejudiced, they seemed less worried about appearing biased.

The mechanism resembles a ledger. People keep loose accounts of their own goodness, and a credit on one side can offset a debit on the other; see [[Mental Accounting]]. Wanting to see oneself as good, while also wanting what a lapse offers, creates tension that a past good deed relieves; see [[Cognitive Dissonance]] and [[Self-Serving Bias]]. Even intending to do good can produce the effect, so that planning a virtuous act licenses an indulgence before the act happens.

The effect has close relatives outside psychology. People who adopt a safety measure sometimes take more risks, giving back part of the benefit; see [[Risk Compensation]]. Energy-efficient appliances can lead people to use more energy overall; see [[Jevons Paradox]]. A healthy-sounding label can make people eat more of a food, a halo carried from one trait to another; see [[Halo Effect]].

Moral licensing has the opposite tendency to contend with. Sometimes doing good makes people more likely to do good again, because it strengthens a self-image they then want to keep; see [[Habit Formation]]. Which effect wins seems to depend on how people think about the first act. Treating it as progress toward a goal tends to license slacking off, while treating it as evidence of commitment tends to encourage consistency.

The size of the effect is disputed. A 2015 meta-analysis found a small-to-moderate average effect, but later large preregistered replications of classic licensing studies found much weaker effects or none; see [[Replication Crisis]]. Related ideas about self-control as a limited resource that runs out have fared similarly; see [[Decision Fatigue]].

Some practical advice follows regardless of the debate. Tying behavior to identity rather than to a running score, and setting up commitments in advance, leaves less room for a good day to excuse a bad one; see [[Commitment Device]] and [[Implementation Intention]].
