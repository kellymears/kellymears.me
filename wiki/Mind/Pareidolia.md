**Pareidolia** is perceiving a meaningful image, usually a face, in something that has none: two windows and a door that form a surprised house, a frowning electrical outlet, a man in the moon, a face on Mars. The 1976 Viking orbiter photograph of a mesa in the Cydonia region, which looked like a human face in low-resolution shadow, became a famous example; later high-resolution images showed an ordinary hill.

Faces are the overwhelming favorite, and the brain treats them as special. Regions in the temporal lobe respond selectively to faces, and imaging studies show they also respond to face-like objects within a fraction of a second, almost as quickly as to real faces. People rapidly assign emotions and even gender to pareidolic faces. Babies orient toward simple face-like arrangements of three dots within hours of birth.

The tendency makes sense as a trade-off between errors. Mistaking a shadow for a face, or a rustle for a predator, costs little. Missing a real face or predator could cost a great deal. A perceptual system that errs toward false alarms will see faces in clouds, a principle sometimes called error management. Under [[Predictive Processing]], perception combines incoming signals with strong expectations, and expectations of faces are among the strongest the brain holds.

**Apophenia**, a term coined by the psychiatrist Klaus Conrad in 1958, is the broader tendency to perceive meaningful connections between unrelated things. Its statistical forms include seeing streaks in random sequences, the clustering illusion, and believing a coin is "due" to land heads. It feeds superstition and conspiracy thinking, where every coincidence seems to confirm the theory, combining with [[Confirmation Bias]], and it is a reason to check claimed patterns against chance and [[Base Rate Fallacy]] before believing them. Related is *agent detection*, the readiness to attribute events to an intention, which may stem from the same machinery as [[Theory of Mind]].

Machines show their own pareidolia. Google's DeepDream, released in 2015, amplified whatever patterns a trained [[Neural Network]] detected, filling images with dog faces and eyes. Image classifiers confidently label random noise as objects, and models can find patterns in noise that do not generalize, the problem of [[Overfitting]]. Language models fill gaps with plausible but false detail, a cousin explored under [[Hallucination]].

Designers use pareidolia deliberately. Car fronts are styled as friendly or aggressive faces, and products are given eyes to seem approachable. Push faces toward realism and the effect can turn unsettling, as described under [[Uncanny Valley]]. The Rorschach inkblot test, published in 1921, tried to use pareidolia diagnostically, reading personality from what people saw in ambiguous shapes, though its scientific validity remains contested. [[Gestalt Principles]] describe the grouping rules that make random marks cohere into figures in the first place.
