summary: The habit of taking credit for success and blaming circumstances for failure, together with the related tendency of most people to rate themselves above average, a bias that protects self-esteem at the cost of learning.
---
**Self-serving bias** is the tendency to credit successes to one's own ability and effort while blaming failures on bad luck, other people, or circumstances. A student who aces a test credits hard study; one who fails had an unfair teacher. A company's annual report typically credits a good year to management's strategy and a bad one to the economy.

Social psychologists have studied the pattern since the 1970s, under the broader heading of attribution: how people explain the causes of behavior. A related error has people explain others' actions by their character while overlooking circumstances; see [[Fundamental Attribution Error]]. For their own failures, people flip the pattern and point to circumstances instead.

A close relative is the **better-than-average effect**. In a frequently cited 1981 study, a large majority of American students rated themselves as safer and more skilled drivers than the median, which can't be true of more than half of them. Similar results appear for judgments of intelligence, honesty, generosity, and ability to get along with others. People also tend to think they're less susceptible to bias than others; see [[Naive Realism]].

Several functions have been proposed:

- **Protecting self-esteem.** Explaining failures away keeps a positive self-image; see [[Motivated Reasoning]] and [[Cognitive Dissonance]].
- **Persistence.** Believing in one's own ability may sustain effort after setbacks; see [[Self-Efficacy]].
- **Impression management.** Public explanations are partly for others, so people may sometimes present a more self-serving account than they privately believe.

The bias varies. A large meta-analysis in 2004 found it widespread but stronger in some cultures and age groups than others, generally weaker in East Asian samples. People with depression often show less of it, a finding sometimes called depressive realism, though that research is contested. The opposite pattern, crediting luck for success and oneself for failure, shows up in impostor feelings; see [[Impostor Syndrome]].

The costs fall mainly on learning. If failures are always someone else's fault, there's nothing to change, and the same mistakes return. Groups show the same pattern collectively, blaming outsiders for their setbacks; see [[Social Identity Theory]]. Organizations that want to learn from failure try to separate explanation from blame, so people can admit their part without fear; see [[Blameless Postmortem]].

Other biases interact with it. After a success, people overestimate how predictable it was; see [[Hindsight Bias]]. Belief that outcomes are under personal control feeds both credit-taking and self-blame; see [[Locus of Control]]. The least skilled often overestimate their abilities most; see [[Dunning-Kruger Effect]]. Kindness toward one's own failures, rather than defensiveness, can make it easier to acknowledge them honestly; see [[Self-Compassion]] and [[Growth Mindset]].
