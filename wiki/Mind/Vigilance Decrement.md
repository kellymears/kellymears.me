The **vigilance decrement** is the drop in performance that comes from watching for rare signals over a long period. Detection rates fall, and responses slow, often within the first fifteen to thirty minutes. It matters most in jobs that consist of waiting for something unusual: radar operators, airport baggage screeners, lifeguards, radiologists, and anyone watching a monitoring dashboard.

The phenomenon was first measured during the Second World War. The Royal Air Force noticed that radar operators on anti-submarine patrols missed more contacts as their watches went on. Psychologist Norman Mackworth built the **Clock Test** in 1948: a pointer moving around a dial in small steps, occasionally jumping a double step. Participants watching for the double jumps missed more of them as time passed, with the largest decline in the first half hour.

Two explanations have competed:

- **Underload.** Monotonous tasks leave attention under-stimulated, so it drifts. The mind wanders, the state associated with the [[Default Mode Network]], and signals pass unnoticed. [[Boredom]] is part of the story.
- **Overload.** Sustained attention is hard work. Research by Joel Warm and colleagues found that vigilance tasks raise reported workload and stress, and that performance falls as mental resources are spent. The finding fits with [[Decision Fatigue]] and with the effort described in [[Selective Attention]].

Most researchers now think both play a part. Either way, the conclusion is the same: people are poor at long, passive watching.

A related problem is the **low prevalence effect**. Jeremy Wolfe's research showed that when targets are rare, observers miss far more of them, because they learn to expect nothing and stop searching early. Airport screeners rarely see a real threat, so some systems project fake threat images onto bags at random, keeping the target rate high enough to sustain attention and to measure performance. [[Inattentional Blindness]] is the extreme case, where an unexpected object goes unseen entirely.

Automation makes the problem worse. In her 1983 paper "Ironies of Automation," Lisanne Bainbridge pointed out that automating a task often leaves humans with only the monitoring, the part they're worst at, and then expects them to take over instantly in a crisis. Over-trusting the system, [[Automation Bias]], compounds it.

Practical defenses follow:

- **Rotate and rest.** Lifeguards and screeners rotate positions, and short breaks restore performance, as [[Attention Restoration Theory]] suggests.
- **Make signals salient.** Alerts that stand out and are rare enough to mean something, since too many teach people to ignore them, the trap of [[Alarm fatigue]].
- **Design for action, not watching.** Good [[Observability]] pushes problems to people through alerts instead of relying on someone staring at graphs, and [[Incident Response]] rotations limit how long anyone is on watch.

Long reviews have the same limit, which is why [[Code Review]] catches less when a change is large.
