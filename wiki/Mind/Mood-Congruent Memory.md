**Mood-congruent memory** is the tendency to remember material that matches one's current emotional state. In a sad mood, sad memories come to mind more easily; in a cheerful mood, pleasant ones do. The effect shapes not just what people recall but how they interpret their own past, and through it how they feel about the present. A person's life story can seem darker or brighter depending on the day it's told.

The psychologist Gordon Bower brought the idea into the laboratory around 1980. Bower induced happy or sad moods in volunteers, sometimes through hypnosis, and then had them learn material or recall events from their lives. People recalled more mood-matching material, and they learned emotionally congruent material better too. Bower proposed that emotions act as nodes in a memory network, so activating a mood spreads activation to associated memories; see [[Priming]].

Psychologists distinguish two related effects:

- **Mood-congruent memory.** Material whose emotional tone matches current mood is easier to learn and retrieve.
- **Mood-dependent memory.** Material learned in one mood, whatever its content, is easier to recall in the same mood, a version of the broader principle that matching conditions at learning and recall helps; see [[Context-Dependent Memory]].

Mood-congruent effects have proved more reliable than mood-dependent ones, which tend to appear only with strong moods and self-generated material, such as memories people produce themselves.

The effect matters most in depression. Depressed people recall negative memories more readily and positive ones less readily, and their memories of good times are often vague and general instead of specific. A study from the early 1980s took advantage of daily mood swings in depressed patients and found they recalled more unhappy memories at sadder times of day. The result is a self-reinforcing loop: low mood retrieves bad memories, which confirm a bleak view of life, which deepens the mood; see [[Rumination]] and [[Negativity Bias]].

The loop distorts judgment too. People in a bad mood recall more evidence that things go badly, so they predict worse futures; see [[Affective Forecasting]]. A similar selective search shows up wherever expectations steer what people look for; see [[Confirmation Bias]].

Several therapies target the loop. Cognitive approaches teach people to notice mood-driven thinking and question it; see [[Cognitive Behavioral Therapy]]. Practices that encourage observing thoughts without following them weaken the pull of rumination; see [[Mindfulness]]. Labeling emotions precisely can help separate a feeling from the story it tells; see [[Emotional Granularity]] and [[Emotional Regulation]].

The effect has a gentler side. Revisiting happy memories deliberately can lift mood, and wistful recollection of the past often blends sadness with warmth; see [[Nostalgia]] and [[Savoring]].
