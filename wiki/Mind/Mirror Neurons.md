**Mirror neurons** are brain cells that fire both when an animal performs an action and when it watches another perform the same action. They were discovered in macaque monkeys in the early 1990s and, for a time, were proposed as the explanation for empathy, imitation, language, and autism. The finding is real; most of the larger claims are not established.

The discovery came from Giacomo Rizzolatti's laboratory at the University of Parma. Researchers recording from single neurons in a motor planning area of the monkey brain found cells that fired when the monkey grasped a piece of food, and also when it watched an experimenter grasp one. Papers in 1992 and 1996 described them and gave them their name. Some were specific to particular actions, and some responded to the sound of an action, like a peanut cracking.

The interpretation that followed was ambitious. If watching an action activates the same neurons as performing it, the observer might understand the action by simulating it internally; see [[Embodied Cognition]] and [[Ideomotor Effect]]. Researchers proposed that mirror systems underlie imitation, learning by watching, grasping others' intentions and feelings, and even the evolution of language from gesture. In 2000 the neuroscientist V. S. Ramachandran predicted that mirror neurons would do for psychology what DNA had done for biology. Popular books spread the idea, often treating mirror neurons as shorthand for empathy.

Evidence in humans is mostly indirect. Brain scans show regions active both when people act and when they watch, but scanners measure blood flow across millions of cells, not individual neurons. Direct recordings from single human neurons, made during surgery for epilepsy, found some with mirror-like properties, a small and specific result.

Critics, notably the cognitive scientist Gregory Hickok in a 2014 book, argued that the theory overreached:

- **Understanding without mirroring.** People understand actions they can't perform, such as a bird flying, and patients with damage to motor areas still understand others' actions.
- **The broken mirror theory of autism**, which claimed autistic people have faulty mirror systems, has not held up in reviews of the evidence; see [[Theory of Mind]] and [[Neurodiversity]].
- **Learned, not innate.** The psychologist Cecilia Heyes argued that mirror properties arise from ordinary association: we see our own hands as we move them, so seeing and doing become linked. Training studies can create or reverse mirror responses.

Mirror neurons illustrate a pattern common in neuroscience: a vivid mechanism, found in one species and one brain region, stretched to explain a wide range of human behavior; see [[Plausible Mechanism]] and [[Correlation and Causation]]. A neuron's activity during a behavior doesn't show that it causes the behavior.

The actual role of mirror systems is likely narrower, possibly helping predict and plan actions; see [[Predictive Processing]] and [[Motor Learning]].
