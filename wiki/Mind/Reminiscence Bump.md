**The reminiscence bump** is the tendency for older adults to remember more events from roughly ages 10 to 30 than from any other period of their lives. Asked to recall personal memories freely, or in response to cue words, people over about 40 produce a cluster of memories from adolescence and early adulthood, more than either a simple decline with age or a steady rate of forgetting would predict.

The psychologist David Rubin and colleagues described the pattern in the 1980s, plotting the ages at which people's remembered events occurred. The resulting curve has three parts:

- **Childhood amnesia**: very few memories from before about age three or four; see [[Episodic Memory]].
- **The bump**: a peak in memories from the teens and twenties.
- **Recency**: a rise in memories from the last few years, which haven't yet faded.

The curve resembles the pattern in recalling a list, where early and late items are remembered best; see [[Serial Position Effect]].

The bump appears across many countries and cultures, and applies not only to personal events but to preferences. People tend to name the music, films, and books from their youth as their favorites, and their judgments of the most important public events of their lifetime also skew toward that period; see [[Nostalgia]] and [[Earworm]].

Several explanations compete, and they may all contribute:

- **Novelty.** Early adulthood is full of firsts: first job, first love, first home, first time living away. Distinct events are easier to remember than routine ones; see [[Von Restorff Effect]].
- **Identity.** Adolescence and early adulthood are when people form a lasting sense of who they are, and memories from that period become anchors for the self-story; see [[Social Identity Theory]].
- **Cognitive peak.** Memory and learning may work at their best in early adulthood, so experiences are encoded more strongly; see [[Memory Consolidation]].
- **Life scripts.** Dorthe Berntsen and Rubin proposed that cultures share expectations about when major life events should happen. These scripts guide what people recall and treat as significant. When people imagine typical lives, they place important events in the same period.

Supporting the life script account, the bump is strongest for positive memories, which match cultural expectations of weddings, graduations, and births. Sad and traumatic memories show a weaker pattern.

Cultures mark these transitions with ceremonies that make them more memorable still; see [[Rite of Passage]]. And people often look back on those years warmly, partly because of how memory selects what to keep; see [[Peak-End Rule]] and [[Savoring]].

The reminiscence bump suggests that a life's memories aren't spread evenly across its years. The periods in which people change most, encounter the most firsts, and decide who they are tend to dominate the story they tell later; see [[Fresh Start Effect]].
