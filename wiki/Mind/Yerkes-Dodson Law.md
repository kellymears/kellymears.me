The **Yerkes–Dodson law** says that performance improves with arousal up to a point and then declines. Too little arousal and a person is bored, sluggish, and distractible. Too much and they are anxious, scattered, and prone to mistakes. In between sits a peak. The curve is usually drawn as an inverted U, and it adds one refinement: the peak sits lower for difficult or complex tasks and higher for simple, well-practiced ones. A sprinter can use a surge of adrenaline. Someone doing long division can't.

The origin is narrower than the textbook version. In 1908 Robert Yerkes and John Dodson trained mice to choose between two chambers, using electric shocks of varying strength as punishment for wrong choices. Learning was fastest at intermediate shock strengths when the discrimination was hard. Their paper said nothing about "arousal," a concept that arrived in the 1950s when the psychologist Donald Hebb and others recast the finding in terms of general activation. Later evidence for a single universal curve is mixed, and many studies find simpler or inconsistent shapes. It is best taken as a useful rule of thumb rather than a law.

As a rule of thumb, it explains a lot:

- **Deadlines.** Arousal rising as a deadline approaches is why some people only get going at the last minute, one engine of [[Procrastination]] and of [[Parkinson's Law]].
- **ADHD and understimulation.** Many ADHD accounts describe chronically sitting on the left side of the curve for tasks that aren't interesting, urgent, or novel, and seeking stimulation to get up the slope. Music, [[Body Doubling]], and movement such as [[Stimming]] can all be ways of adjusting arousal toward the peak.
- **Overload.** On the right side, [[Sensory Overload]] and stress narrow attention and drain [[Executive Function]]. [[Demand Avoidance]] can be partly read as the response to a demand that pushes arousal past the peak.
- **Flow.** [[Flow]] occurs when challenge and skill are matched, which is roughly the same balance seen from the task's side.

Designers apply the same idea. A game's [[Difficulty Curve]] tries to keep players between boredom and frustration, and [[Assist Mode]] lets players move the curve themselves. Training plans work the same way: [[Training Load]] has to be high enough to drive adaptation and low enough to recover from.

The practical lesson is to adjust stimulation to the task rather than maximize focus or calm. Interesting background noise for dull work, quiet for hard work, and a break when everything starts to feel urgent.
