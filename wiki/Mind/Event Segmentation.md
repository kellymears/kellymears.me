**Event segmentation** is the mind's habit of dividing continuous experience into separate events. Life arrives as an unbroken stream of sights, sounds and actions, yet people perceive and remember it as a sequence of episodes: making coffee, walking to the bus, a conversation. The points where one event ends and another begins are called **event boundaries**.

People agree remarkably well on where those boundaries fall. In studies beginning in the 1970s with the psychologist Darren Newtson, participants watched films of everyday activities and pressed a button whenever they felt one meaningful unit ended and another began. Different viewers marked boundaries at nearly the same moments, and they could segment at a coarse grain, such as washing the dishes, or a fine one, such as picking up a plate.

**Event segmentation theory**, developed by Jeffrey Zacks and colleagues in the 2000s, explains boundaries through prediction. The mind keeps a working model of the current situation and uses it to predict what happens next; see [[Predictive Processing]] and [[Working Memory]]. While predictions hold, the model stays in place. When something changes, a new location, a new goal, or a new person, prediction errors spike, the model is updated, and a boundary is marked.

Boundaries shape memory. Information from the current event is easy to access, while information from just before a boundary becomes harder to reach. Gabriel Radvansky's experiments showed that walking through a doorway makes people more likely to forget what they were carrying or planning, the **doorway effect**; see [[Context-Dependent Memory]]. Moments at boundaries themselves tend to be remembered well, and events are stored as separate episodes; see [[Episodic Memory]] and [[Memory Consolidation]].

Boundaries also affect time perception. A stretch of time filled with many distinct events seems longer in retrospect than a monotonous one of the same length, which is why a week of travel can feel longer than a month of routine; see [[Time Perception]]. Starting a new task leaves traces of the previous one active, which is easier to manage if the boundary is clear; see [[Attention Residue]] and [[Zeigarnik Effect]].

Film editors rely on segmentation without naming it. Cuts placed at event boundaries go largely unnoticed, because the mind is already updating its model there, while cuts in the middle of an action are jarring; see [[Kuleshov Effect]]. Stories that open in the middle of action exploit the brain's readiness to build a model from partial information; see [[In Medias Res]].

People also mark larger boundaries in their lives deliberately, using birthdays, new years and ceremonies to close one chapter and start another; see [[Fresh Start Effect]] and [[Rite of Passage]]. Work intervals with clear breaks create boundaries on purpose; see [[Pomodoro Technique]].
