The **Von Restorff effect**, or isolation effect, is the tendency to remember an item that differs noticeably from those around it. In a list of words printed in black, the one printed in red is more likely to be recalled. In a list of numbers, the single word stands out. The psychiatrist Hedwig von Restorff reported the effect in 1933, working in the Gestalt psychology tradition that studied how the mind organizes perception into figures and grounds.

Distinctiveness can come from almost any dimension: color, size, font, sound, meaning, or emotional charge. What matters is contrast with the immediate context. A red word among red words gains nothing, and a calm word among alarming ones can become the standout.

The usual explanation is that distinctive items get extra processing. They capture attention as they arrive, a form of bottom-up [[Selective Attention]], and they are encoded with more detail, which makes them easier to retrieve later. In [[Predictive Processing]] terms, an item that breaks the pattern is a surprise, and surprises are flagged for learning. The effect also works on recall: a distinctive item is easier to pick out from memory because few other memories compete with it.

The effect is strong enough to overcome position. In the [[Serial Position Effect]], items in the middle of a list are usually forgotten, but a distinctive item in the middle escapes that slump.

Designers rely on it constantly:

- **Calls to action.** One button colored differently from everything else on the page draws the eye and is remembered. If many elements are emphasized, none stand out, a failure of [[Visual Hierarchy]].
- **Pricing pages.** A "recommended" plan with a highlighted border and badge pulls attention away from the others.
- **Highlighting and notes.** Marking a key sentence makes it memorable, though highlighting everything defeats the purpose.
- **Alerts and notifications.** Distinct sounds and colors make critical warnings stand out from routine ones. Too many alerts erode this, the problem known as alarm fatigue.

Distinctiveness has costs. Isolating one item can reduce memory for its neighbors, as attention shifts away from them. The effect can also be weaponized. A [[Dark Pattern]] may make the option a company prefers bright and large while hiding alternatives in plain gray text. And it depends on perceiving the difference: an item that stands out only by color fails for people with [[Color Vision Deficiency]], so emphasis should also use size, shape, or text.

The effect connects to broader ideas about memory and attention. Vivid, unusual events dominate recall, which feeds the [[Availability Heuristic]]: a rare plane crash is easier to remember than thousands of uneventful flights. The same principle explains why memory techniques such as the [[Method of Loci]] favor bizarre images.
