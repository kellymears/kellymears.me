The **extended mind** is the claim that cognition can reach beyond the brain into the tools we think with. The philosophers Andy Clark and David Chalmers made the argument in a 1998 paper with a thought experiment. Inga hears about a museum exhibition, recalls that the museum is on 53rd Street, and goes. Otto has Alzheimer's disease and carries a notebook everywhere. He hears about the same exhibition, looks up the address in his notebook, and goes. Clark and Chalmers argued that the notebook plays the same role for Otto that memory plays for Inga. If it is reliably available, automatically trusted, and easy to consult, it is part of his mind in every way that matters.

Philosophers still debate the strong claim, but the practical version, **cognitive offloading**, is widely studied and uncontroversial. People routinely move mental work into the world: writing lists, setting reminders, counting on fingers, tilting their head to read a rotated sign. Offloading frees limited [[Working Memory]] for the task itself and reduces [[Cognitive Load]].

The trade-off is that what we offload, we don't retain. In a 2011 study, people who expected to be able to look up facts later remembered them less well but remembered where to find them better, the so-called **Google effect**. Later replications were mixed, but the basic trade is well established: memory follows from expecting to need it.

For many neurodivergent people, offloading isn't a convenience but the main accommodation:

- **Time.** Visible timers and calendars compensate for [[Time Blindness]].
- **Tasks.** Writing down the next action, not just the goal, makes it easier to start, which helps with [[Autistic Inertia]] and [[Executive Function]] gaps generally. An [[Implementation Intention]] is a plan offloaded in advance.
- **Open loops.** Capturing unfinished tasks quiets the nagging the [[Zeigarnik Effect]] describes.
- **Other people.** [[Body Doubling]] offloads part of self-regulation onto someone else's presence.

Note-taking systems are the extended mind built on purpose. A [[Zettelkasten]] is a memory designed to be consulted and to talk back, and personal wikis and linked-note tools are its digital descendants, often sold as a "second brain." The same design problem appears for software agents: a [[Large Language Model]] has no lasting memory beyond its [[Context Window]], so [[Agent Memory]] and [[Retrieval-Augmented Generation]] give it an Otto's notebook to consult.
