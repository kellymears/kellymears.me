**Mental accounting** is the tendency to sort money into separate mental categories, treating it differently depending on where it came from or what it is meant for. Economic theory says money is fungible: a dollar is a dollar, whether found on the street or earned. People don't behave that way. The economist Richard Thaler developed the idea from the 1980s, part of the work that won him the 2017 Nobel Prize in economics.

A classic example comes from Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman. Imagine you've bought a ten-dollar theater ticket and lose it on the way in. Would you buy another? Many say no. Now imagine you lose a ten-dollar bill on the way to buy the ticket. Most would still buy one. The loss is the same, but in the first case the ticket's cost is charged twice to the "theater" account, making the show feel like it costs twenty; see [[Framing Effect]].

Common patterns:

- **Source matters.** Windfalls, tax refunds, and gambling winnings are spent more freely than earned income. Gamblers who are ahead take bigger risks with "house money."
- **Labels matter.** People keep savings earning little interest while carrying credit card debt at high interest, because the savings are labeled for something else.
- **Sunk costs count.** Having paid for something, people feel obliged to use it even when they no longer want to; see [[Sunk Cost Fallacy]].
- **Gains and losses are grouped strategically.** Separate gains feel better than one combined gain, and one combined loss hurts less than several, which is why sellers bundle costs and list bonuses separately; see [[Loss Aversion]].

The **pain of paying** varies with how payment happens. Cash, which you watch leaving your hand, hurts more than a card, and a card more than a subscription billed automatically. Casinos use chips and games use premium currencies partly because they separate spending from money; see [[Gacha Monetization]] and [[Faucets and Sinks]].

Mental accounting is not purely an error. Budgeting by category, including the old envelope method of putting cash in envelopes labeled rent and food, works because it exploits these rules. Earmarking money for a goal makes it harder to spend on impulse, a self-imposed limit; see [[Commitment Device]] and [[Hyperbolic Discounting]]. It also saves effort, sparing people from weighing every purchase against every other; see [[Decision Fatigue]].

The cost is that categories can blind people to tradeoffs. Every dollar spent in one account is still a dollar unavailable elsewhere; see [[Opportunity Cost]]. Designers of financial products and policies shape these accounts deliberately, for example by defaulting a portion of pay into savings so it never enters the spending account; see [[Choice Architecture]].
