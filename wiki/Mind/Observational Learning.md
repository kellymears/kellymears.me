**Observational learning** is acquiring a behavior, a fear or a piece of knowledge by watching someone else, without having to perform it or be rewarded oneself. Most of what people know how to do, from tying shoes to using a fork, was learned this way long before anyone explained it.

The classic demonstration is the **Bobo doll experiment**. In 1961 Albert Bandura, Dorothea Ross and Sheila Ross had preschool children watch an adult play with an inflatable clown, either quietly or by hitting, kicking and shouting at it. Left alone with the doll afterward, children who had seen the aggressive model copied many of the same actions, including the exact phrases. Critics have noted that a punching doll invites punching, so the setup may say more about play than about violence, but the core finding of precise imitation held.

A 1965 follow-up separated learning from doing. Children who saw the model punished for aggression imitated less, yet when offered a reward to show what the model had done, they could reproduce it just as well. The behavior had been learned and simply not performed. Seeing others rewarded or punished is called **vicarious reinforcement**, an extension of [[Operant Conditioning]] that does not require personal experience.

Bandura developed this into **social learning theory**, later renamed social cognitive theory. It names four requirements: attention to the model, retention of what was seen, the ability to reproduce it, and motivation to do so. The same framework produced the concept of [[Self-Efficacy]], since watching someone similar succeed is one of the main ways people come to believe they can.

Emotions can be learned by observation too. Laboratory-raised rhesus monkeys, which show little fear of snakes, acquired a lasting fear after watching wild-born monkeys react to one, an observational counterpart to [[Classical Conditioning]].

Humans are unusually committed imitators. In a 2005 study by Victoria Horner and Andrew Whiten, children who watched an adult open a puzzle box copied unnecessary steps even when they could see the steps did nothing, while chimpanzees skipped them. This **over-imitation** looks inefficient, but it may be what lets cultures pass down rituals, tools and techniques whose purpose no single learner understands. Behaviors that spread by copying are the subject of [[Social Proof]], [[Conformity]] and the [[Meme]] in its original sense.

Teaching makes deliberate use of the process. Worked examples, apprenticeships and demonstrations rely on it, and learners gain the most from models only slightly ahead of them; see [[Zone of Proximal Development]]. Watching is rarely enough for expert skill, which still needs feedback on one's own attempts; see [[Deliberate Practice]]. [[Mirror Neurons]] were once proposed as the brain basis of imitation, a link that remains debated.
