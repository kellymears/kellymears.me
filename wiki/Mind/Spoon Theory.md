**Spoon theory** is a metaphor for living with a limited, unpredictable supply of energy. Christine Miserandino, who has lupus, wrote it down in a 2003 essay describing how she explained her illness to a friend at a diner. She handed her friend a fistful of spoons and asked her to walk through an ordinary day. Each activity cost a spoon: getting up, showering, getting dressed, commuting. The friend ran out before lunch. That was the point. Healthy people start the day with an effectively unlimited supply and never count. Some people start with twelve and have to.

The metaphor spread far beyond lupus, into chronic illness, disability, and [[Neurodiversity]] communities, where people call themselves "spoonies". It works because it captures several things that are otherwise hard to explain:

- **Ordinary tasks have costs.** A phone call or a trip to the shop that is free for one person may cost a significant share of another's budget.
- **The budget varies from day to day.** Sleep, pain, illness, and stress set how many spoons are available, often unpredictably. Yesterday's capacity is not a promise about today's.
- **Borrowing from tomorrow has interest.** Pushing past the budget is possible, but the cost lands on the next day or week, sometimes as a crash.
- **Invisible costs count.** [[Masking]], sensory strain ([[Sensory Overload]]), and [[Decision Fatigue]] spend spoons without visible effort, which is why someone can be exhausted by a day that looked easy.

The model is close to how engineers already think about constrained systems. A [[Performance Budget]] or [[Token Budget]] makes a scarce resource explicit and assigns every feature a cost so that tradeoffs happen on purpose. Spoon theory does the same for a day: plan the expensive things, cut or delegate what can go, and keep a reserve.

It also changes how others read behavior. Cancelling plans or skipping something that "only takes five minutes" stops looking like flakiness, the [[Fundamental Attribution Error]] reading, and starts looking like budgeting. Athletes already manage energy this way; [[Training Load]] and recovery are spoon accounting with better instruments. The difference is that for spoonies, the budget isn't chosen.
