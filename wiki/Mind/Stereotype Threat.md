**Stereotype threat** is the proposed effect in which people perform worse on a task when reminded of a negative stereotype about their group's ability at it. Introduced by the psychologists Claude Steele and Joshua Aronson in 1995, it became one of the most influential findings in social psychology and later one of the most contested.

In the original experiments, Black and white students at Stanford took a difficult verbal test. When it was described as a measure of intellectual ability, Black students scored lower than white students with similar entrance scores; when it was described as a lab task unrelated to ability, the gap largely disappeared. Similar studies followed on women and mathematics, older adults and memory, and white men compared with Asian men in math. The idea was that the worry of confirming a stereotype becomes a burden, adding pressure much like other threats to performance; see [[Choking Under Pressure]].

Proposed mechanisms included anxiety, attempts to suppress thoughts of the stereotype, and monitoring one's own performance, all of which consume limited attention; see [[Working Memory]] and [[Ironic Process Theory]]. Researchers suggested remedies: describing tests as non-diagnostic, teaching that ability grows with effort, and brief writing exercises affirming students' values; see [[Growth Mindset]] and [[Self-Efficacy]].

The finding shaped education and policy, and it was often cited as a partial explanation of real-world gaps in test scores. That reach invited scrutiny:

- **Small samples.** Many early studies used a few dozen participants, enough to produce striking effects by chance; see [[Statistical Power]].
- **Publication bias.** Meta-analyses found that the published literature showed the pattern expected if null results went unpublished, and corrected estimates were much smaller, possibly near zero for some groups; see [[Publication Bias]].
- **Failed replications.** A large preregistered study of Dutch high school girls in 2018 found no effect of stereotype threat on math performance; see [[Replication Crisis]].
- **Flexible analysis.** Early studies often adjusted scores for prior performance in ways that made effects easier to find; see [[P-Hacking]].

Researchers disagree about what remains. Some argue the effect is real but smaller and dependent on conditions, such as tests at the limit of a person's ability and strong identification with the domain. Others argue the evidence can't distinguish it from zero.

Stereotype threat illustrates a broader pattern from the replication crisis: an elegant, socially meaningful effect, established with small studies, grows in influence faster than in evidence; see [[Effect Size]]. It also overlaps with related ideas about how expectations affect performance, such as feeling like a fraud; see [[Impostor Syndrome]] and [[Priming]].

Even if a short reminder before a test has little effect, long experience of low expectations may still shape effort, interest, and choices.
