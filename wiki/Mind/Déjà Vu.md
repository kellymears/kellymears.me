**Déjà vu**, French for "already seen," is the feeling that a new situation has been experienced before, combined with the knowledge that it can't have been. About two-thirds of people report having had it. It usually lasts a few seconds and leaves people unsettled, sometimes wondering whether they have predicted the future, which they haven't.

The feeling is more common in younger people, and it becomes less frequent with age. It occurs more often in people who travel a lot, who remember their dreams, and who are tired or stressed; see [[Sleep Deprivation]]. It can also be part of an aura, the warning experience before seizures that start in the temporal lobe, which helped researchers find the brain regions involved.

The leading explanation treats déjà vu as a mismatch in the brain's two ways of judging memory. **Familiarity** is a fast sense that something has been encountered before; **recollection** is the slower retrieval of where and when. Normally the two agree. In déjà vu, familiarity fires without any matching recollection, and the conscious mind notices the contradiction; see [[Processing Fluency]] and [[Metacognition]].

Several mechanisms could trigger the false familiarity:

- **Similar layouts.** Anne Cleary's experiments in the 2010s showed people new scenes whose spatial layout matched scenes they had seen earlier but forgotten. They reported déjà vu more often for these, suggesting that a forgotten resemblance can produce the feeling; see [[Context-Dependent Memory]].
- **Divided attention.** Glancing at a scene briefly, then looking at it properly a moment later, may make the second look feel like a memory.
- **Neural noise.** A brief misfire in memory regions may produce the feeling with no cause in the scene at all.

Some researchers see déjà vu as a sign of a healthy checking system: the brain detects that the familiarity signal is wrong and flags it. This would explain why people find it strange rather than simply believing they have been there before; see [[Predictive Processing]].

**Jamais vu**, "never seen," is the opposite: something familiar suddenly feels new or strange. Repeating a word over and over until it seems meaningless produces a version of it; Chris Moulin and Akira O'Connor studied this by having people write a common word repeatedly, and many stopped because it began to look wrong; see [[Semantic Satiation]]. Some people with dementia experience **déjà vécu**, a persistent feeling that everything has been lived before, and may refuse to watch TV because they believe they've seen every show.

Déjà vu belongs with other glitches of memory and recognition, such as the frustration of knowing a word without being able to retrieve it, and memories of things that never happened; see [[Tip of the Tongue]] and [[False Memory]]. Each shows that remembering is reconstruction.
