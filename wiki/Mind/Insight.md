**Insight** is the sudden understanding of a problem's solution after a period of being stuck, often experienced as an "aha!" moment. Unlike step-by-step reasoning, where progress is steady, insight problems feel hopeless until the answer appears all at once, usually with a burst of confidence and pleasure.

The story of Archimedes shouting "Eureka!" in his bath is probably legend, but insight is well studied. Gestalt psychologists in the early twentieth century described it as **restructuring**: seeing the problem in a new way, so that elements combine differently; see [[Gestalt Principles]]. Wolfgang Köhler reported chimpanzees that, after failing to reach bananas, suddenly stacked boxes or joined sticks together.

Classic laboratory problems capture the experience:

- **The candle problem.** Karl Duncker (1945) gave people a candle, a box of thumbtacks, and matches, and asked them to attach the candle to a wall. The solution is to empty the box and tack it up as a shelf. People solve it much more often when the tacks are presented outside the box, because seeing the box as a container hides its use as a platform; see [[Functional Fixedness]].
- **The nine-dot problem.** Connecting nine dots in a square with four straight lines requires lines that extend past the square, an option people assume is forbidden.
- **Remote associates.** Finding the word linking three others (pine, crab, sauce: apple) often arrives suddenly.

What blocks insight is usually an assumption the solver doesn't know they're making. Past experience creates a mental set, and a familiar method keeps people from seeing a simpler one, the Einstellung effect. Experts can be especially prone to it in their own field, which is why outsiders sometimes solve problems specialists can't.

**Incubation** is the finding that setting a problem aside often helps. People who take a break, especially doing an undemanding task, solve more insight problems afterward than those who keep working. Explanations include forgetting misleading approaches, and continued unconscious processing while the mind wanders; see [[Mind-Wandering]] and [[Default Mode Network]]. Sleep may help too: in one study, people who slept were more than twice as likely to discover a hidden shortcut in a number task; see [[Memory Consolidation]].

Brain recordings show a burst of activity in the right temporal lobe just before people report an insight, preceded by a quieting of visual input, as if attention turns inward. The feeling of certainty that comes with insight is usually justified, but not always: false insights feel just as compelling; see [[Processing Fluency]].

Practical lessons follow. When stuck, explain the problem aloud or to someone else, which forces hidden assumptions into the open; see [[Rubber Duck Debugging]]. Take a walk. Question the constraints you assumed. And keep a positive, relaxed mood where possible, since anxiety narrows attention onto the obvious approach; see [[Yerkes-Dodson Law]].
