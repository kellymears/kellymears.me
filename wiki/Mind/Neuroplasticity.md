**Neuroplasticity** is the brain's capacity to change its structure and function in response to experience. Every lasting memory, skill, and habit is a physical change somewhere in the nervous system. For much of the twentieth century the adult brain was thought to be largely fixed after childhood. That view has been overturned, though the popular version now overstates how easy change is.

Plasticity works at several scales:

- **Synaptic.** Donald Hebb proposed in 1949 that when one neuron repeatedly helps fire another, the connection between them strengthens, later summarized as "cells that fire together wire together." Tim Bliss and Terje Lømo found a mechanism in 1973, *long-term potentiation*, a lasting increase in synaptic strength after repeated stimulation. Its opposite, long-term depression, weakens connections. Both underlie [[Memory Consolidation]].
- **Structural.** Neurons grow and prune connections. London taxi drivers, who memorize the city's streets, were found in 2000 to have larger posterior hippocampi than average, and the difference grew with years on the job.
- **Map-level.** Brain areas take over neighboring territory. After an amputation, the cortical area for the missing hand may be claimed by the face, so touching the cheek can produce sensations in the phantom hand. Stroke rehabilitation exploits the same tendency by forcing use of the weaker limb.

Plasticity is strongest during **critical periods**. David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel showed in the 1960s that a kitten deprived of sight in one eye early in life never developed normal vision in it, while the same deprivation in an adult cat did little. Language sounds and binocular vision both have such windows. Adult brains remain plastic, but more slowly and selectively.

Change is gated by relevance. Attention, effort, and the dopamine signals behind [[Reward Prediction Error]] tell the brain which experiences are worth encoding, which is why practice with feedback works and passive exposure does little. [[Motor Learning]] and [[Habit Formation]] are plasticity at work, and [[Habituation]] is plasticity in its simplest form.

Not all plasticity helps. In [[Central Sensitization]], pain pathways learn to amplify signals until pain persists without injury. Addiction and [[Rumination]] are also, in part, well-worn circuits.

The commercial version oversells. Brain-training games mostly improve performance on the games themselves, with little transfer to everyday thinking, and in 2016 the maker of Lumosity paid $2 million to settle US Federal Trade Commission charges over claims to the contrary. The reliable route to a changed brain is the slow one: repeated, effortful practice of the actual skill, spread over time with sleep in between, much like [[Supercompensation]] in training.
