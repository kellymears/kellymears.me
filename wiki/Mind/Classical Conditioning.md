**Classical conditioning** is learning by association: a neutral signal that repeatedly comes before something meaningful starts to trigger a response on its own. Ivan Pavlov discovered it in the 1890s while studying digestion in dogs. He noticed the dogs began to salivate before food arrived, at the sight of the assistant who fed them. He then paired food with sounds, including a metronome, and found that after enough pairings the sound alone made the dogs salivate.

The vocabulary comes from his experiments:

- An **unconditioned stimulus** (food) naturally triggers an **unconditioned response** (salivation).
- A **conditioned stimulus** (the sound) starts out neutral.
- After pairing, the conditioned stimulus alone triggers a **conditioned response**.

Several later findings shaped how the process is understood:

- **Extinction.** If the signal keeps appearing without the food, the response fades. But extinction is new learning layered on top, not erasure. The old response can return, which is why relapse is common.
- **Prediction, not just pairing.** Leon Kamin and Robert Rescorla showed that animals learn a signal only when it adds information. A signal paired with food that is already predicted by another cue is barely learned. The 1972 Rescorla-Wagner model formalized this: learning is driven by prediction error, the gap between what was expected and what happened. That idea now underlies [[Reward Prediction Error]] theories of dopamine and [[Predictive Processing]] accounts of the brain.
- **Preparedness.** John Garcia found in the 1960s that rats learn to avoid a taste after one illness, even with hours between eating and sickness. People do the same: one bout of food poisoning can spoil a food for years.

It is distinct from operant conditioning, where behavior is shaped by its consequences, as in [[Variable-Ratio Reinforcement]]. Classical conditioning involves responses that happen to you, such as salivation, fear, or arousal. In practice the two mix, and both feed into [[Habit Formation]], where cues come to trigger routines automatically.

Conditioning is everywhere in daily life:

- **Emotion.** Fear can be conditioned to a place, a sound, or a smell. The 1920 "Little Albert" experiment, now seen as unethical, conditioned an infant to fear a white rat.
- **Body.** Some [[Placebo Effect]] responses are conditioned, as when a body reacts to a pill that resembles a drug taken before, and the [[Nocebo Effect]] can work the same way.
- **Technology.** A notification sound comes to trigger anticipation before anyone looks at the screen.
- **Advertising.** Products are paired with music, attractive people, and good feelings so those feelings transfer.

Therapy uses the mechanism directly. Exposure-based treatments in [[Cognitive Behavioral Therapy]] extinguish conditioned fear by meeting the trigger safely, repeatedly, and in varied settings, since extinction learned in one context doesn't always transfer, as [[Context-Dependent Memory]] predicts.
