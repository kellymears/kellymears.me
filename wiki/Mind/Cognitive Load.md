**Cognitive load** is the demand a task places on [[Working Memory]]. John Sweller's cognitive load theory (1988), developed for instructional design, splits it into kinds that matter because only some of them can be removed:

- **Intrinsic load** is the inherent complexity of the material: how many elements must be held and related at once. Long division has more than addition. It can be sequenced but not eliminated.
- **Extraneous load** is the cost added by *presentation*: a diagram whose labels sit in a separate legend, instructions split across pages, inconsistent terminology, jargon. It does nothing for understanding, so it is pure waste.
- **Germane load** (a later and more debated addition) is the effort spent building lasting mental structures, the useful kind of struggle.

The practical rule is to cut extraneous load so the limited budget goes to intrinsic load. Several findings follow. The **split-attention effect**: integrating labels into a diagram beats a separate key. **Worked examples** beat problem-solving for novices, because searching for a method eats the capacity needed to learn it. And the **expertise-reversal effect** cuts the other way: scaffolding that helps novices slows experts, who already hold the structure, so the right design depends on who is reading.

The theory generalizes well beyond classrooms. In interface design it explains why [[Hick's Law]] and [[Progressive Disclosure]] work, and why consistency in a [[Design System]] is a cognitive feature and not just an aesthetic one. In code it is the case for small functions, good [[Naming]], and [[Plain Language]] in docs and error messages. In operations it explains why an incident goes worse when the runbook is ambiguous: the responder's working memory is already full of the incident.

"Mental load" has also come to mean the ongoing, invisible work of tracking a household's or team's open loops: remembering, anticipating, scheduling. It is the same mechanism stretched over weeks instead of seconds. That load drains [[Executive Function]] whether or not anyone can see it, and when unevenly shared it is a real cost that never shows up in a task list.
