**Dyscalculia** is a specific learning difference in understanding numbers and arithmetic. People with it struggle to grasp quantities, learn number facts, and follow calculations, despite ordinary intelligence and teaching. Estimates put it at 3 to 7 percent of people, roughly as common as [[Dyslexia]], but it is far less recognized, and many adults with it have never been assessed.

Common signs:

- Counting on fingers long after classmates stop.
- Difficulty judging which of two numbers is bigger, or estimating a quantity.
- Trouble remembering basic facts like multiplication tables.
- Problems with everyday numbers: telling time on clocks, handling money, reading timetables, or following a score.
- Losing track partway through multi-step procedures.

Research points to a weakness in **number sense**, an intuitive grasp of quantity that humans share with many animals and that infants show before learning to count. Most people can see at a glance whether a group holds one, two, three, or four items without counting, a skill called **subitizing**, and can roughly compare larger collections. Many people with dyscalculia are slower and less accurate at these basic tasks. Brain imaging links number processing to the parietal lobes, and some studies find differences there, though no single cause is established.

Dyscalculia often overlaps with other conditions. It co-occurs with dyslexia and with [[ADHD]] more than chance would predict, and difficulties with [[Working Memory]] can make multi-step calculation harder by making it hard to hold intermediate results.

It is distinct from **math anxiety**, the fear and tension many people feel when doing math, though the two feed each other. Anxiety uses up the mental resources calculation needs, so anxious people perform worse, which confirms their fear; see [[Choking Under Pressure]] and [[Yerkes-Dodson Law]]. Children who repeatedly fail in math class often develop anxiety on top of the underlying difficulty, and come to believe they are "not a math person"; see [[Growth Mindset]] and [[Self-Efficacy]].

Support works best when it starts early and builds understanding rather than drilling procedures:

- **Concrete materials.** Blocks, number lines, and visual models that make quantities physical; see [[Embodied Cognition]].
- **Explicit teaching** of strategies others pick up without instruction, such as breaking numbers into tens.
- **Accommodations** such as calculators, extra time, and reference sheets, which separate reasoning from fact recall.
- **Tools in daily life**, from phone calculators to apps that track budgets and time, which offload the hardest parts; see [[Extended Mind]].

Advocates frame dyscalculia as part of the range of human cognition rather than a defect, while noting that a society that runs on numbers imposes real costs on people who find them hard, from missed appointments to financial mistakes; see [[Neurodiversity]]. Time can be affected too, since judging durations draws on quantity sense; see [[Time Blindness]].
