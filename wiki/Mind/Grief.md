**Grief** is the response to losing someone or something one is attached to, most often a death. It includes emotions such as sadness, yearning, anger, and guilt, along with physical effects such as poor sleep, fatigue, and appetite changes. Many familiar ideas about it don't match the evidence.

The best-known model is the **five stages**: denial, anger, bargaining, depression, and acceptance, described by the psychiatrist Elisabeth Kübler-Ross in her 1969 book *On Death and Dying*. They came from interviews with dying patients about facing their own deaths, not with bereaved people. Kübler-Ross herself later said the stages were not meant as a fixed sequence. Studies of bereaved people find that acceptance is often the most common response from the start, and yearning peaks within a few months, with no reliable order. The model can be harmful when people believe they are grieving incorrectly because they skipped a stage.

Research has produced better accounts:

- **Resilience is common.** The psychologist George Bonanno found that most bereaved people show a pattern of resilience, with relatively stable functioning and distress that eases within months. Lack of visible grief is not usually suppression.
- **Oscillation.** The dual process model of Margaret Stroebe and Henk Schut describes coping as moving back and forth between facing the loss, through remembering and crying, and restoration, attending to daily life and new roles. Taking breaks from grief is healthy, not avoidance.
- **Continuing bonds.** Rather than letting go, many people keep a relationship with the person who died, through memories, rituals, conversation, and objects, and this is often adaptive; see [[Episodic Memory]] and [[Rite of Passage]].

Grief can also become stuck. **Prolonged grief disorder**, in which intense yearning and preoccupation persist and impair life long after the loss, was added to the World Health Organization's diagnostic manual in 2019 and to the American psychiatric manual in 2022. It affects a minority of the bereaved. Treatments adapted from cognitive therapy, including gradual approach to avoided reminders, have shown benefit; see [[Cognitive Behavioral Therapy]] and [[Exposure Therapy]]. Repetitive brooding about the circumstances of a death predicts worse outcomes; see [[Rumination]].

Bereavement is a physical stress. The risk of heart attack rises in the days after a loved one's death, and a temporary heart weakness called broken heart syndrome is often triggered by acute emotional stress; see [[Allostatic Load]].

Grief follows the loss of anything one is bonded to: a pet, a relationship, a home, health, or an expected future. Its intensity tends to track the depth of attachment; see [[Attachment Theory]]. Expected losses are often imagined as worse and longer than they turn out, though for some they are not; see [[Affective Forecasting]].

Kindness toward oneself and others' presence help, rather than pressure to move on; see [[Self-Compassion]] and [[Loneliness]].
