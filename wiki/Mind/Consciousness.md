**Consciousness** is subjective experience: the redness of red, the sting of a cut, the sense of being someone looking out at the world. Philosopher Thomas Nagel put it in a 1974 essay asking "What is it like to be a bat?" An organism is conscious, in that sense, if there is something it is like to be it. The individual felt qualities of experience are often called **qualia**.

In 1995 philosopher David Chalmers separated the "easy problems" of consciousness from the **hard problem**. The easy problems, which are still very difficult, concern functions: how the brain discriminates stimuli, integrates information, directs attention and reports its states. The hard problem asks why any of that processing is accompanied by experience at all. Chalmers illustrated it with the **philosophical zombie**, a hypothetical being physically identical to a person, behaving the same way, with no inner experience. If such a thing is even conceivable, the argument runs, explaining the functions leaves something out. Many philosophers and scientists reject the argument, holding that the hard problem will dissolve once the easy problems are solved.

Meanwhile neuroscience studies the neural correlates of consciousness, the brain activity that differs between seeing something and not seeing it while the stimulus stays the same. Useful cases come from the edges of awareness. In [[Multistable Perception]], the input is fixed while experience flips. In [[Blindsight]], people with damage to visual cortex can point to objects they insist they cannot see. [[Inattentional Blindness]] and [[Hemispatial Neglect]] show how much reaches the brain without reaching experience. [[Dreaming]] is vivid experience with almost no sensory input.

Two theories dominate the debate:

- **Global workspace theory**, proposed by Bernard Baars and developed by Stanislas Dehaene, holds that information becomes conscious when it is broadcast widely across the brain, making it available to memory, speech and planning at once.
- **Integrated information theory**, from Giulio Tononi, holds that consciousness is the degree to which a system's parts form an integrated, irreducible whole, a measure that would apply to some machines and not to others regardless of behavior.

A large adversarial collaboration published results in 2023 that challenged predictions of both, and the same year more than a hundred researchers signed a letter calling integrated information theory pseudoscience, which prompted a sharp counter-response. The field remains unsettled. [[Predictive Processing]] offers a third approach, treating experience as the brain's best guess about the causes of its input.

The question has become practical with [[Large Language Model]] systems that talk fluently about their inner lives. Behavior alone cannot settle whether anything is felt, the point of the Chinese room argument; see [[Turing Test]]. Related puzzles about who is doing the doing appear under [[Sense of Agency]].
