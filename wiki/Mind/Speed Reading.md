**Speed reading** is a family of techniques that claim to multiply reading speed, often to over a thousand words a minute, without losing comprehension. It has been sold in courses and apps since the 1950s. Research on how reading works has consistently found that the claim doesn't hold: past a modest point, faster reading means understanding less.

Typical adults read ordinary prose at around 200 to 300 words a minute. The eyes don't glide along a line; they jump in quick movements called saccades and pause for about a quarter of a second on most words, taking in only a few letters clearly on each side of the fixation point; see [[Eye Tracking]]. Speed reading courses target these mechanics:

- **Reducing fixations** by taking in more words per pause, said to widen the "visual span."
- **Eliminating regressions**, the backward jumps readers make to recheck earlier words.
- **Suppressing subvocalization**, the silent pronunciation many readers hear in their heads, which is said to cap speed at talking pace; see [[Inner Speech]].
- **Pacing**, sweeping a finger or pointer down the page to drive the eyes faster.

A 2016 review by Keith Rayner and colleagues found little support for any of these. The visual span is limited by how sharp vision is outside the center of gaze, and training doesn't widen it much. Regressions are often needed to fix misreadings, and readers prevented from making them understand less. Inner speech appears to help hold words in [[Working Memory]] while sentences are assembled, and suppressing it hurts comprehension of anything demanding. The real limit is language processing, recognizing words and building meaning, not the eyes.

Apps that flash words one at a time at a fixed spot, so the eyes never move, run into the same limit. They remove the ability to look back or pause at hard passages, and comprehension drops as speed rises; see [[Attentional Blink]].

What speed readers actually do, when tested, resembles **skimming**: they get the gist of easy material and miss detail. Skimming is a real skill, and good readers adjust speed to purpose, slowing for difficult passages and skipping what doesn't matter. People who read a lot do read faster, mostly because they recognize more words instantly and know more about the subject, which makes text predictable; see [[Expert Intuition]] and [[Predictive Processing]].

The evidence suggests better ways to get through more text: read more to build vocabulary and background knowledge, decide what deserves close reading, and use headings and summaries to skim the rest. Typography helps a little too, with comfortable line lengths reducing wasted eye movements; see [[Line Length]].

Speed reading persists partly because people judge their own understanding poorly. Fast reading feels productive, and readers overestimate how much they took in; see [[Metacognition]] and [[Processing Fluency]].
