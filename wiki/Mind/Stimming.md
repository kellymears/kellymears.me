**Stimming**, short for self-stimulatory behavior, is a repetitive movement, sound, or sensory action done for its own effect rather than to accomplish something: rocking, hand-flapping, spinning, tapping, humming, repeating words, rubbing a texture, chewing. Nearly everyone stims in mild forms, such as clicking a pen, bouncing a leg, twirling hair, or pacing on a phone call. For many autistic and ADHD people, stims are more frequent, more visible, and more important.

Stims do several jobs:

- **Regulating sensory input.** A predictable, self-generated sensation can mask or anchor against overwhelming input during [[Sensory Overload]], or supply input when there is too little, through the body's own [[Proprioception]] and [[Vestibular System]] channels.
- **Managing emotion.** Many people stim more when anxious, excited, or happy. Flapping hands can simply be what joy looks like for an autistic person.
- **Supporting attention.** Some research on ADHD finds that more movement accompanies *better* performance on demanding [[Working Memory]] tasks, consistent with the idea that movement raises arousal to a workable level. Fidgeting while listening can be how someone pays attention, not a sign that they aren't.

Stimming has been treated very differently over time. Older behavioral therapies often targeted visible stims for elimination, teaching "quiet hands" so autistic children would look typical. Autistic adults have argued strongly against this. Suppressing a harmless regulatory behavior removes a coping tool and teaches [[Masking]], with its costs. The current consensus among advocates and many clinicians is to accept harmless stims and to redirect only those that cause injury or real harm. Self-injury is a separate problem, usually a sign of overwhelming distress, and is addressed by addressing the distress.

The commercial fidget toy boom of the late 2010s made stimming mainstream almost by accident: spinners, cubes, and putty sold to everyone, and a behavior once pathologized became a desk accessory. That is a small instance of the [[Neurodiversity]] argument. Traits that look like deficits in one frame are ordinary human variation in another, and design that accommodates them, such as fidget-friendly classrooms and meetings where moving around is fine, costs very little.
