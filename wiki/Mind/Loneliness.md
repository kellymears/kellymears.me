**Loneliness** is the distressing feeling that one's social relationships fall short of what one wants, in number or in closeness. It is different from **social isolation**, the objective state of having few contacts. A person can live alone contentedly, or feel lonely in a crowd or a marriage. The two overlap but are measured separately, and each predicts outcomes the other does not. Loneliness is common at every age, with peaks often reported in young adulthood and late life.

The psychologist John Cacioppo proposed an evolutionary account. For a social species, being cut off from the group was dangerous, so loneliness evolved as an aversive signal, like hunger or thirst, motivating people to restore connection. Like hunger, it is useful when brief and harmful when it persists.

Persistent loneliness changes how people perceive others. Cacioppo's research suggested that lonely people become more alert to social threat: they notice rejection more quickly, interpret ambiguous behavior more negatively, and expect to be rebuffed; see [[Negativity Bias]] and [[Rejection Sensitivity]]. These expectations can make interactions more guarded and less rewarding, confirming the feeling and deepening it. Comparing oneself with others' visible social lives can add to it; see [[Social Comparison]].

Loneliness and isolation are associated with poorer health. A 2010 meta-analysis of 148 studies found that people with stronger social relationships had about 50 percent greater odds of survival over the study periods, a comparison often summarized by likening isolation to well-known risks such as smoking. Associations include heart disease, depression, poor sleep, and dementia. Proposed mechanisms include chronic stress responses and inflammation; see [[Allostatic Load]]. Since these come mostly from observational data, some of the association reflects illness causing isolation rather than the reverse; see [[Observational Study]].

Governments have taken notice. The United Kingdom appointed a minister for loneliness in 2018, and the US Surgeon General issued an advisory on loneliness and isolation in 2023. Whether loneliness is rising overall is debated, but survey data show Americans, especially young people, spending less time with friends in person than in previous decades.

Interventions take several forms: increasing contact, building social skills, providing support, and changing the negative expectations that sustain loneliness. A 2011 meta-analysis found that the last kind, addressing patterns of thought, was the most effective; see [[Cognitive Behavioral Therapy]].

Shared spaces where people meet repeatedly and informally support connection; see [[Third Place]]. One-sided bonds with media figures can ease loneliness or substitute for reciprocal ones; see [[Parasocial Relationship]]. Working alongside others, even silently, offers a lighter form of company; see [[Body Doubling]].

There are limits to how many close relationships people can maintain, and loneliness depends more on the quality of a few than on the number; see [[Dunbar's Number]] and [[Attachment Theory]].
