**Savoring** is the practice of noticing, appreciating, and extending positive experiences. The psychologists Fred Bryant and Joseph Veroff developed the concept in their 2007 book *Savoring*, arguing that how people attend to good things matters as much for wellbeing as how many good things happen to them.

Savoring can face three directions in time:

- **Anticipation**: enjoying something before it happens. Planning a trip often brings as much pleasure as the trip itself.
- **The present**: attending fully to an experience as it happens, rather than through a phone or with half a mind elsewhere; see [[Mindfulness]].
- **Reminiscence**: revisiting good memories, alone or with others; see [[Nostalgia]] and [[Episodic Memory]].

Strategies that research associates with more savoring:

- **Sharing.** Telling others about good news, or experiencing something together, amplifies it.
- **Building memories.** Pausing to take a mental snapshot of a moment, or keeping a souvenir.
- **Absorption.** Letting attention sink fully into an experience; see [[Flow]].
- **Marking the moment as fleeting.** Recognizing that something won't last, such as the last weeks of a year abroad, can increase how much people appreciate it.
- **Comparing downward**, to times when things were worse.

The opposite habit, **killjoy thinking**, dwells on what could be better, what's wrong, or how something will soon end badly. A tendency to weigh bad events more heavily makes it a common default; see [[Negativity Bias]].

Savoring works against adaptation. People get used to good things quickly, so a new pleasure fades into the background; see [[Hedonic Treadmill]] and [[Habituation]]. Some studies suggest ways to slow this:

- **Interruption.** A 2009 study by Leif Nelson and colleagues found that people enjoyed television programs more when commercials broke them up, apparently because breaks reset adaptation.
- **Abstention.** People who gave up chocolate for a week enjoyed it more afterward than people who ate it freely.
- **Variety** in how and when a pleasure is enjoyed.

A wandering mind is linked to lower moment-to-moment happiness, even when it wanders to pleasant topics, which suggests that attention itself is part of enjoyment; see [[Mind-Wandering]]. Habitual scrolling crowds out savoring by filling idle moments; see [[Doomscrolling]].

Savoring also shapes memory. People judge past experiences largely by their most intense moment and their ending, so lingering over a high point and closing on a good note shape how an experience is remembered; see [[Peak-End Rule]]. People are also poor at predicting how much future events will affect them, which makes anticipation a mixed guide; see [[Affective Forecasting]].

Savoring isn't forced positivity. It doesn't deny bad experiences; it gives good ones their full weight. Some researchers link it to the experience of vastness and wonder; see [[Awe]].
