**Affective forecasting** is predicting how we will feel about future events: how happy a promotion will make us, how bad a breakup will be, how long either will last. Psychologists Timothy Wilson and Daniel Gilbert began studying it in the 1990s and found that people are usually right about whether an event will feel good or bad, but consistently wrong about how much and for how long.

The main error is the **impact bias**: people overestimate the intensity and duration of their emotional reactions. Assistant professors predicted that being denied tenure would make them unhappy for years; those actually denied were about as happy a few years later as those who got it. Voters predicted lasting gloom if their candidate lost, and recovered within weeks. The pattern holds for good events too, which fade faster than expected; see [[Hedonic Treadmill]].

Several causes have been identified:

- **Immune neglect.** People don't anticipate their own ability to cope. After setbacks, the mind finds silver linings, reframes, and moves attention elsewhere, a "psychological immune system" that forecasts leave out; see [[Emotional Regulation]].
- **The focusing illusion.** Thinking about one aspect of life makes it seem more important than it is. Daniel Kahneman's summary: "Nothing in life is as important as you think it is while you are thinking about it." People believed Californians were happier than Midwesterners because of the weather, but the two groups reported similar satisfaction.
- **Empathy gaps.** People in one state misjudge how they'll feel or act in another. A full shopper underestimates future hunger, and someone calm underestimates how strong a craving or fear will feel; see [[Hyperbolic Discounting]].
- **Memory errors.** Forecasts draw on remembered experiences, and memory over-weights the most intense moment and the end; see [[Peak-End Rule]].

Forecasting errors shape real decisions. People choose jobs, homes, and purchases by imagined feelings, so they overpay for things whose pleasure fades and avoid risks whose pain would fade too. They also misjudge which choices they'll regret: people expect regret about actions more than inactions, while in the long run they tend to regret what they didn't do.

A practical correction is **surrogation**: ask someone who has gone through the event how they feel, rather than imagining it. In one study, people predicted their enjoyment of a date more accurately from a stranger's report than from a description of the date itself, yet most participants preferred the description. Base rates from other people beat vivid imagination, as in many other predictions; see [[Base Rate Fallacy]].

Affective forecasting matters for anxiety too. Imagining a feared event as overwhelming and permanent feeds avoidance, and therapies work partly by testing those predictions against what actually happens; see [[Cognitive Behavioral Therapy]] and [[Rumination]].
