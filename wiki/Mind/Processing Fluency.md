**Processing fluency** is the subjective ease with which information is perceived and understood. A statement in clear type, a name that is easy to pronounce, and an idea heard before all feel fluent. The mind uses that feeling as a quick signal, part of the fast system described under [[Dual Process Theory]], and tends to read ease as meaning something: that the thing is true, familiar, good, or understood. Often the inference is reasonable. Sometimes it badly misleads.

Several well-known effects are fluency at work:

- **[[Illusory Truth Effect|Illusory truth]].** Repeated statements are rated more likely to be true than new ones, even when people know better. Lynn Hasher and colleagues showed this in 1977, and it holds for headlines, rumors, and advertising slogans. Repetition makes a claim easier to process, and easy feels true.
- **Mere exposure.** Robert Zajonc showed in 1968 that people come to like shapes, words, and faces simply from seeing them more often, even when they do not remember having seen them.
- **Rhyme as reason.** Rhyming aphorisms, such as "woes unite foes", are judged more accurate than non-rhyming versions with the same meaning.
- **Easy names.** A 2006 study found that shares of companies with easily pronounced names outperformed others shortly after listing.
- **Beauty as usability.** Attractive, well-typeset interfaces are judged easier to use, as described under [[Aesthetic-Usability Effect]].

Fluency is especially treacherous in learning. Rereading notes or watching a clear explanation feels smooth, which creates an **illusion of competence**: the material feels known because it is familiar, not because it can be recalled or applied. Retrieval practice, as in [[Spaced Repetition]], feels harder and works better, the central finding behind [[Desirable Difficulty]]. Judging one's own learning accurately is a [[Metacognition]] skill that fluency undermines.

A related trap is the **illusion of explanatory depth**. In 2002 Leonid Rozenblit and Frank Keil asked people how well they understood everyday devices such as zippers and flush toilets. Ratings were high until people were asked to write out a step-by-step explanation, after which their ratings dropped sharply. Familiarity had felt like understanding, which helps explain the [[Curse of Knowledge]] in reverse and the confident ignorance described by the [[Dunning-Kruger Effect]].

Some fluency findings have not held up. A widely cited 2011 claim that hard-to-read fonts improve learning by forcing deeper processing failed to replicate in larger studies, one of many small effects caught up in the [[Replication Crisis]].

The practical lessons run in two directions. When communicating, fluency helps: plain language, clear type, and familiar structure make messages easier to accept. When evaluating, fluency is a warning sign. A claim that feels obviously true may just be familiar, an error that compounds with the [[Availability Heuristic]] and [[Confirmation Bias]].
