**Embodied cognition** is the view that the mind is shaped by the body: that thought depends on perception, movement, and physical experience, rather than being abstract symbol processing that could in principle run on any hardware. It challenged the picture of the mind as a computer that dominated cognitive science from the 1950s.

The idea grew from several sources in the 1980s and 1990s. In *The Embodied Mind* (1991), Francisco Varela, Evan Thompson, and Eleanor Rosch drew on phenomenology and Buddhist meditation to argue that cognition arises from a living body interacting with its surroundings. George Lakoff and Mark Johnson argued that abstract concepts are built from bodily metaphors, such as understanding importance as size or affection as warmth; see [[Conceptual Metaphor]]. In robotics, Rodney Brooks built machines that behaved intelligently by reacting directly to sensors, without building internal models of the world.

The broader family is sometimes called **4E cognition**: embodied, embedded in an environment, enacted through action, and extended into tools; see [[Extended Mind]].

Evidence comes from several directions:

- **Gesture.** People gesture even when nobody can see them, as on the phone, and children who gesture while explaining math problems learn faster. Gesture seems to help thinking, not only communication.
- **Action and perception.** Players of *Tetris* rotate pieces on screen to see whether they fit rather than imagining the rotation, using the world as a faster scratchpad than [[Working Memory]].
- **Simulation.** Reading action words activates brain regions involved in performing those actions, suggesting that understanding involves partly reenacting.
- **Bodily signals.** Emotions depend on awareness of heart rate, breathing, and gut sensations; see [[Interoception]] and [[Predictive Processing]].

Skilled movement is a clear example: much of what an expert knows lives in trained bodily patterns rather than explicit rules; see [[Motor Learning]] and [[Expert Intuition]]. Balance and spatial sense also shape reasoning about space; see [[Proprioception]].

Some of the most publicized findings did not hold up. Studies claiming that holding a warm drink made people judge strangers as warmer, or that standing in a "power pose" raised hormones and confidence, failed to replicate in larger studies; see [[Replication Crisis]] and [[Priming]]. Critics argue that the stronger versions of the theory are vague and hard to test, while the weaker versions are hard to dispute.

The debate has returned with language models. Systems trained only on text have no body or senses, yet they handle many concepts competently, which some take as evidence that much of meaning can be learned from language alone; others argue that the models' gaps, such as weak physical reasoning, show what embodiment provides; see [[Large Language Model]]. Questions about whether thinking requires an inner voice raise related issues; see [[Inner Speech]].
