**Flow** is the state of complete absorption in an activity, where action seems to follow itself, self-consciousness drops away, and time runs strangely, usually faster. The psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi described it in the 1970s from interviews with climbers, chess players, surgeons, and artists, who described the same experience in very different domains.

His work names the conditions that tend to produce flow:

- **Challenge matched to skill.** Too easy and the mind wanders into boredom. Too hard and it tips into anxiety. Flow lives in the band between, and the band moves as skill grows.
- **Clear goals.** At each moment you know what you are trying to do next.
- **Immediate feedback.** You can tell right away whether the last action worked.
- **Protection from interruption,** because [[Attention Residue]] and flow do not coexist.

Those conditions read like a design specification, and games have taken them as one. A well-tuned [[Difficulty Curve]] keeps the player in the band as their skill climbs, [[Game Feel]] provides the immediate feedback, and dynamic difficulty adjusts the challenge in real time. Good tools try for the same thing: a fast build and test loop gives the immediate feedback that lets programming become absorbing. A slow one breaks every attempt into fragments.

Flow is not automatically good. It is indifferent to what it attaches to, and a [[Compulsion Loop]] can produce something flow-like with no growth in skill at all, just a schedule of [[Variable-Ratio Reinforcement|variable rewards]]. The distinction is whether the challenge is real and the person is getting better. Flow also differs from [[Hyperfocus]]. Flow depends on the activity's structure and tends to feel restorative. Hyperfocus depends on the person's attention, is often hard to leave or direct, and can leave someone depleted, having missed meals and appointments. The two can look the same from outside, and the second sometimes gets mistaken for the first.
