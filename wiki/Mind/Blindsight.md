**Blindsight** is the ability of some people with damage to the primary visual cortex to respond to visual stimuli they report not seeing. Asked to point to a light, guess the direction of movement, or say whether a line is vertical or horizontal, they insist they are guessing, yet they perform far better than chance. It shows that seeing and being aware of seeing can come apart.

The primary visual cortex, at the back of the brain, is the main entry point for signals from the eyes. Damage to it on one side causes blindness in the opposite half of the visual field. In the 1970s the psychologist Lawrence Weiskrantz and colleagues studied a patient known as DB, who had part of this region removed to treat an abnormal tangle of blood vessels. DB described seeing nothing in the affected area but could locate objects and distinguish shapes there with high accuracy. Weiskrantz named the phenomenon. Later patients could also avoid obstacles: a man known as TN, blind after two strokes, walked down a cluttered corridor without hitting anything while saying he saw nothing.

Some patients report a vague sense that something happened, without visual experience, called type 2 blindsight; others report no awareness at all. Some can sense the emotional expression of faces in the blind field, which suggests routes that bypass the visual cortex reach regions that process emotion.

The likely explanation is that the visual cortex isn't the only path. Signals from the eye also reach older structures in the midbrain and thalamus, which connect to other visual areas and guide eye and body movements. These routes appear to support behavior without generating conscious experience, consistent with theories that split fast automatic processing from slow deliberate processing; see [[Dual Process Theory]].

Blindsight matters for questions about consciousness. If the brain can process and act on visual information without awareness, then awareness is not required for that processing, and the question becomes what awareness adds. Researchers compare conditions where the same stimulus is seen or unseen, a method also used with masking and attention; see [[Inattentional Blindness]] and [[Attentional Blink]].

It also raises questions of measurement. Patients' confidence often doesn't track their accuracy, a gap in self-monitoring; see [[Metacognition]]. Skeptics have argued that some cases reflect residual islands of working cortex or lowered response criteria rather than unconscious vision, and careful studies control for these.

Other conditions show the same kind of dissociation between processing and awareness. People with face blindness may show physical responses to familiar faces they can't consciously recognize; see [[Prosopagnosia]]. Neglect patients ignore half of space they can technically see. And healthy people respond to subliminal primes they never noticed; see [[Priming]].
