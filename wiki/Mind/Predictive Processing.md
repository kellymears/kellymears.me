**Predictive processing** is a theory of how the brain works: rather than building perception upward from raw sensory data, it continually predicts what its senses are about to report and mainly processes the difference, the *prediction error*. Perception, on this view, is the brain's best guess about what is causing its input, corrected wherever the guess goes wrong. The neuroscientist Anil Seth calls it a "controlled hallucination."

The idea has deep roots. Hermann von Helmholtz described perception as "unconscious inference" in the 1860s. Rajesh Rao and Dana Ballard's 1999 model of the visual cortex showed how layered predictions and errors could work in neural circuits, and Karl Friston generalized it into the free energy principle. The philosopher Andy Clark, also known for the [[Extended Mind]], brought it to a wide audience with *Surfing Uncertainty* (2016).

A key ingredient is **precision**: how much weight the brain gives prediction errors compared with its existing expectations. Errors from reliable signals get attention and update the model. Errors from noisy signals are discounted. In this framing, attention is the process of setting precision, which links it to [[Selective Attention]].

The framework explains a range of familiar effects:

- **Illusions and mishearings.** Strong expectations override input, so we hear lyrics we expect, as with [[Mondegreen]], and see meaning a film edit implies, as with the [[Kuleshov Effect]].
- **Missing the unexpected.** What the model doesn't predict and attention doesn't weight can go unseen, as in [[Inattentional Blindness]].
- **Placebo and pain.** Expectations shape how much something hurts, which is part of why the [[Placebo Effect]] and [[Nocebo Effect]] work, and why [[Nociception]] and felt pain can diverge.
- **The body.** Applied inward, prediction underlies [[Interoception]]: the brain predicts heartbeat, hunger, and temperature and acts to keep them in range.
- **Learning from surprise.** Dopamine's [[Reward Prediction Error]] signal is a special case: learning driven by the gap between expected and actual reward.

Predictive accounts of autism propose that perception there relies less on prior expectations, the priors of [[Bayes' Theorem]], and gives more weight to incoming detail. That would make the world feel less predictable and more intense, plausibly contributing to [[Sensory Overload]] and to the comfort found in routine and sameness. Researchers still debate whether that is right.

Critics argue the framework is so flexible it risks explaining everything and predicting nothing, the worry raised by [[Falsifiability]]. The analogy to machine learning is close, though: a [[Large Language Model]] is trained only to predict the next token, and much of what it does emerges from that one objective.
