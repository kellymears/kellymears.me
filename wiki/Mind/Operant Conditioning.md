**Operant conditioning** is learning from consequences. A behavior followed by something good tends to be repeated, and a behavior followed by something bad tends to fade. Where [[Classical Conditioning]] links a signal to a response that simply happens, operant conditioning concerns actions an animal or person chooses to perform, and how their payoffs change how often they are chosen.

The idea starts with Edward Thorndike, who in the 1890s put cats in puzzle boxes and timed how long they took to escape. The escapes got faster as accidental successes were repeated, and Thorndike summed it up as the **law of effect**. B. F. Skinner built that into a full research program from the 1930s on. The operant chamber Skinner designed, which others nicknamed the **Skinner box**, let a rat press a lever or a pigeon peck a key and recorded every response automatically.

Skinner's vocabulary is still the standard one, and it trips people up because positive and negative mean adding and removing, not good and bad:

- **Positive reinforcement** adds something wanted, such as food after a lever press.
- **Negative reinforcement** removes something unwanted, as when buckling a seatbelt silences a chime. Both make the behavior more likely.
- **Punishment** makes a behavior less likely, either by adding something unpleasant or by taking something valued away.
- **Extinction** is what happens when a reinforced behavior stops paying off. It usually fades, often after a brief burst of more effort.

The timing of rewards matters as much as their size. A **reinforcement schedule** sets which responses pay off, and unpredictable payouts produce the most persistent behavior of all; see [[Variable-Ratio Reinforcement]]. Complex behavior can be built by **shaping**, rewarding closer and closer approximations of a target, which is how animal trainers teach tricks no animal would perform by chance. Skinner also reported pigeons developing odd rituals when food arrived on a timer regardless of what they did, a study Skinner presented as a model of superstition.

The modern account adds a brain mechanism. Dopamine neurons respond to rewards that are better than expected, and that signal is thought to strengthen the actions that preceded them; see [[Reward Prediction Error]]. Repeated often enough in a stable setting, rewarded actions become cue-driven routines; see [[Habit Formation]].

The principles are everywhere in designed environments. Points, streaks and badges in [[Gamification]] are reinforcers, and some interfaces deliberately exploit the schedules; see [[Dark Pattern]]. Rewards can also backfire. Paying people for something they already enjoyed can lower their interest once the payments stop, the [[Overjustification effect]].

Operant work also produced [[Learned Helplessness]], found when animals given unavoidable shocks later failed to escape avoidable ones.
