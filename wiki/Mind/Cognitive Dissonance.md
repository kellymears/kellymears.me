**Cognitive dissonance** is the discomfort of holding two ideas that conflict, or of acting against what you believe. The social psychologist Leon Festinger proposed the theory in 1957. Its central claim is that people are strongly motivated to reduce dissonance, and that the easiest way is often to change a belief to fit what they have already done rather than the other way around.

Festinger's first evidence came from a doomsday group. In *When Prophecy Fails* (1956), he and colleagues joined a small group whose leader predicted the world would be destroyed on December 21, 1954, and that believers would be rescued by a flying saucer. When nothing happened, the most committed members didn't give up the belief. They decided their faith had saved the world and began seeking converts for the first time. Having given up jobs and possessions, admitting the error would have cost too much.

Experiments followed:

- **Insufficient justification.** In Festinger and Carlsmith's 1959 study, participants did an extremely boring task, then were paid either $1 or $20 to tell the next person it was enjoyable. Afterward, those paid $1 rated the task as more enjoyable. Twenty dollars justified the lie, while a dollar didn't, so those paid a dollar had to change their minds instead.
- **Effort justification.** People who endure a harsh initiation to join a group value the group more. The effort has to have been worth it, part of why hazing persists and why teams defend tools they struggled to adopt, a contributor to [[Not Invented Here]].
- **Post-decision dissonance.** After a hard choice, people rate the chosen option higher and the rejected one lower than before, spreading the alternatives apart.
- **Ben Franklin effect.** Doing someone a favor makes you like them more, since you would hardly help someone you disliked.

Dissonance fuels other biases. It makes contradicting evidence feel aversive, which drives [[Confirmation Bias]]. It makes abandoning an investment feel like admitting a mistake, the engine of the [[Sunk Cost Fallacy]]. Group-level versions show up in [[Groupthink]], and [[Terror Management Theory]] can be read as a large-scale case, with worldviews defended against the dissonance of mortality. When challenged, people can also retreat to a defensible claim and return to the bolder one later, as with [[Motte and Bailey]].

The idea has also lent its name elsewhere. [[Ludonarrative Dissonance]] describes a game whose story says one thing while its mechanics reward another. Some autistic people describe [[Masking]] as a chronic form of dissonance, a constant gap between inner experience and performed behavior.

Some classic findings have replicated less cleanly than textbooks suggest, but the core observation, that people justify what they have already done, has held up.
