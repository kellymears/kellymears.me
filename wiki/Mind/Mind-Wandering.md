**Mind-wandering** is the drift of attention from the task at hand to thoughts unrelated to it: memories, plans, worries, fantasies. Everyone does it, constantly. In a 2010 study, Matthew Killingsworth and Daniel Gilbert used a phone app to ask people at random moments what they were doing and thinking. People's minds were wandering in about 47% of samples, and they were less happy while wandering than while focused, even when the thoughts were pleasant.

The research, led by Jonathan Smallwood and Jonathan Schooler among others, distinguishes two forms. People sometimes notice they've drifted, but often they don't: they **zone out**, reading several pages without taking any of it in, a phenomenon called *mindless reading*. Catching oneself depends on meta-awareness, the ability to monitor one's own thinking; see [[Metacognition]].

Mind-wandering is linked to the brain's [[Default Mode Network]], which becomes active when attention isn't directed at the outside world. Unfinished goals are a common source: thoughts drift toward whatever is still open; see [[Zeigarnik Effect]].

Its costs are clear:

- **Performance.** Wandering minds miss information, make errors in reading comprehension and sustained tasks, and do worse on tests; see [[Vigilance Decrement]].
- **Mood.** Wandering toward problems can slide into repetitive negative thinking; see [[Rumination]].
- **Safety.** Driving while lost in thought is a common factor in accidents.

But it isn't simply a failure:

- **Planning.** Much wandering is about the future, rehearsing what to do next.
- **Creativity.** In a 2012 study by Benjamin Baird and colleagues, people who did an undemanding task during a break came up with more uses for common objects than those who rested or did a demanding task. Letting the mind roam can loosen fixation on a first idea; see [[Functional Fixedness]].
- **Relief.** A wandering mind can make dull tasks bearable; see [[Boredom]].

The balance depends on context. During demanding work, wandering costs a lot; during a walk or a shower, it's often where solutions show up. Tasks that are too easy invite wandering, and so do tasks that are too hard; see [[Yerkes-Dodson Law]].

People with [[ADHD]] report more frequent and less controllable mind-wandering, especially the spontaneous kind, while the same tendency can sit alongside intense, absorbing focus; see [[Hyperfocus]]. Deliberate wandering, chosen and enjoyed, seems to differ from the unwanted kind.

Several practices reduce unwanted wandering. Meditation trains noticing the drift and returning, which is less about stopping thought than about catching it sooner; see [[Mindfulness]]. Reducing interruptions and closing loose ends also helps, since unfinished business keeps pulling at attention; see [[Attention Residue]].
