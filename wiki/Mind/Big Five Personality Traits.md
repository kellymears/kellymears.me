The **Big Five** is the standard model of personality in research psychology. It describes people along five broad dimensions, often remembered by the acronym **OCEAN**:

- **Openness to experience**: curiosity, imagination and appetite for novelty and ideas; see [[Curiosity]].
- **Conscientiousness**: organization, persistence, self-discipline and reliability.
- **Extraversion**: sociability, assertiveness and the tendency to feel positive emotions and seek stimulation. Its low end is **introversion**.
- **Agreeableness**: warmth, cooperation, trust and concern for others; see [[Empathy]].
- **Neuroticism**: the tendency toward negative emotions such as worry, sadness and irritability; see [[Anxiety]].

The model came from language rather than theory. The **lexical hypothesis** holds that important differences between people end up encoded as words. In 1936 Gordon Allport and Henry Odbert combed an English dictionary and found roughly 18,000 terms describing people. Later researchers asked people to rate themselves and others on condensed lists of those terms, then used factor analysis, a statistical method related to [[Dimensionality Reduction]], to see which words clustered. Air Force researchers Ernest Tupes and Raymond Christal found five recurring factors in 1961, Lewis Goldberg popularized the name "Big Five," and Paul Costa and Robert McCrae built the questionnaires that made it standard. Similar factors appear in many languages, though not identically. The HEXACO model adds a sixth, honesty-humility.

Each trait is a continuum, and most people sit near the middle of each. That is the biggest difference from the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator, which sorts people into sixteen types. Because scores near a cutoff flip on retesting, many people get a different Myers-Briggs type when they take it again, while Big Five scores are fairly stable. Personality descriptions of any kind should also be read with the [[Barnum Effect]] in mind, since vague profiles feel accurate to almost everyone.

The traits predict real outcomes, modestly. Conscientiousness is among the better predictors of school and job performance and is associated with living longer, partly through health habits. Neuroticism predicts risk for depression and anxiety disorders. Extraversion is associated with greater day-to-day positive mood. The effects are real but small for any one person, and situations still matter a great deal.

Twin studies suggest that roughly 40 percent to half of the variation in each trait is heritable, with most of the rest coming from experiences not shared by siblings. Personality also changes. On average, people become more conscientious and agreeable and less neurotic from adolescence through middle age, a pattern researchers call the maturity principle.

The model is descriptive. It says how people differ, not why, and it has been criticized for missing qualities that some cultures consider central. Cognitive ability is usually treated separately; see [[Intelligence Quotient]].
