A **cognitive bias** is a systematic pattern of error in judgment, one that pushes many people in the same direction rather than scattering them randomly. Random mistakes cancel out in a crowd; biases don't, which is why they matter for markets, juries, elections and engineering. Kahneman, Olivier Sibony and Cass Sunstein later stressed the other half of the picture in Noise (2021): judges, doctors and underwriters given identical cases also scatter widely, and that unsystematic variation can do as much damage as bias.

Most biases are side effects of **heuristics**, quick rules of thumb that save effort and usually work. The modern study of them began with Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman's 1974 paper "Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases," which described three shortcuts. People judge probability by how much something resembles a category, the [[Representativeness Heuristic]], by how easily examples come to mind, the [[Availability Heuristic]], and by adjusting from whatever number they saw first, the [[Anchoring Effect]]. Each works often enough to be useful and fails in predictable ways. Expertise offers limited protection, since experts show many of the same patterns inside their own fields. Kahneman later framed the shortcuts as products of fast, intuitive thinking that slower deliberation fails to check; see [[Dual Process Theory]].

Lists of cognitive biases now run to well over a hundred entries, though many overlap. Some broad families:

- **Belief-preserving** biases, which protect what one already thinks; see [[Confirmation Bias]] and [[Motivated Reasoning]].
- **Choice and framing** biases, in which equivalent options feel different depending on description; see [[Framing Effect]] and [[Loss Aversion]].
- **Probability** errors, such as ignoring how common something is in the first place, the base rate fallacy.
- **Self-assessment** biases, such as the [[Dunning-Kruger Effect]] and the habit of crediting oneself for successes while blaming circumstances for failures.

The research program has critics. Gerd Gigerenzer argues for **ecological rationality**: simple heuristics are often well matched to real environments, and many apparent biases shrink when problems are posed in natural frequencies rather than percentages. Others note that some famous effects, especially in social priming, failed to replicate; see [[Replication Crisis]]. And a few biases may be rational responses to limited time and information; see [[Satisficing]].

Knowing about biases is a weak defense. People readily spot bias in others while rating themselves as less affected, the **bias blind spot**; see [[Naive Realism]]. Studies of **debiasing** find that general warnings do little. What helps more is changing the process instead of the person: asking people to consider the opposite, using structured procedures and checklists, gathering independent estimates before discussion, and imagining in advance how a plan could fail; see [[Pre-mortem]].
