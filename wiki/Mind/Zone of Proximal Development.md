The **zone of proximal development** is the gap between what a learner can do independently and what they can do with help from someone more skilled. Tasks below the zone are already mastered and teach little. Tasks above it are impossible even with assistance. Learning happens in between, where a learner can succeed with support and gradually needs less of it.

The idea comes from the Soviet psychologist Lev Vygotsky, who developed it in the late 1920s and early 1930s before dying of tuberculosis in 1934 at the age of 37. Vygotsky's work was little known in the West until translations appeared from the 1960s onward, most influentially a 1978 collection titled Mind in Society. Vygotsky argued that intelligence tests measuring only independent performance missed something important: two children with the same score might differ greatly in what they could achieve with a little help, and that difference predicted future development.

The concept is part of a broader view that thinking is social in origin. Vygotsky held that children first solve problems in conversation with others and later internalize that conversation as private thought; see [[Inner Speech]].

In 1976, the psychologists David Wood, Jerome Bruner, and Gail Ross introduced the related term **scaffolding** to describe how a tutor supports a learner within the zone: simplifying the task, keeping attention on the goal, highlighting important features, demonstrating, and managing frustration. Like the scaffolding on a building, it's meant to be temporary, removed as the learner becomes able to stand alone.

The principle appears across fields:

- **Challenge.** Tasks pitched slightly beyond current skill produce the most engagement and growth; see [[Flow]] and [[Desirable Difficulty]].
- **Practice.** Expert training targets weaknesses just past current ability, with feedback from a coach; see [[Deliberate Practice]].
- **Games.** Good games introduce one new demand at a time, teaching through play instead of instruction; see [[Difficulty Curve]].
- **Software teams.** Working alongside a more experienced colleague transfers tacit skill faster than documentation; see [[Pair Programming]] and [[Onboarding]].

Experts often misjudge where a learner's zone lies. Having forgotten what it was like not to know, they explain at the wrong level or skip steps; see [[Curse of Knowledge]]. Learners overloaded with new information can't use help effectively; see [[Cognitive Load]].

Support works best when it builds independence. A tutor who does too much keeps a learner dependent, while one who withdraws at the right moment builds confidence; see [[Self-Efficacy]]. Learners who believe ability grows with effort are more likely to stay in the uncomfortable zone where growth happens; see [[Growth Mindset]]. Knowing what one does and doesn't yet understand is itself a skill teachers can scaffold; see [[Metacognition]].
