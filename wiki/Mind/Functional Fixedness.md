**Functional fixedness** is the tendency to see an object only in terms of its usual function. Someone who needs to hang a candle on a wall may not see that a box holding thumbtacks could become a shelf, because the box is "for" holding tacks. The psychologist Karl Duncker described this candle problem in 1945. When the tacks were presented outside the box, far more people solved it: once the box stopped looking like a container, it could be seen as a platform.

A related effect, the **Einstellung effect** or *mental set*, is about methods rather than objects. In Abraham Luchins's water jar experiments of 1942, people solved a series of problems using one complex formula. When later problems could be solved by a much simpler method, most kept using the complex one, and many failed problems that only the simple method could solve. Chess experts show the same thing: a familiar winning pattern can stop them from seeing a shorter win on the board, and eye tracking shows their gaze keeps returning to the familiar pattern.

Both effects come from the same source as expertise. Knowing what things are for and how problems are usually solved makes people fast and efficient most of the time; see [[Expert Intuition]]. The cost is that the first idea that comes to mind crowds out the rest. Experience narrows the search.

The software version is familiar:

- **Hammer and nail.** Teams reach for the tool or pattern they already know, whether or not it fits.
- **Debugging.** A developer convinced the bug is in one module keeps looking there; see [[Confirmation Bias]].
- **Design fixation.** Designers shown an example solution tend to reproduce its features, including its flaws, even when told to avoid them.
- **Legacy framing.** A system named for its original purpose becomes hard to see as anything else; see [[Naming]].

Several techniques loosen fixedness:

- **Describe parts generically.** Calling the box "cardboard" rather than "a tack box" frees its use. Engineers taught to strip objects down to their material and shape solve more insight problems.
- **Step away.** Taking a break lets the dominant idea fade, one explanation of why solutions arrive in the shower; see [[Mind-Wandering]].
- **Explain it to someone.** Talking a problem through forces a re-description; see [[Rubber Duck Debugging]].
- **Bring in outsiders.** People without the usual training aren't fixed on the usual uses.
- **Ask what else.** Deliberately listing alternatives before choosing counters the first-idea pull.

Children younger than about five show less functional fixedness than older children, presumably because they haven't yet learned firm categories of use. The fixedness is a sign that learning has happened, not a defect; it just needs occasional overriding.
