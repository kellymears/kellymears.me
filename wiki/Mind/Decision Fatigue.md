**Decision fatigue** is the idea that the quality of decisions declines after a long run of decision-making: people tired of choosing fall back on defaults, avoid choosing, or choose impulsively. The idea is intuitive, widely cited, and attached to famous anecdotes, such as executives who wear the same outfit every day to save their choosing for work.

Its scientific history is cautionary. It grew out of **ego depletion**, Roy Baumeister's 1998 theory that self-control draws on a limited resource that runs down with use, like a muscle. The founding experiment had hungry participants resist fresh cookies and eat radishes instead; they then gave up sooner on an unsolvable puzzle. Hundreds of follow-up studies seemed to confirm it. Then a 2016 registered replication across 23 laboratories found an effect near zero, and analyses of the literature pointed to [[Publication Bias]] and flexible analysis ([[P-Hacking]]) inflating the original record. A famous field study claiming that judges granted parole far less often just before meal breaks was also challenged. The effect was implausibly large for a fatigue mechanism, and case ordering offered a simpler explanation.

What survives is more modest:

- **Decisions cost time and attention.** Every choice adds to [[Cognitive Load]], and more options make choosing slower, the logic of [[Hick's Law]]. This doesn't require a depletable willpower reserve.
- **Fatigue is real.** Tired, hungry, or stressed people make worse decisions, though as a general state rather than specifically from choosing.
- **Too many options can paralyze.** *Choice overload* findings are themselves mixed, but large, unstructured choice sets reliably feel worse.

The practical advice survives the shakier theory because it rests on these modest points. Reduce the number of decisions, with defaults, routines, and pre-made plans, so attention goes to the decisions that matter. That is a [[Defensive Default]] for ordinary life, and the reason good interfaces choose sensible defaults instead of asking. The same lever works in the opposite direction: choice architecture that exhausts users into accepting the default is a [[Dark Pattern]].

The case is a good example of a [[Plausible Mechanism]] outrunning its evidence. Saying "decisions are tiring" is reasonable. A precise, muscle-like resource was the part that did not hold up.
