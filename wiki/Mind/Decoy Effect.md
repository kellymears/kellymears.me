The **decoy effect** is a shift in preference between two options when a third, inferior option is added. The decoy is designed to be clearly worse than one option, the target, but not clearly worse than the other. People then choose the target more often, even though the decoy itself is almost never picked. It's also called the **asymmetric dominance effect**, after the way the decoy is dominated by only one of the two.

Joel Huber, John Payne and Christopher Puto described the effect in 1982. Its best-known illustration comes from Dan Ariely's 2008 book Predictably Irrational. A magazine offered a web subscription, a print subscription at a much higher price, and print plus web at the same price as print alone. Nobody wants print alone when print plus web costs the same, yet in Ariely's classroom test most students chose the bundle. Remove the useless print-only option and most chose web only instead.

The effect is a puzzle for standard accounts of choice. Rational choice assumes that adding an option you'd never pick shouldn't change how you rank the ones you might; see [[Satisficing]]. Voting theory calls this the independence of irrelevant alternatives, and its failure in elections is part of what makes fair voting systems impossible to design; see [[Arrow's Impossibility Theorem]]. A third candidate who draws votes from a similar one is a relative of the decoy; see [[Duverger's Law]].

The usual explanation is that people find it hard to judge options in absolute terms, so they look for easy comparisons. A decoy offers one: the target is obviously better than something, which feels like a reason to choose it. The same reliance on reference points underlies other pricing tricks; see [[Anchoring Effect]] and [[Framing Effect]]. A medium popcorn priced just below the large makes the large look like a bargain.

Designers who arrange options deliberately to steer choices are practicing choice architecture; see [[Choice Architecture]]. When the steering works against the user's interests, it shades into manipulation; see [[Dark Pattern]]. Companies test menu layouts to find which decoy works best; see [[AB Testing]].

The effect isn't as universal as early demonstrations suggested. A 2014 study found it weak or absent when options were shown as pictures or described in realistic detail rather than as tidy tables of numbers, and several replications have found smaller effects than the originals; see [[Replication Crisis]]. It seems strongest when attributes are easy to compare and the chooser has no strong prior preference.

People also rationalize choices after the fact, explaining a selection in terms of features that didn't drive it; see [[Choice Blindness]]. Faced with too many options, they lean on shortcuts like the decoy's ready-made comparison even more; see [[Decision Fatigue]].
