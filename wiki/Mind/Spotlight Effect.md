The **spotlight effect** is the tendency to believe that other people notice one's appearance, mistakes, and behavior far more than they actually do. People feel as though they are under a spotlight, when most others are too busy thinking about themselves to pay much attention.

Thomas Gilovich, Victoria Medvec, and Kenneth Savitsky demonstrated it in 2000. They asked college students to put on a T-shirt with a large picture of the singer Barry Manilow, then unfashionable among their peers, and walk into a room of other students. The wearers estimated that about half the people in the room would remember the shirt. In fact, about a quarter did. Later studies found the same overestimation for bad hair days, performance in group discussions, and absences from meetings.

A related bias is the **illusion of transparency**: people overestimate how much their internal states show. Liars think their nervousness is obvious, speakers think the audience can see their anxiety, and people who feel disgusted while tasting something think observers can tell. In experiments, observers were much worse at detecting these feelings than the people experiencing them predicted.

Both biases come from the same source. People start from their own vivid experience, which is very salient to them, and adjust insufficiently for the fact that others don't share it; see [[Anchoring]] and [[Curse of Knowledge]]. Imagining others' perspectives requires effort, and people stop adjusting too early; see [[Theory of Mind]].

The effects have practical consequences:

- **Social anxiety.** People who feel watched and judged avoid situations, and the spotlight effect makes small embarrassments feel more serious than they are; see [[Rejection Sensitivity]] and [[Rumination]].
- **Public speaking.** In one study, telling speakers about the illusion of transparency before a talk made them calmer, and audiences rated their speeches as better; see [[Choking Under Pressure]].
- **Group silence.** Each bystander in an emergency may think their concern is obvious to others, and seeing others look calm, concludes that nothing is wrong; see [[Bystander Effect]] and [[Pluralistic Ignorance]].
- **Self-presentation.** Constant self-monitoring, such as hiding traits seen as unusual, takes energy, partly because people think any slip will be noticed; see [[Masking]] and [[Impostor Syndrome]].

The effect fades with time and distance. People overestimate how much others will remember an embarrassing moment, and how harshly they'll judge it, but observers tend to be more forgiving than people expect. Knowing this can help reduce self-criticism; see [[Self-Compassion]] and [[Cognitive Behavioral Therapy]].

The spotlight effect has a mirror image: people also underestimate how much others notice their positive actions, such as a compliment or a small kindness, and as a result do them less often than they might.
