summary: The experience of an unchanging image that flips between two or more interpretations, such as a cube that faces two ways or a drawing that is both a duck and a rabbit, revealing perception as an active guess the brain keeps revising.
---
**Multistable perception** occurs when a single, unchanging stimulus supports more than one interpretation, and perception switches between them. A line drawing of a cube seems to face one way, then suddenly the other. A silhouette is a vase, then two faces in profile. Nothing on the page changes; what changes is the brain's reading of it.

Classic examples have names. The **Necker cube** was described in 1832 by the Swiss crystallographer Louis Albert Necker, who noticed that drawings of crystals seemed to flip in depth. The duck-rabbit drawing, which circulated in a German humor magazine in the 1890s, was taken up by the psychologist Joseph Jastrow in 1899 and later by the philosopher Ludwig Wittgenstein. The Danish psychologist Edgar Rubin published the vase-and-faces figure in 1915 to study how the visual system separates figure from ground; see [[Gestalt Principles]].

In **binocular rivalry**, each eye is shown a different image, such as a face to one and a house to the other. Instead of blending them, perception alternates between the two every few seconds, with brief mixed transitions. Rivalry has become a tool for studying consciousness, since the stimulus stays constant while awareness changes.

Several features recur across these phenomena:

- **Only one at a time.** People see one interpretation or the other, almost never both at once.
- **Spontaneous switching.** Perception flips every few seconds without any change in the image, at rates that vary between individuals.
- **Partial control.** Attention and intention can bias which interpretation dominates and how long it lasts, but can't stop the switching entirely; see [[Selective Attention]].
- **Adaptation.** The current interpretation seems to fatigue, making the other more likely to take over; see [[Habituation]].

Modern accounts treat perception as inference. The brain continuously predicts what is out there and checks those predictions against sensory input. When two hypotheses explain the input equally well, neither wins decisively, and perception alternates between them; see [[Predictive Processing]] and [[Bayesian Inference]].

The same machinery produces related experiences. Seeing faces in clouds or electrical outlets is the brain committing to an interpretation the stimulus only weakly supports; see [[Pareidolia]]. Missing obvious objects shows how much perception depends on what the brain is set to find; see [[Inattentional Blindness]].

In 2015, a photograph of a dress divided the internet between people who saw it as white and gold and those who saw it as blue and black. Most individuals saw it one way and couldn't switch, so it differs from true multistability, but it made the same point: perception is an interpretation, and people can be certain their interpretation is simply what's there; see [[Naive Realism]].
