**Dyslexia** is a specific difficulty with learning to read and spell accurately and fluently, despite adequate teaching and intelligence. It is the most common learning difference, with estimates from 5 to 10 percent of people depending on where the line is drawn, and it runs strongly in families. It is not a problem with vision, and it is not reading letters backward, though young children of all kinds reverse letters while learning.

The best-supported account places the core difficulty in **phonological processing**: perceiving, holding, and manipulating the sounds that make up words. Reading an alphabetic script means mapping letters to those sounds, so a weak grip on the sounds makes the mapping slow and effortful. People with dyslexia often also show slow rapid naming, the speed of naming familiar items such as colors or digits, and limited verbal [[Working Memory]].

How dyslexia shows up depends on the writing system. English has a deep orthography, where spelling and sound are loosely related, so dyslexia appears as inaccurate reading and spelling. In languages with shallow, regular spelling, such as Finnish or Italian, dyslexic readers usually decode accurately but read slowly. Its form tracks the language, which supports the phonological account over a visual one.

Brain imaging shows reduced activity in left-hemisphere reading networks, and some of that activity increases after effective teaching, a case of [[Neuroplasticity]]. The most effective intervention is **structured literacy**: explicit, systematic teaching of sound-letter relationships, practiced to fluency, started as early as possible.

Some popular remedies have weak evidence:

- **Dyslexia fonts.** Special typefaces with weighted letter bottoms have not outperformed ordinary ones in controlled studies. Wider letter and line spacing helps more than the [[Typeface]] itself.
- **Colored overlays and lenses.** Research has not found reliable benefits.
- **Vision therapy.** Eye exercises don't address a sound-processing difference.

What does help in daily life is reducing the load of print:

- **Audio.** Audiobooks and [[Text-to-Speech]] give access to content without decoding.
- **Readable text.** Generous spacing, comfortable [[Line Length]], left-aligned text, and [[Plain Language]] lower effort for everyone.
- **Time.** Extra time on exams accounts for slower, more effortful reading.
- **Tools.** Spell checkers and dictation separate thinking from spelling.

These accommodations follow the logic of [[Inclusive Design]] and [[Accessibility]]: the barrier is often a print-only environment rather than the reader.

Dyslexia often co-occurs with [[ADHD]] and with dyscalculia, a related difficulty with numbers. Adults frequently describe years of being thought careless or slow before diagnosis, with lasting effects on [[Self-Efficacy]]. Within the framework of [[Neurodiversity]], some argue dyslexia brings strengths in big-picture or spatial thinking. Those claims have mixed evidence, but the cost of treating reading difficulty as a measure of intelligence is clear.
