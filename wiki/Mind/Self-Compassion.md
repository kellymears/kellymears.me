**Self-compassion** is the practice of responding to one's own failures and suffering with kindness rather than judgment. The psychologist Kristin Neff defined it in 2003 with three components, each set against its opposite:

- **Self-kindness versus self-judgment.** Speaking to oneself as one would to a friend who made the same mistake, rather than with contempt.
- **Common humanity versus isolation.** Recognizing that failure and pain are part of being human, rather than proof that one is uniquely flawed.
- **Mindfulness versus over-identification.** Noticing painful feelings clearly without suppressing them or being swept away by them, the balance taught in [[Mindfulness]].

Self-compassion is often confused with self-esteem, but they differ. Self-esteem is a judgment of one's worth, and it tends to depend on success and comparison with others, so it collapses after failure exactly when it is needed. Self-compassion does not require feeling above average; it applies most when things go badly.

The common objection is that self-kindness breeds complacency, and that the [[Inner Speech]] of a harsh inner critic is what keeps people striving. The evidence suggests the opposite. In a 2012 study by Juliana Breines and Serena Chen, students who were encouraged to be self-compassionate after failing a test spent more time studying for a retest than those encouraged to boost their self-esteem. Self-compassion is associated with less [[Procrastination]], since avoidance is often a way of escaping the self-criticism that a task threatens, and with less [[Rumination]], anxiety, and depression.

Paul Gilbert's compassion-focused therapy offers an explanation. It describes three emotional systems: a *threat* system tuned for danger, a *drive* system for pursuing goals, and a *soothing* system associated with safety and connection. Self-criticism activates the threat system, flooding the body with stress and narrowing attention, which undermines learning and pushes people outside their [[Window of Tolerance]]. Self-compassion engages the soothing system, which makes it safe to look honestly at mistakes.

Practices that build it include:

- **The friend test.** Asking what you would say to a friend in the same situation, then saying that to yourself.
- **Self-compassion breaks.** Naming the pain, recalling that others feel it too, and offering a kind phrase or a hand on the chest.
- **Reframing the critic.** Noticing harsh self-talk and responding to it, a technique shared with [[Cognitive Behavioral Therapy]].

Self-compassion matters especially for people who spend much of their lives being told they are doing things wrong. For neurodivergent people, years of correction, [[Masking]], and [[Rejection Sensitivity]] often leave a well-trained inner critic, and self-compassion is a recurring theme in recovery from [[Autistic Burnout]]. It also counters [[Impostor Syndrome]], which feeds on the belief that any flaw is disqualifying.
