**Inattentional blindness** is failing to notice something fully visible because attention is engaged elsewhere. The eyes take in the scene, but what isn't attended doesn't reach awareness. Arien Mack and Irvin Rock named the effect in their 1998 book, and Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris made it famous a year later. They asked viewers to count basketball passes in a short video. Partway through, a person in a gorilla suit walks into the middle of the game, thumps their chest, and walks off. About half of viewers never saw it, and many refused to believe the gorilla had been there until they watched again.

A sibling effect, **change blindness**, is failing to notice that something has changed. If a scene flickers or is briefly interrupted, people miss large changes, such as a building disappearing from a photo, that would be obvious side by side. In one field study, an experimenter asking a pedestrian for directions was swapped for a different person while a door was carried between them, and about half of the pedestrians didn't notice they were now talking to someone else.

The effects follow from how [[Selective Attention]] works. Perception is limited, and the brain spends it on what the current task makes relevant. Expertise doesn't protect against this, it can narrow it further. When Trafton Drew and colleagues hid a matchbook-sized gorilla in lung CT scans, 83 percent of radiologists searching for nodules missed it, though eye tracking showed most had looked straight at it. Expectation steers attention too, in line with [[Predictive Processing]]: we tend to see what we are looking for, a perceptual cousin of [[Confirmation Bias]] and the [[Streetlight Effect]].

It has practical consequences:

- **Safety.** Drivers on the phone miss cyclists in plain view, and pilots watching a display can miss an aircraft on the runway. Monitoring automated systems invites the same failure, part of [[Automation Bias]].
- **Interface design.** A message that appears where the user isn't looking may as well not exist. [[Motion Design]] and transitions help people track what changed, and [[Progressive Disclosure]] limits how much competes for attention.
- **Review and testing.** People reliably miss differences between two screenshots, which is why [[Visual Regression Testing]] compares pixels mechanically. Scripted checks see only what they are told to look for, a gap [[Exploratory Testing]] exists to fill.

Strong focus, as in [[Monotropism]] or [[Hyperfocus]], exaggerates both sides: deep engagement with the task and little awareness of anything outside it.
