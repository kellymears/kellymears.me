**Self-determination theory** is a broad theory of human motivation developed by psychologists Edward Deci and Richard Ryan, beginning in the 1970s and formalized in the 1980s. It distinguishes kinds of motivation by where they come from and argues that people have three basic psychological needs whose satisfaction drives wellbeing and lasting engagement.

The three needs:

- **Autonomy.** Feeling that one's actions are self-chosen and endorsed, not controlled from outside. Autonomy doesn't mean independence; one can freely choose to rely on others.
- **Competence.** Feeling effective and able to master challenges, and seeing progress.
- **Relatedness.** Feeling connected to and cared about by others.

The theory places motivation on a spectrum. **Intrinsic motivation** is doing something because it is inherently interesting or enjoyable, as children play or hobbyists tinker. **Extrinsic motivation** is doing it for a separate outcome, a reward, a grade, avoiding punishment. Extrinsic motivation varies, though: it can be fully external and controlled, or internalized so that a person values the outcome as their own, as when someone exercises because health matters to them. More internalized motivation lasts longer and feels better.

Deci's early experiments produced the theory's most famous claim, the **overjustification effect**. In a 1971 study, students worked on engaging puzzles. Those paid for each solution spent less time on the puzzles during a free period afterward than those who were never paid. A reward had turned play into work. Later studies found the effect for expected, performance-contingent rewards, while unexpected rewards and informative praise did little harm. A 1999 meta-analysis by Deci, Richard Koestner, and Ryan confirmed the pattern across 128 studies, though the size and generality of the effect have been debated.

The theory has wide application:

- **Work.** Micromanagement and surveillance undermine autonomy, while meaningful choice and feedback support it. Daniel Pink's popular book *Drive* (2009) built on these ideas. Metrics used as controlling targets can backfire much as [[Goodhart's Law]] predicts.
- **Education.** Students who feel autonomous and competent learn more deeply than those driven by grades alone.
- **Games and apps.** Games satisfy all three needs, through choice, mastery shaped by the [[Difficulty Curve]], and shared play. Systems that lean on extrinsic hooks, such as a [[Compulsion Loop]], streaks, and rewards on a schedule, can capture behavior without satisfying the needs.

The needs map onto other ideas in this collection. Competence and absorption meet in [[Flow]]. Loss of autonomy features in [[Demand Avoidance]], where demands themselves trigger resistance. Repeated failure to control outcomes can lead to [[Learned Helplessness]], and reward systems in the brain are described under [[Reward Prediction Error]]. [[Procrastination]] often reflects tasks experienced as externally imposed rather than chosen. [[Habit Formation]] offers a complementary route, building behaviors that run on cues rather than motivation.
