**Self-handicapping** is putting obstacles in one's own path before a performance, so that if things go badly there's an excuse ready. A student who parties the night before an exam, a musician who skips rehearsal, a job seeker who sends the application at the last minute: each has made failure more likely, but also made failure less damning. If they fail, it was the handicap. If they succeed despite it, they must be especially capable.

The psychologists Steven Berglas and Edward Jones described the pattern in a 1978 experiment. Participants worked on problems, and some were told they had done well on items that were in fact nearly impossible, leaving them uncertain how they had succeeded. Before a second test, they were offered a choice between a drug said to improve performance and one said to impair it. Those whose earlier success felt unearned were more likely to choose the impairing drug, giving themselves an explanation in advance for a failure they feared.

Handicaps come in two broad forms:

- **Behavioral handicaps** actually reduce the chance of success: not practicing, drinking, choosing an unrealistically hard goal, starting too late; see [[Procrastination]].
- **Claimed handicaps** are excuses offered in advance without changing performance: announcing that one is ill, anxious, or didn't sleep.

The strategy protects a particular belief: that ability is fixed and a failure would reveal its limits. People who see ability as something that grows with effort have less reason to hide behind excuses, since a failure is information instead of a verdict; see [[Growth Mindset]]. Self-handicapping is common where people doubt their competence despite evidence of it; see [[Impostor Syndrome]]. It also flourishes when people feel judged by others watching closely; see [[Spotlight Effect]].

The tactic exploits how people explain outcomes. Failure can be blamed on circumstances and success credited to ability, a pattern that protects self-esteem; see [[Self-Serving Bias]]. Observers, though, tend to see behavior as a reflection of character, and often view chronic self-handicappers as unreliable; see [[Fundamental Attribution Error]].

The costs accumulate. Over time, handicapping produces worse performance and grades, and people who use it regularly tend to report lower well-being. Each excuse avoids learning what one could actually do, so confidence never gets the evidence it needs; see [[Self-Efficacy]]. Under threat of confirming a negative stereotype, people may handicap themselves to protect against the comparison; see [[Stereotype Threat]].

Alternatives involve making failure less threatening. Treating oneself kindly after setbacks, instead of harshly, reduces the need to defend against them; see [[Self-Compassion]]. Feeling that outcomes depend mainly on one's own actions also helps; see [[Locus of Control]].
