**Exposure therapy** treats fear and anxiety by having a person confront what they fear, in a planned, gradual way and without escaping, until the fear subsides and they learn the situation is safe. It is one of the best-supported treatments in psychology, especially for phobias, panic disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and post-traumatic stress, and a core component of [[Cognitive Behavioral Therapy]].

Avoidance is what keeps fears alive. Each time someone escapes a feared situation, the relief rewards the escape, and they never find out that the feared outcome wouldn't have happened. Exposure breaks that loop.

History:

- **1924.** Mary Cover Jones treated a toddler's fear of rabbits by gradually bringing a rabbit closer while he ate, an early application of conditioning research; see [[Classical Conditioning]].
- **1958.** Joseph Wolpe developed **systematic desensitization**, pairing relaxation training with a graded ladder of imagined fear scenes.
- **1966.** Victor Meyer described **exposure and response prevention** for obsessive-compulsive disorder: the person faces the trigger, such as touching a doorknob, and refrains from the compulsion, such as washing, until the urge passes.

Practice usually follows a **fear hierarchy**, a ranked list of situations from mildly to very frightening. The person starts low and moves up as each step becomes manageable. Exposure can be in person, imagined, or in virtual reality. For panic, it includes provoking the feared bodily sensations themselves, such as a racing heart from running in place; see [[Interoception]].

Early theory said fear fades through repetition, as the nervous system stops responding; see [[Habituation]]. The newer **inhibitory learning** model, associated with Michelle Craske, holds that the original fear memory isn't erased. Instead, the person learns a new memory, that the situation is safe, which competes with the old one. This explains why fears can return after time passes or in new places; see [[Context-Dependent Memory]]. It also changes practice:

- **Violate expectations.** Exposures should test a specific feared prediction, and the surprise when it doesn't come true drives learning; see [[Reward Prediction Error]].
- **Vary contexts** so that safety learning generalizes.
- **Drop safety behaviors**, small protective habits like carrying medication or gripping a rail, which let people credit their survival to the crutch.
- **Expect difficulty.** Exposures that remain uncomfortable can still produce learning; see [[Desirable Difficulty]].

Exposure works within limits. Pushing too far too fast overwhelms a person rather than teaching them; see [[Window of Tolerance]]. Repeated success builds confidence in one's ability to cope; see [[Self-Efficacy]]. Sensory distress in autistic people is a different matter: it reflects how stimuli are processed rather than a learned fear, and forced exposure can harm rather than help; see [[Sensory Overload]].

The same principle helps with social anxiety and fear of rejection; see [[Rejection Sensitivity]].
