The **representativeness heuristic** is the habit of judging how likely something is by how much it resembles a typical example. A quiet, tidy person who loves books seems like a librarian, so people guess librarian, even though there are far more salespeople and farmers in the world. Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman described the heuristic in the early 1970s as one of the shortcuts behind everyday judgment, alongside the [[Availability Heuristic]] and [[Anchoring]].

Resemblance is a reasonable guide much of the time, since typical members of a category usually do share its features; see [[Prototype Theory]]. The trouble is that it replaces a question about probability with a question about similarity, and the two come apart in predictable ways.

The most famous demonstration is the **Linda problem** (1983). Participants read that Linda is 31, single, outspoken, and very bright, majored in philosophy, and cared deeply about discrimination and social justice as a student. Asked which is more probable, that Linda is a bank teller or that Linda is a bank teller active in the feminist movement, most chose the second. That is impossible: every feminist bank teller is a bank teller, so the combined description can't be more likely. Tversky and Kahneman called this the **conjunction fallacy**. Adding detail makes a story more representative, and so more convincing, even as it makes it less probable.

Other errors follow the same pattern:

- **Ignoring base rates.** People judging whether a described person is an engineer or a lawyer largely ignored how many of each were in the group; see [[Base Rate Fallacy]] and [[Bayes' Theorem]].
- **Small samples.** People expect short sequences of random events to look random, so a run of heads seems due to end; see [[Gambler's Fallacy]].
- **Predictions from extremes.** An outstanding performance seems representative of skill, so people fail to expect the next one to be closer to average; see [[Regression to the Mean]].

Not everyone accepts this account of the errors. Gerd Gigerenzer argued that people reason much better when problems are stated as counts, such as "out of 100 people like Linda," rather than as single-case probabilities, and that many participants read "bank teller" as meaning "bank teller who is not a feminist." Rates of the conjunction fallacy do drop with frequency formats, though they don't vanish.

In practice, the heuristic matters wherever stories are used as evidence. Doctors can fixate on a classic presentation and miss a common disease that looks atypical; investors back founders who look like past successes; see [[Survivorship Bias]]. Detailed, vivid scenarios feel more plausible than vague ones, which is why forecasters are taught to check a story's likelihood piece by piece; see [[Dual Process Theory]] and [[Occam's Razor]].
