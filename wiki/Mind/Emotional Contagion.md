**Emotional contagion** is the spread of emotion from one person to another, often without either noticing. A cheerful colleague lifts a room; an anxious one makes everyone tense. People catch moods the way they catch yawns, through processes that mostly run below awareness.

The psychologists Elaine Hatfield, John Cacioppo, and Richard Rapson gave the idea its modern form in the early 1990s, culminating in a 1994 book. They described a three-step mechanism. First, people automatically mimic the facial expressions, voices, and postures of those around them, often within fractions of a second. Second, the feedback from those movements nudges their own feelings in the same direction. Third, they come to share the emotion.

The process starts early. Newborns cry more when they hear other newborns crying than when they hear other sounds. Adults mirror smiles and frowns in muscle activity measurable with electrodes even when they're unaware of doing it. Brain regions that are active when performing an action are also active when watching it, which has been proposed as one basis of the effect; see [[Mirror Neurons]]. Yawning offers a familiar physical parallel; see [[Contagious yawning]].

Contagion shapes group life:

- **Workplaces.** Studies of teams have found that a single member's mood can shift the mood and cooperation of the whole group; a manager's stress often spreads down. Groups where people feel safe to speak up recover from bad moods more easily; see [[Psychological Safety]].
- **Caring professions.** Nurses, therapists, and others exposed to constant distress can absorb it, contributing to exhaustion; see [[Compassion fatigue]].
- **Crowds.** Panic, euphoria, and outrage spread quickly through gatherings, sometimes overriding individual judgment; see [[Conformity]] and [[Moral Panic]].

Contagion also travels through screens. In a 2014 study, researchers adjusted the news feeds of nearly 700,000 social media users to show fewer positive or fewer negative posts, and found a small shift in the emotional tone of what those users then wrote. The effect was tiny, but the experiment drew heavy criticism because users hadn't knowingly agreed to have their emotions manipulated. Feeds that reward outrage and alarm may spread those emotions at scale; see [[Doomscrolling]].

Contagion isn't the same as empathy. Catching a feeling is automatic, while understanding what another person feels and why requires taking their perspective; see [[Theory of Mind]]. People can also misread the source of their own arousal, attributing a feeling caught from a room to something else entirely; see [[Misattribution of Arousal]].

The effect can be harnessed deliberately. Working alongside a calm, focused person can steady attention, and many people find quiet company makes hard tasks easier to start; see [[Body Doubling]]. People who hide their own expressions to fit in may find that the effort itself is draining; see [[Masking]].
