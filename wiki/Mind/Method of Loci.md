The **method of loci**, often called the *memory palace*, is a memory technique in which a person imagines walking through a familiar place, such as their home or the route to work, and leaves a vivid image of each item to remember at a specific spot along the way. To recall the items, they mentally walk the route again and "see" what they placed. *Loci* is Latin for "places."

The technique is ancient. Roman writers attribute its invention to the Greek poet Simonides of Ceos, who, according to the story, left a banquet hall moments before its roof collapsed and identified the crushed guests by remembering where each had been sitting. Cicero described the method, and the anonymous Roman textbook *Rhetorica ad Herennium*, from around 80 BCE, gave detailed instructions. Orators used it to deliver long speeches without notes.

The method works by recruiting abilities the brain is especially good at:

- **Spatial memory.** People easily remember the layout of familiar places, a skill that likely evolved for navigation. The route supplies a ready-made order.
- **Visual imagery.** Concrete images are remembered better than abstract words.
- **Distinctiveness.** Practitioners are taught to make images bizarre, exaggerated, funny, or emotional, since unusual things stick, the [[Von Restorff Effect]] in action.
- **Elaboration.** Turning a word into a scene forces deeper processing than repetition, linking new information to what is already known.

The technique is highly effective. Studies going back to the 1960s show large gains in list recall. A 2017 study by Martin Dresler and colleagues found that people with no special memory ability, after six weeks of training in the method, more than doubled their recall of word lists, and their patterns of brain connectivity shifted toward those of memory champions. Competitors in memory sports, who memorize shuffled decks of cards in under 30 seconds, nearly all use variants of it.

The method has limits. It suits ordered lists, names, numbers, and speeches better than understanding a complex subject, where connections between ideas matter more than sequence. It takes practice to build and maintain palaces, and reusing the same route can blur old and new images. For long-term learning of material that must be understood, [[Spaced Repetition]] and other [[Desirable Difficulty]] techniques usually do more.

It still offers a useful lesson about memory: it is not a passive recording but an active construction tied to context and imagery, which also makes it fallible, as research on [[False Memory]] shows. Spatial cues can serve as [[Context-Dependent Memory]] triggers. The memory palace also resembles the [[Extended Mind]] idea in reverse: rather than storing information in the world, it builds an imagined world to store information in. Its demands on visualization connect it to [[Working Memory]] research.
