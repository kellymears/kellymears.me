A **cognitive map** is an internal model of the layout of an environment, one that lets an animal plan routes, take shortcuts, and find its way from new starting points instead of only repeating learned turns. The psychologist Edward Tolman introduced the term in 1948, arguing against behaviorists who saw maze learning as chains of stimulus and response; see [[Classical Conditioning]].

Tolman's rats provided two kinds of evidence. Rats that wandered a maze without reward, then were given food at the end, quickly matched rats rewarded all along, showing they had learned the layout without reinforcement, which he called latent learning. And when a familiar route was blocked, rats chose new paths heading in the right direction, as if consulting a map.

The neural basis emerged later:

- **Place cells.** In 1971 John O'Keefe found neurons in the rat hippocampus that fire when the animal is in a specific location.
- **Grid cells.** In 2005 May-Britt and Edvard Moser found neurons in the nearby entorhinal cortex that fire at points forming a triangular grid across any space, acting like coordinates.

The three shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2014. Head-direction and border cells complete the system.

The hippocampus also supports memory for events, and the overlap isn't a coincidence. Memories are often anchored in places, and the ancient memory palace technique exploits the spatial system to store unrelated items; see [[Method of Loci]] and [[Episodic Memory]]. During sleep, place cells replay routes from the day, part of how memories stabilize; see [[Memory Consolidation]].

Training changes the system. A 2000 study by Eleanor Maguire found that London taxi drivers, who memorize the city's tangle of streets for an exam called the Knowledge, had a larger rear hippocampus than controls, growing with years on the job; see [[Neuroplasticity]] and [[Deliberate Practice]]. Satellite navigation may have the opposite effect: studies associate heavy GPS use with weaker spatial memory, since following turn-by-turn directions doesn't require building a map. Offloading navigation to a device makes the device part of the thinking; see [[Extended Mind]].

Researchers increasingly think the same machinery maps abstract spaces, organizing concepts, social relationships, and problem states as if they were locations. People describe ideas spatially, as close or far apart. Designers of buildings and websites rely on people forming accurate maps quickly, through landmarks, consistent structure, and clear signs; see [[Wayfinding]] and [[Information Architecture]].

A cognitive map is a model, and like any model it simplifies and distorts. People straighten curved roads, align streets to a grid, and misjudge distances; see [[Map and Territory]] and [[Mental Model]]. Agents in artificial intelligence face the same choice between learning a model of their world and learning fixed responses; see [[Reinforcement Learning]].
