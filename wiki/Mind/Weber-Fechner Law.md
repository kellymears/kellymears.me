The **Weber-Fechner law** describes how perceived intensity relates to physical intensity. Its core finding is that people notice changes in proportion to what is already there, not in absolute amounts. A candle lit in a dark room is obvious; the same candle added to a sunlit room is invisible.

It has two parts:

- **Weber's law.** In the 1830s the physiologist Ernst Weber found that the **just-noticeable difference**, the smallest change a person can reliably detect, is a constant fraction of the starting stimulus. If someone can just tell 100 grams from 103, they need about 206 versus 200 to notice the same difference at double the weight.
- **Fechner's law.** In 1860 the physicist Gustav Fechner built on this to argue that sensation grows with the logarithm of stimulus intensity: each doubling of the stimulus adds the same amount of perceived change. His *Elements of Psychophysics* founded the field.

In 1957 Stanley Stevens proposed a revision, **Stevens' power law**, after asking people to assign numbers to sensations directly. Perceived magnitude follows a power of intensity, with exponents that vary by sense: brightness grows much more slowly than light energy, while the sensation of electric shock grows faster than the current.

Logarithmic perception shapes many measurement scales:

- **Sound.** Decibels measure loudness on a logarithmic scale, matching how hearing compresses an enormous range of pressures; see [[Decibel]].
- **Pitch.** Each octave doubles frequency, yet octaves sound like equal steps.
- **Brightness and color.** Images are stored with gamma encoding, spending more bits on dark tones where the eye is more sensitive; see [[Gamma Correction]]. Color spaces built for perceptual uniformity make equal numeric steps look equal; see [[OKLCH]] and [[Color Scale]].
- **Stars.** The astronomical magnitude scale, inherited from ancient Greece, turned out to be roughly logarithmic in brightness.

Charts often switch to a log axis for the same reason; see [[Logarithmic Scale]].

Number sense follows a similar pattern. Young children, and adults in cultures without formal schooling, place numbers on a line in a compressed, logarithmic way, giving more space to small numbers; see [[Dyscalculia]].

The law extends to money. Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman found in 1981 that people would travel across town to save $5 on a $15 calculator but not on a $125 jacket, though the saving was identical. Prices, raises, and discounts are judged relative to a reference point; see [[Anchoring Effect]] and [[Framing Effect]]. Gains and losses in prospect theory follow a diminishing curve for the same reason; see [[Loss Aversion]]. Each additional increase in income or comfort brings less felt change; see [[Hedonic Treadmill]].

Steady stimuli fade further as the senses adapt; see [[Habituation]]. Differences in how strongly people register stimuli are central to accounts of sensory sensitivity; see [[Sensory Processing]].
