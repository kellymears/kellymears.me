**Attachment theory** holds that infants form a deep emotional bond with their caregivers, that this bond evolved to keep them close to protection, and that its quality shapes how they handle distress and closeness. The British psychiatrist John Bowlby developed the theory from the 1950s, and the American-Canadian psychologist Mary Ainsworth gave it its measurement and evidence.

Bowlby studied children separated from their families, including hospitalized children and those evacuated in wartime, and saw the same sequence: protest, despair, and detachment. Against the then-dominant view that babies love whoever feeds them, he argued that attachment is a separate need. Harry Harlow's experiments, in which infant monkeys clung to a soft cloth figure rather than a wire one that gave milk, pointed the same way.

Ainsworth developed the **Strange Situation** in the 1960s and 1970s, a twenty-minute lab procedure in which a toddler is briefly separated from a parent and left with a stranger, then reunited. The reunion is the key. Patterns she identified:

- **Secure.** The child is upset by the separation, seeks comfort on return, is quickly soothed, and goes back to playing. The parent serves as a safe base for exploring; see [[Curiosity]].
- **Avoidant.** The child shows little distress and ignores the parent on return, though measures of heart rate show stress.
- **Ambivalent or resistant.** The child is very distressed and seeks contact but can't be soothed, alternately clinging and pushing away.
- **Disorganized**, a category added later, describes contradictory or frightened behavior, often linked to frightening or abusive care.

Secure attachment is associated with caregivers who respond sensitively and consistently. It predicts, modestly, better emotional regulation and social skill later in childhood; see [[Emotional Regulation]] and [[Window of Tolerance]]. Attachment styles are not fixed traits; they can change with circumstances, relationships, and therapy, and the effect sizes linking infant attachment to adult outcomes are small; see [[Effect Size]].

In 1987 the researchers Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver extended the framework to adult romantic relationships, describing anxious and avoidant patterns of handling intimacy. Adult attachment research is now measured mostly by questionnaires on two dimensions, anxiety and avoidance, rather than by sorting people into types.

Popular culture has embraced attachment styles as personality labels, often diagnosing partners from a few behaviors. Researchers caution that this oversimplifies: adults vary along continua, behave differently in different relationships, and change over time. Labels can also become self-fulfilling; see [[Locus of Control]].

The core insight, that people regulate distress partly through others and that responsive relationships build the confidence to explore, appears across psychology: in sensitivity to rejection, in the comfort of company, and in feeling safe to take risks in groups; see [[Rejection Sensitivity]], [[Body Doubling]], and [[Psychological Safety]].
