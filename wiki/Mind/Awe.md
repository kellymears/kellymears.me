**Awe** is the emotion people feel in the presence of something vast that they can't immediately make sense of: a canyon, a storm, a starry sky, a cathedral, a piece of music, or an idea that changes how they see the world. It mixes wonder with a sense of being small, sometimes with a trace of fear.

In 2003, the psychologists Dacher Keltner and Jonathan Haidt proposed that awe has two core features. The first is **vastness**, something much larger than the self in size, power, complexity, or significance. The second is a **need for accommodation**: the experience doesn't fit existing understanding, so the mind has to stretch to take it in; see [[Predictive Processing]] and [[Curiosity]]. Philosophers had long described a related experience, the **sublime**, which Edmund Burke in 1757 tied to things vast, powerful, and a little frightening.

Researchers have linked awe to several effects:

- **The small self.** Awe shrinks attention to personal concerns. In one study, people who spent a minute gazing up at towering eucalyptus trees were more helpful afterward than people who looked at a building.
- **Time.** People feeling awe report having more time available and are more willing to give it to others.
- **Less rumination.** Awe pulls attention outward and may quiet self-focused thought; see [[Rumination]] and [[Default Mode Network]].
- **Openness to learning.** The need for accommodation can make people more curious and more aware of the gaps in their knowledge.

A 2020 study gave older adults weekly "awe walks," asking them to look at their surroundings with fresh eyes. Compared to ordinary walkers, they reported more joy and prosocial feelings, and their selfies over the weeks showed them taking up less of the frame. Awe overlaps with the restorative effects of nature on attention; see [[Attention Restoration Theory]].

Bodily signs include goosebumps, chills, widened eyes, and a slowed heart, possibly linked to activity of the [[Vagus Nerve]]. Music produces chills in many listeners at moments of surprise or release.

A famous version is the **overview effect**, the shift in perspective reported by astronauts seeing Earth from space: a small, fragile planet without visible borders. Many describe a lasting sense of connection and concern for the planet.

Awe has a darker side. Leaders, rallies, and spectacles can deliberately inspire awe, and the small self can make people more willing to submit to a group or cause. Threatening vastness, such as natural disasters, produces awe tinged with fear. Some researchers link awe to meaning-making in the face of mortality; see [[Terror Management Theory]].

Awe is one of several emotions studied as distinct from general happiness, which helps people describe their experiences more precisely; see [[Emotional Granularity]]. Unlike pleasures that fade with repetition, awe often renews itself with new encounters; see [[Hedonic Treadmill]] and [[Nostalgia]].
