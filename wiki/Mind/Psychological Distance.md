**Psychological distance** is how removed something feels from one's own here and now. Construal level theory, developed by the psychologists Yaacov Trope and Nira Liberman from the late 1990s, proposes that distance comes in four kinds and that all of them change how we think in the same way.

The four kinds are **time** (now versus next year), **space** (here versus far away), **social** (me versus a stranger), and **likelihood** (certain versus hypothetical). The theory's claim is that greater distance leads to more abstract thinking, focused on why and on central features, while closeness leads to concrete thinking, focused on how and on details. Asked about a trip next year, people think of adventure and rest; asked about one next week, they think of packing and connections.

This has practical consequences:

- **Plans.** Distant goals are judged by desirability and near ones by feasibility. Commitments that seemed appealing months ago become burdens when the date arrives, and plans made from a distance ignore the obstacles visible up close; see [[Planning Fallacy]] and [[Affective Forecasting]].
- **Self-control.** Distant rewards feel abstract and small next to concrete present temptations; see [[Hyperbolic Discounting]]. Thinking about goals abstractly can strengthen resistance to temptation, while turning a distant intention into concrete steps helps it happen; see [[Implementation Intention]].
- **Risk.** Problems that seem distant in time, place, and likelihood, such as slow environmental change, feel abstract and less urgent.

The same effect applies to giving advice. In **Solomon's paradox**, named for the biblical king who advised others wisely but managed his own life badly, people reason more wisely about others' problems than about their own. In 2014 Igor Grossmann and Ethan Kross found that people thinking about a friend's relationship conflict showed more humility and attention to other perspectives than when thinking about their own, and the difference vanished when people imagined their own problem from an outsider's view.

That led to **self-distancing**, a deliberate technique. Recalling a painful event as an observer, a fly on the wall, rather than reliving it reduces distress without avoiding it. Talking to oneself using one's own name or "you" rather than "I" has similar effects; see [[Self-talk]]. These help interrupt repetitive negative thought and support calmer regulation of emotion; see [[Rumination]] and [[Emotional Regulation]]. Cognitive therapy uses related techniques, such as asking what you'd tell a friend in the same situation; see [[Cognitive Behavioral Therapy]].

Explaining a problem aloud to someone, or to an object, may work partly by forcing the same outsider view; see [[Rubber Duck Debugging]].

Some construal level effects have proved weaker in larger replications, and the theory's breadth makes it hard to test as a whole; see [[Replication Crisis]]. The core pattern, that distance shifts attention from details to essentials, has held up well.
