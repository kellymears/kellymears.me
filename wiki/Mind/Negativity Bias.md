**Negativity bias** is the tendency for bad things to affect people more than good things of equal size. Criticism lingers longer than praise, one rude comment outweighs several kind ones, and a single bad experience can spoil an otherwise good day. In a 2001 review titled "Bad Is Stronger Than Good," Roy Baumeister and colleagues found the pattern across nearly every area of psychology they examined: close relationships, feedback, learning, impressions of other people, and health.

Paul Rozin and Edward Royzman broke it into parts:

- **Potency.** Negative events of the same size have more impact than positive ones.
- **Steeper gradients.** Negative things feel more urgent as they get closer in time or space.
- **Dominance.** Combining good and bad produces an overall impression more negative than the sum would suggest.
- **Contagion.** One bad thing contaminates many good ones. A cockroach briefly dipped in a bowl of soup ruins the soup; no single good ingredient can rescue a bad meal.

The usual explanation is evolutionary. Missing a meal costs little, while missing a predator can be fatal, so a nervous system tuned to notice threats would be favored even at the cost of false alarms. Psychiatrist Randolph Nesse called this the smoke detector principle: a detector that sometimes goes off for toast is better than one that sometimes misses a fire.

The bias shows up widely:

- **Decisions.** Losses outweigh equal gains, the special case known as [[Loss Aversion]].
- **Memory.** Negative events tend to be remembered in more detail, and reliving them feeds [[Rumination]].
- **Attention.** Threats and angry faces capture attention faster than neutral ones, which shapes how [[Selective Attention]] filters the world.
- **Feedback.** People need several positive interactions to balance one negative one. Relationship research once proposed a five-to-one ratio, though the exact figure is disputed. It matters in [[Code Review]] too, where blunt comments carry more weight than the reviewer intends.
- **Social sensitivity.** For some people, especially those with [[Rejection Sensitivity]], perceived criticism or rejection hits with outsized force.

Media and online platforms amplify it. Negative headlines draw more clicks, which rewards more negative coverage, and feeds tuned for engagement turn the bias into [[Doomscrolling]]. A string of vivid bad stories can make dangers seem far more common than they are, through the [[Availability Heuristic]], and fuel a [[Moral Panic]].

The bias isn't simply an error. Taking threats seriously is often wise, and dismissing bad news is its own mistake. But in safe modern settings it often over-fires. Practices that counter it include deliberately noticing good events, which some gratitude research supports, and the reframing techniques of [[Cognitive Behavioral Therapy]]. Organizations that respond to failure without blame, as in a [[Blameless Postmortem]], avoid teaching people to hide bad news.
