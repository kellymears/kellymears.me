**Rumination** is repeatedly and passively focusing on one's distress, its causes, and its consequences, without moving toward a solution. The word comes from the way cattle chew cud, bringing food back up to chew again. Ruminating thoughts circle: *Why do I feel this way? Why did I say that? What's wrong with me?* They feel like problem-solving but rarely produce anything except more of the same.

The psychologist Susan Nolen-Hoeksema developed the modern study of rumination from the late 1980s. Her *response styles theory* proposed that how people respond to a low mood shapes how long and deep it gets. Those who ruminate stay depressed longer and are more likely to develop major depression than those who distract themselves or take action. Her research also found women ruminate more than men on average, which may help explain higher rates of depression among women.

Researchers distinguish types:

- **Brooding** is moody comparison between one's situation and some standard, *Why can't I handle things better?* It is strongly linked to depression.
- **Reflection** is a more neutral analysis aimed at understanding. It can help in the long run, though it overlaps with brooding.
- **Worry** is rumination's future-oriented cousin, focused on what might go wrong rather than what did. Both are now often grouped as *repetitive negative thinking*, a process seen across depression, anxiety, insomnia, and other conditions.

Rumination does harm in several ways. It magnifies negative thinking, since a low mood makes negative memories easier to recall, a form of [[Context-Dependent Memory]] tied to mood. It impairs problem-solving, consuming [[Working Memory]] and attention. It saps motivation to act.

Brain research has linked rumination to the [[Default Mode Network]], the system active during self-referential thought and mind-wandering, which tends to be more active and harder to disengage in people prone to depression.

Approaches that help:

- **Distraction and action.** Engaging in an absorbing activity interrupts the loop. Activities that produce [[Flow]] or involve other people work especially well.
- **Mindfulness.** Noticing thoughts as passing events rather than truths to be analyzed, as trained in [[Mindfulness]] practice. Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy was designed to reduce relapse in recurrent depression.
- **Concrete thinking.** Shifting from abstract "why" questions to concrete "how" questions, such as what the next step is, moves thinking toward problem-solving.
- **Reappraisal.** Reinterpreting a situation, one of the main strategies of [[Emotional Regulation]].
- **Scheduled worry time.** Postponing worries to a set daily period can shrink their reach.

Rumination is common in [[Rejection Sensitivity]], where a perceived slight is replayed at length, and features in [[Autistic Burnout]] and [[Sensory Overload]] recovery for some people. Unfinished tasks also nag at attention, as in the [[Zeigarnik Effect]], and writing down a plan can quiet them.
