The **just-world hypothesis** is the tendency to believe that the world is fair, so that good things happen to good people and bad things to bad ones. The social psychologist Melvin Lerner described it in the 1960s after noticing how often people, including his own students, blamed the poor and the sick for their condition. The belief is comforting, because a fair world is predictable, but it leads people to conclude that those who suffer must have earned it.

Lerner and Carolyn Simmons demonstrated the effect in a 1966 experiment. Participants watched a woman who appeared to receive painful electric shocks for wrong answers in a learning task; she was actually an actor. When participants could do nothing to stop the shocks, they rated her more negatively, as less attractive and less likeable, than when they could arrange for her to be compensated. Faced with suffering they couldn't fix, observers restored their sense of fairness by deciding that the victim deserved less sympathy.

The belief serves real psychological needs. It supports a sense of control, the feeling that careful planning and hard work will pay off, and research links it with motivation and well-being; see [[Locus of Control]]. The idea that the world is random and unfair is threatening, especially when the suffering could happen to oneself; see [[Terror Management Theory]].

Its costs fall mainly on others:

- **Victim blaming.** Victims of crime, illness, or poverty are judged as careless, foolish, or morally weak; see [[Fundamental Attribution Error]].
- **Hindsight.** Once a bad outcome has happened, the warning signs seem obvious, so the victim should have seen it coming; see [[Hindsight Bias]].
- **Merit stories.** Success is credited to effort and talent, overlooking luck and advantage; see [[Matthew Effect]] and [[Survivorship Bias]].
- **Chronic illness.** Patients with conditions that can't be seen are told they would recover if they tried harder, which adds stigma to symptoms; see [[Spoon Theory]].

When suffering can be relieved, people with strong just-world beliefs often help. When it can't, they are more likely to reinterpret it. Reminding people that outcomes depend heavily on chance can reduce blame.

The belief can also turn inward. People who experience misfortune may blame themselves rather than accept that it was random, which can feel more bearable than helplessness but adds guilt; see [[Learned Helplessness]] and [[Self-Compassion]]. Clinicians sometimes address this in therapy; see [[Cognitive Behavioral Therapy]].

Many cultures and religions express versions of the belief, from karma to sayings about getting one's due. Holding onto the belief when evidence contradicts it is a form of [[Cognitive Dissonance]] reduction: changing the interpretation is easier than changing the worldview. Moral panics often follow the same logic, finding someone to blame when something goes wrong; see [[Moral Panic]].
