**Prospective memory** is remembering to do something in the future: take the medication at eight, buy milk on the way home, mention the bug in tomorrow's meeting. Most memory research studies retrospective memory, recalling what already happened. Prospective memory is the kind that fails most often in daily life, and the failure is usually not that the intention was forgotten but that it did not come to mind at the right moment.

Gilles Einstein and Mark McDaniel distinguished two kinds in 1990:

- **Event-based.** The intention is tied to a cue: "When I see Sam, return the book." The cue does much of the work, if it is noticed.
- **Time-based.** The intention is tied to a clock: "Call at three." With no external cue, it depends on checking the time, which is harder and fails more, especially under [[Time Blindness]].

Remembering at the right moment happens in two ways. *Monitoring* keeps the intention partly active, scanning for the cue, which consumes [[Working Memory]] and [[Executive Function]] and slows other work. *Spontaneous retrieval* lets the intention surface when a strong cue appears, at no ongoing cost. The [[Zeigarnik Effect]] is the felt side of monitoring: unfinished intentions nag, and that nagging is a tax. So is [[Attention Residue]] after switching away from a task with open threads.

Failures cluster around interruption and deviation from routine. A pilot defers a step to deal with a radio call, then never returns to it. On Northwest Airlines Flight 255 in 1987, the crew never extended the flaps, the taxi checklist that should have caught it was skipped, and the plane crashed on takeoff from Detroit. A surgeon closes with a sponge still inside. The task did not slip from memory; the trigger to recall it never came.

The defenses follow from the mechanism:

- **Externalize.** Write it down, set an alarm, put the book by the door. Offloading to the world, as in the [[Extended Mind]], frees monitoring capacity.
- **Strengthen the cue.** An [[Implementation Intention]] ("when I close my laptop, I will text Sam") links the intention to a specific event and shifts it from monitoring to spontaneous retrieval.
- **Use checklists.** Aviation and surgery rely on them because experts are no better at prospective memory under interruption than anyone else.
- **Place the cue where the action happens.** A reminder at the wrong place or time is noise, and noisy reminders train people to dismiss them, a form of [[Habituation]].

ADHD hits prospective memory hard, and the result is often mislabeled as not caring. The intention is real; the retrieval at the right moment is what fails. The fix is not to try harder but to build cues into the environment.
