**Motivated reasoning** is reasoning steered by a desired conclusion. People evaluate evidence and arguments less like scientists and more like lawyers, building a case for what they already believe or want to be true. The psychologist Ziva Kunda's 1990 review set out the idea: motivation affects reasoning, but within limits, since people need to feel they could justify their conclusions to a neutral observer.

That constraint shapes how it works. People rarely ignore evidence outright. Instead they apply **asymmetric standards**, asking of welcome evidence "can I believe this?" and of unwelcome evidence "must I believe this?" A study supporting their view is accepted; a study against it is scrutinized until a flaw appears. In a classic 1979 experiment, supporters and opponents of the death penalty read the same mixed studies and each side became more confident, finding the evidence on their side convincing and the rest flawed; see [[Confirmation Bias]].

Several mechanisms contribute:

- **Selective search.** People look for evidence likely to support them, and stop searching once they find enough; see [[Filter Bubble]].
- **Selective memory.** Supporting facts are remembered better.
- **Reinterpretation.** Ambiguous evidence is read in the favored direction.
- **Dissonance reduction.** Evidence that one's choices were mistaken is uncomfortable, so beliefs shift to justify them; see [[Cognitive Dissonance]] and [[Sunk Cost Fallacy]].

The motive is often identity rather than self-interest. The legal scholar Dan Kahan found that more numerate and scientifically literate people were sometimes more polarized on politically charged topics, using their skill to defend their group's position. Being wrong about an abstract fact costs an individual little; disagreeing with one's community can cost a lot; see [[Groupthink]].

The **backfire effect**, the claim that correcting a false belief makes people hold it more strongly, was reported in a 2010 study and widely cited. Larger follow-up studies found it rare: corrections usually move beliefs modestly in the right direction, even if they don't change attitudes or votes. The original finding is itself a lesson in early results outrunning evidence; see [[Replication Crisis]].

Everyone does it, and intelligence offers no protection. People readily see it in others while believing their own views simply reflect reality; see [[Naive Realism]]. Repeated exposure to a claim also makes it feel truer regardless of motive; see [[Illusory Truth Effect]].

Countermeasures that have some support:

1. **Consider the opposite.** Asking how one would judge the same evidence if it pointed the other way reduces bias.
2. **State the other side's best case** before rejecting it; see [[Steelmanning]].
3. **Make accuracy matter**, by keeping score of predictions or attaching stakes; see [[Superforecasting]].
4. **Separate identity from belief**, so changing a mind doesn't feel like betraying a group.
