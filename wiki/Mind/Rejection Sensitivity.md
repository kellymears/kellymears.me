**Rejection sensitivity** is a tendency to anxiously expect rejection, to perceive it readily in ambiguous situations, and to react to it intensely. Psychologists Geraldine Downey and Scott Feldman described it in 1996 as a measurable trait. People high in it read a delayed reply, a neutral face, or a short message as disapproval, and their reactions (withdrawal, anger, appeasement, or [[Rumination|rumination]]) can create the rejection they feared. It is associated with early experiences of rejection, and it predicts relationship difficulties and depression.

In ADHD circles a related term is common: **rejection sensitive dysphoria (RSD)**, popularized by the psychiatrist William Dodson to describe sudden, overwhelming emotional pain in response to perceived criticism or rejection. RSD is not a formal diagnosis, and research specifically on it is limited. Emotional dysregulation is well documented in ADHD, though, and many people with ADHD find the description strikingly accurate. Life experience plausibly contributes. Children with ADHD typically receive far more correction and criticism than their peers, an environment that would teach anyone to brace for it.

The mechanisms connect to familiar biases:

- **Ambiguity resolved negatively.** A missing reply has many causes, and a sensitized mind picks the painful one. It is [[Confirmation Bias]] applied to social cues, made worse by text, which strips tone.
- **The fundamental attribution error, turned inward.** Others' neutral behavior is read as a verdict on the self, the mirror of the [[Fundamental Attribution Error]].
- **Avoidance as protection.** Not applying, not sharing work, not asking. Avoiding the chance of rejection becomes a form of [[Learned Helplessness]] and narrows a life over time.
- **Overcompensation.** People-pleasing and perfectionism, often part of [[Masking]], are attempts to prevent criticism in advance.

What helps is mostly recognition and slowing down: naming the reaction ("this is the rejection feeling") before acting on it, checking the ambiguous evidence, and choosing environments that give explicit, kind feedback. Practices from code review culture apply directly. Feedback aimed at the work rather than the person, as in a [[Blameless Postmortem]], and written in clear [[Plain Language]] lowers the ambiguity that sensitized readers fill in for themselves.
