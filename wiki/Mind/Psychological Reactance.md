**Psychological reactance** is the motivation to restore a freedom that feels threatened or taken away. When people sense they are being pushed, told what to do, or denied an option, they tend to push back: by doing the forbidden thing, valuing it more, or rejecting the message along with the pressure. The psychologist Jack Brehm proposed the theory in 1966.

Reactance shows up in familiar ways:

- **Forbidden fruit.** Banned or restricted things become more desirable. Labels warning of violent content can make programs more appealing to some viewers, and censoring information draws attention to it; see [[Streisand Effect]].
- **Boomerang effect.** Strong persuasive messages can move people in the opposite direction. Anti-drinking campaigns using commanding language have sometimes increased intentions to drink among students.
- **Scarcity.** Items that become unavailable seem more valuable, which is why "limited time" offers work; see [[Loss Aversion]].
- **Romeo and Juliet effect.** A widely cited 1972 study found parental opposition associated with stronger romantic attachment, though later attempts to replicate it failed.

The strength of reactance depends on how important the threatened freedom is, how much is threatened, and how forceful the threat seems. Controlling language like "you must" and "you have to" provokes more of it than suggestions do. People also differ: some are much more reactance-prone than others, and it peaks in toddlerhood and adolescence, both periods when independence is being established.

Reactance connects to the human need for autonomy, one of the basic psychological needs in [[Self-Determination Theory]]. Being controlled undermines motivation even for things a person would otherwise want to do; see [[Locus of Control]]. For some autistic people, the response to demands, including self-imposed ones, is strong enough to block action entirely, a pattern discussed as [[Demand Avoidance]].

Reactance has practical consequences for anyone trying to change behavior:

- **Health messaging.** Campaigns that acknowledge choice ("the decision is yours") often persuade better than commands.
- **Interface design.** Nagging prompts, forced choices, and countdown pressure can drive people away; respectful defaults that preserve choice tend to work better; see [[Choice Architecture]] and [[Dark Pattern]].
- **Therapy and coaching.** Motivational interviewing, a counseling approach, avoids arguing for change, because arguing tends to make clients defend the status quo; the counselor draws out the client's own reasons instead.
- **Correcting beliefs.** Aggressive corrections of misinformation can entrench it among people who feel their identity is under attack, though the strong "backfire effect" turned out to be rarer than first reported; see [[Cognitive Dissonance]].

The simplest antidote is to restore the freedom: offer options, explain reasons, and make clear that the choice belongs to the other person. Even a short phrase like "but you are free to choose" has been found to raise compliance in some experiments, the reverse of reactance; see [[Framing Effect]].
