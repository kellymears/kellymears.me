**Context-dependent memory** is the finding that information is easier to recall in the same context in which it was learned. The surroundings, sounds, smells, even one's mood or physical state at the time of learning become linked to the memory, and they act as cues that help bring it back.

The best-known demonstration is a 1975 study by Duncan Godden and Alan Baddeley with scuba divers. Divers learned lists of words either on a beach or underwater, then were tested in the same place or the other one. Words learned underwater were recalled better underwater, and words learned on land were recalled better on land, by a margin of roughly a third.

Endel Tulving and Donald Thomson formalized the idea in 1973 as the **encoding specificity principle**: a retrieval cue helps only to the extent that it was encoded along with the original memory. A memory is not stored as an isolated fact but as an episode bound up with its circumstances.

Related forms:

- **State-dependent memory.** Recall is better in the same internal state as learning.
- **Mood-congruent memory.** People in a sad mood recall sad memories more easily, and happy moods favor happy ones. This can trap people in low moods, feeding [[Rumination]].
- **The doorway effect.** Walking through a doorway into another room makes people more likely to forget what they came for. Studies by Gabriel Radvansky suggest that crossing a boundary marks the end of one mental episode, so the intention is filed with the previous room.

The effects are real but usually modest in daily life. They are strongest when other cues are weak, as with free recall of lists, and shrink when people have good retrieval cues or understand the material deeply.

Still, the principle is useful:

- **Mental reinstatement.** Simply imagining the original setting in detail improves recall. Investigators use this in the cognitive interview, asking witnesses to picture the scene before describing it, which reduces the risk of [[False Memory]] compared with leading questions.
- **Varying study settings.** Learning in several places makes knowledge less tied to any one of them, a form of [[Desirable Difficulty]].
- **Habit design.** Behaviors are cued by context, which is why changing environments is a powerful way to break or build habits. See [[Habit Formation]] and [[Implementation Intention]].
- **Smell.** Odors are strong context cues, linked closely to memory and emotion, part of why a scent can trigger [[Nostalgia]].

The phenomenon fits a broader view of memory as reconstruction guided by cues rather than retrieval of stored recordings. Spatial memory techniques such as the [[Method of Loci]] exploit this by building a context deliberately. The [[Extended Mind]] view goes further, treating cues in the environment as part of how thinking works.
