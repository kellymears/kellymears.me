**Expert intuition** is the ability of experienced people to size up a situation and know what to do without deliberate reasoning. A chess master glances at a board and sees the right move; a nurse senses a patient is deteriorating before the monitors show it; a senior engineer reads a stack trace and points at the bug. It feels like a hunch, but it is recognition: the situation matches patterns stored from long experience.

The classic evidence comes from chess. Adriaan de Groot found in the 1940s that grandmasters did not search more moves than weaker players; they simply considered better ones first. In 1973 William Chase and Herbert Simon showed masters a real game position for five seconds and asked them to rebuild it. Masters placed far more pieces correctly than novices. But with pieces scattered at random, the advantage vanished. Experts were not remembering more pieces; they were remembering meaningful groups, *chunks*, such as a castled king with its pawn shield. Each chunk fits in one slot of a small [[Working Memory]], so experts effectively hold more.

Gary Klein studied firefighters, nurses, and military commanders in the 1980s and found they rarely compared options. Instead they recognized the situation as a familiar type, which brought to mind a plausible action, then simulated it mentally and acted unless the simulation revealed a problem. He called this the **recognition-primed decision** model. It describes the fast, automatic side of [[Dual Process Theory]] trained by experience.

Intuition is not always reliable. In 2009 Daniel Kahneman, skeptical of intuition, and Klein, its champion, wrote a joint paper on when it can be trusted, and largely agreed. Two conditions are needed:

- **A regular environment.** The situation must contain valid cues that actually predict outcomes. Chess, firefighting, and anesthesiology qualify. Stock picking and long-range political forecasting largely do not.
- **Practice with feedback.** The person needs extensive experience with quick, clear feedback on whether judgments were right, the conditions of [[Desirable Difficulty]] and deliberate practice.

Where these are missing, confident intuitions are often just [[Overfitting]] to noise, and confidence itself is no guide, as [[Dunning-Kruger Effect]] research suggests. Experts are also poor at explaining what they see, since the patterns are not stored as rules, which feeds the [[Curse of Knowledge]] and makes expertise hard to teach. Talking the reasoning through, as in [[Rubber Duck Debugging]], can surface the cues behind a hunch.

Expert patterns are also brittle outside their domain. The chess master's memory advantage disappears on random boards, and an expert in one system can be confidently wrong in a superficially similar one. Good [[Metacognition]] means knowing which environments one's intuition was trained in. The same chunking underlies physical skill, described under [[Motor Learning]].
