**Metacognition** is thinking about your own thinking: knowing what you know and don't know, noticing when you have lost the thread, and choosing a strategy to fix it. The psychologist John Flavell named it in the 1970s while studying how children learn to remember. It has two halves. *Monitoring* is the inward look: "Do I understand this? Will I remember it tomorrow?" *Control* is acting on the answer: rereading, slowing down, asking for help, or switching approach.

Monitoring is less accurate than it feels. People judge how well they know something mostly by how easily it comes to mind right now, and ease is a poor proxy for learning:

- **Illusion of fluency.** Rereading notes makes the material feel familiar, and familiarity is mistaken for mastery. Testing yourself feels harder and less productive but produces much more durable memory, which is why [[Spaced Repetition]] works despite feeling worse.
- **Overconfidence at the bottom.** The skills needed to do a task well are often the skills needed to judge whether it was done well, part of the argument behind the [[Dunning-Kruger Effect]].
- **Hindsight.** Once you understand something, you can't easily recall not understanding it, the [[Curse of Knowledge]].

Metacognition overlaps heavily with [[Executive Function]], since noticing that a plan has stalled is the first step in fixing it, and with [[Emotional Regulation]], which starts with recognizing what you feel. It competes for the same limited [[Working Memory]] as the task itself, so it tends to vanish under high [[Cognitive Load]], exactly when it is most needed.

Practical tools mostly externalize it, making the inner check visible:

- **Explaining aloud.** [[Rubber Duck Debugging]] works because putting reasoning into words forces gaps into view.
- **Predict, then check.** Guessing an answer before looking it up calibrates confidence over time.
- **Writing the plan down.** A checklist or [[Implementation Intention]] moves monitoring from memory onto paper.

The same distinction appears in software that reasons. A [[Large Language Model]] often states wrong answers with the same fluency as right ones, a form of [[Hallucination]], and research on model calibration asks whether a system can know when it doesn't know. [[Chain-of-Thought Prompting]] is a crude form of externalized monitoring. [[Dual Process Theory]] frames the human case: metacognition is part of what decides when a fast intuitive answer needs a slower second look.
