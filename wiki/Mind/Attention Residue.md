**Attention residue** is the part of attention that stays with a previous task after you switch to a new one. The organizational researcher Sophie Leroy named it in 2009. People who moved from an unfinished task to a new one performed worse on the new one, because part of their mind was still working on the old. Finishing, or deliberately parking the first task, reduced the effect.

It is one piece of a broader finding: **task switching is not free**. Laboratory studies since the 1990s show that switching between even simple tasks costs time and raises errors compared with staying on one, and the cost grows with task complexity. Much of that cost is rebuilding [[Working Memory]]: reloading the goal, the rules, and where you were. For deep work such as writing, debugging, or design, the reloading can take many minutes. A two-minute interruption therefore costs far more than two minutes. This is the same economics as a context switch in a [[CPU Scheduler]], where the expensive part is not the switch itself but the cold caches afterwards.

Several practices follow:

- **Batch shallow work.** Messages, email, and reviews handled in blocks cost one switch, not dozens.
- **Protect long uninterrupted blocks.** Paul Graham's essay on the *maker's schedule* argues that one meeting in the middle of an afternoon destroys it for someone who works in half-day units, while costing a manager on an hourly schedule almost nothing.
- **Park explicitly.** Writing down where you stopped and what comes next before switching reduces residue, because the open loop has somewhere to go.
- **Treat notifications as interruptions,** which they are. Each one is a small forced switch, and constant badges feed the same loop as [[Doomscrolling]].

"Multitasking" mostly means rapid switching, with its costs hidden. Real parallelism happens only when tasks use different resources and one is automatic, like walking while talking. It is the [[Concurrency and Parallelism]] distinction applied to people. Minds differ in switching cost, too. For people whose attention runs [[Monotropism|monotropically]], transitions are especially expensive, and a day of interruptions can drain [[Executive Function]] without any single task being hard.
