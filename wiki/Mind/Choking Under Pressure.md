**Choking under pressure** is performing worse than your usual level precisely when it matters most. A golfer who sinks a putt a thousand times in practice misses it to win; a student who knows the material blanks in an exam. Choking is not simply nerves, since some arousal helps performance, as the [[Yerkes-Dodson Law]] describes. It is the specific failure that comes from how pressure redirects attention.

Psychologist Sian Beilock's research identifies two main mechanisms, and they affect different kinds of tasks:

- **Distraction.** Worry about the outcome takes up [[Working Memory]], leaving less for the task. This hurts thinking tasks such as math problems and exam questions. Counterintuitively, people with the most working memory often choke most, since they rely on it most and lose the most when worry takes a share.
- **Explicit monitoring.** Pressure makes people pay close attention to skills that normally run automatically. A practiced golf swing or tennis serve is stored as procedural skill, described under [[Motor Learning]], and conscious step-by-step control disrupts it. Experts suffer this more than novices, whose skills are still under conscious control anyway.

These map onto the two systems of [[Dual Process Theory]]: pressure either starves the slow, deliberate system or drags it into territory the fast system handles better. [[Expert Intuition]] works because it bypasses deliberate thought, which is exactly what explicit monitoring undoes. [[Flow]] is in a sense choking's opposite: total absorption with no self-monitoring.

The **yips** are an extreme form: sudden, involuntary loss of a fine motor skill, such as short putts in golf or routine throws in baseball. Some cases seem driven by anxiety and monitoring; others resemble focal dystonia, a neurological movement disorder. Either way, trying harder doesn't bring the skill back.

Research suggests several remedies:

- **Practice under pressure.** Training with mild stakes, an audience, or a clock makes real pressure feel familiar.
- **Write it out.** In a 2011 study, students who wrote about their exam worries for ten minutes beforehand scored better than those who didn't, apparently by unloading worry from working memory.
- **External focus.** Attending to the target or the effect of a movement, rather than the body, keeps automatic skills automatic.
- **Routines.** A consistent pre-performance routine occupies attention that would otherwise turn to monitoring.
- **Reappraise arousal.** Telling yourself "I'm excited" instead of trying to calm down improved performance in some studies, a technique related to [[Emotional Regulation]].

Choking also connects to [[Ironic Process Theory]]: telling yourself not to miss puts the miss in mind. Fear of being exposed, central to [[Impostor Syndrome]], can raise the stakes of ordinary situations enough to trigger it. A strong sense of [[Self-Efficacy]] seems to buffer against it, perhaps by lowering the threat the situation poses.
