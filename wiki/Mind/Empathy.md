**Empathy** is the capacity to understand and share what another person is feeling. The English word is young. Psychologist Edward Titchener coined it in 1909 to translate the German Einfühlung, "feeling into," a term first used for the way people project themselves into art and landscape.

Researchers usually split it in two:

- **Cognitive empathy** is working out what someone else feels or thinks, a skill that overlaps heavily with [[Theory of Mind]].
- **Affective empathy** is feeling something of their state yourself, wincing at another person's injury or tearing up at their grief.

The two can come apart. A skilled negotiator or a con artist may read others well while feeling little, and some people are flooded by others' feelings yet misread what caused them. Below both sits [[Emotional Contagion]], the automatic spread of moods through faces, voices and posture, which appears even in infants who cry when other infants cry. [[Mirror Neurons]] were once proposed as the neural basis for all of this, a claim that has been considerably scaled back.

Empathy also has well-documented limits. It flows most easily toward people who seem similar or close, and it fades with distance in space, time and social group; see [[Psychological Distance]]. It is drawn to individuals rather than numbers, so a single named child can move donors more than thousands of anonymous ones; see [[Identifiable Victim Effect]]. And people misjudge states they are not currently in, the **empathy gap**, underestimating how hunger, pain or fear would change their own choices, let alone someone else's; see [[Empathy gap]].

Those limits led psychologist Paul Bloom to argue in Against Empathy (2016) that emotional empathy is a poor moral guide, a spotlight that illuminates whoever it happens to land on. Defenders reply that it is the motive behind much helping, and that the answer is to pair it with reasoning rather than discard it.

A related line of research separates empathy from compassion. Feeling another person's pain intensely can produce **empathic distress**, an aversive state that pushes people to withdraw to protect themselves. Compassion, caring about the suffering without fully taking it on, is associated with warmer feelings and more sustained helping. Training studies by Tania Singer and colleagues found that compassion practice shifted brain and emotional responses in that direction. The difference matters for caregivers, nurses and therapists, whose exhaustion is often described as [[Compassion fatigue]]; turning the same care inward is the subject of [[Self-Compassion]].

Empathy also runs in both directions. The [[Double empathy problem]] proposes that misunderstandings between autistic and non-autistic people are a mutual gap in understanding, not a deficit on one side only.
