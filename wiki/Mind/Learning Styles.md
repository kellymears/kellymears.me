**Learning styles** are the idea that people learn best in different modes, such as visual, auditory, reading and writing, or hands-on, and that teaching should be matched to each learner's style. The idea is enormously popular. Surveys of teachers in several countries have found large majorities endorsing it, and style questionnaires are common in schools and corporate training. Its central claim has not held up.

A widely used scheme, VARK, introduced by Neil Fleming in New Zealand around 1987, sorts learners into visual, aural, read/write and kinesthetic types. Such schemes rest on two claims: that people differ in how they prefer to receive information, and that matching instruction to those preferences improves learning. The second claim is called the **meshing hypothesis**.

The first claim is true. People do have preferences, and some report vivid mental imagery while others report almost none; see [[Aphantasia]]. The second is the problem. In 2008, Harold Pashler, Mark McDaniel, Doug Rohrer and Robert Bjork reviewed the evidence and set out what a proper test needs: classify learners by style, teach each group in matched and mismatched ways, and show that matched teaching works better for each. They found almost no studies with that design, and the few that existed mostly contradicted the hypothesis.

Why does the idea persist? It flatters both teachers and students, suggesting everyone has a hidden strength. Questionnaire results feel accurate because they describe preferences people already know they have; see [[Barnum Effect]]. Teachers notice cases that fit and forget ones that don't; see [[Confirmation Bias]]. And learning in a preferred mode feels easier, which people mistake for learning better; see [[Processing Fluency]].

What actually helps tends to apply to everyone. The best mode depends on the content, not the learner: geography needs maps, music needs sound, and a skill like swimming needs practice. Combining words with relevant diagrams helps most people. Retrieval practice, spacing study over time, and mixing problem types improve retention across learners, often by making study feel harder; see [[Spaced Repetition]] and [[Desirable Difficulty]]. Thinking about meaning rather than surface features helps too; see [[Levels of Processing]].

None of this denies that individuals differ. Prior knowledge matters a great deal, and teaching that's pitched just beyond what a learner can already do works best; see [[Zone of Proximal Development]]. Specific conditions change what works, which is why accommodations for dyslexia are evidence-based in a way that style matching isn't; see [[Dyslexia]] and [[Accessibility]].

Learning styles are often grouped with other **neuromyths**, popular beliefs about the brain that sound scientific, such as the claims that people use only ten percent of their brains or are either left-brained or right-brained. Teaching how memory actually works helps students judge their own learning more accurately; see [[Metacognition]].
