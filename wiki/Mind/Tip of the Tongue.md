A **tip-of-the-tongue state** is the experience of being sure you know a word, often a name, without being able to say it. You can feel it is close. You may know its first letter, how many syllables it has, or what it sounds like, while the word itself will not come. It happens roughly once a week to young adults and more often with age, and proper names are the most frequent culprit.

Roger Brown and David McNeill studied it in 1966 by reading out dictionary definitions of rare words, such as a small boat used in the harbors of China, and asking people for the word (*sampan*). Those caught in the state could often report the first letter and syllable count, and offered similar-sounding guesses like *Saipan* and *Siam*. The word was partly retrievable, which showed that knowing a word is not all-or-nothing.

The leading explanation is that the brain stores a word's meaning and its sound separately. Speaking requires going from the concept to the sound, and the tip-of-the-tongue state is a failure of that final link. The *transmission deficit* account proposes that connections to rarely used sounds weaken, which fits why names and uncommon words are most affected and why the states increase with age. Bilingual people have more of them, plausibly because each language gets less use. Deaf signers report a parallel "tip of the fingers" state, recalling a sign's handshape but not its movement, so the split is not about speech as such.

Several practical patterns follow:

- **Blockers make it worse.** A wrong word that keeps coming to mind crowds out the right one, and repeatedly forcing retrieval strengthens the blocker.
- **Walking away helps.** The word often pops up later, unprompted. Shifting attention lets the blocker fade, and the [[Default Mode Network]] may keep working in the background.
- **Partial cues help.** Hearing the first sound often unlocks it, as do cues from the original setting, per [[Context-Dependent Memory]].
- **Successful retrieval sticks.** As with [[Spaced Repetition]], a hard-won recall strengthens the word's path, one of the [[Desirable Difficulty]] effects.

The state is a clear case of [[Metacognition]], knowing about your own knowledge. You can be confident something is stored before you can access it, and that feeling of knowing is usually right. It also strains [[Working Memory]], which has to hold the meaning, the partial sound, and the rejected guesses at once.

Its opposite, [[Semantic Satiation]], is when an accessible word loses its meaning through repetition. Both show that meaning and form are joined by connections that can weaken or tire. Vector [[Embedding]] search gives machines a version of the reverse trick, finding a word from a description of its meaning.
