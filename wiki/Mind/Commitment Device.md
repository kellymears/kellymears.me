A **commitment device** is a choice made now that limits, or adds a cost to, choices made later. It's a way for the planning self to overrule the self who will actually be in the moment. The classic image is Odysseus, who had his crew tie him to the mast so he could hear the Sirens without steering onto the rocks, which is why such arrangements are also called **Ulysses contracts**.

Commitment devices exist because preferences shift predictably as rewards get closer. From a distance, people prefer the larger, later reward; up close, the smaller, sooner one wins; see [[Hyperbolic Discounting]]. Someone who knows this about themselves can act while the distant view still holds.

They come in several strengths:

- **Removal.** Make the tempting option unavailable: don't keep snacks at home, delete the app, use a site blocker, leave the credit card behind.
- **Penalty.** Put something at stake. Services let people pledge money that goes to a charity, or even a cause they dislike, if they miss a goal.
- **Friction.** Make the unwanted choice slower, without forbidding it; see [[Friction]].
- **Social.** Tell others the goal, work alongside someone, or schedule a session that someone else depends on; see [[Body Doubling]].
- **Automatic.** Arrange for the right thing to happen by default, as with automatic savings transfers; see [[Choice Architecture]].

A well-known field study by Nava Ashraf, Dean Karlan, and Wesley Yin offered savings accounts in the Philippines that locked funds until a goal date or amount. Many chose them despite earning no extra interest, and after a year their savings were substantially higher than a comparison group's. People will pay to restrict themselves when they understand their own patterns.

Software has its own commitment devices. A CI pipeline that blocks merges until tests pass is one: the team decided in calm conditions that no deadline would override it; see [[Continuous Integration]]. Pre-commit hooks, branch protection rules, and error budgets work the same way, binding a stressed future team to a policy chosen in advance; see [[Service Level Objective]].

Commitment devices have costs. Rigid ones can't adapt when circumstances change, and penalties can turn a goal into a source of anxiety. People often underuse them, being overconfident about their future willpower, or overuse them, binding themselves to plans that should be revised; see [[Sunk Cost Fallacy]]. For people with [[ADHD]] or other differences in [[Executive Function]], external structure is often more reliable than intentions alone, and choosing such structure is a strategy, not a failure.

The milder relative of the commitment device is the plan: deciding in advance exactly when and where to act; see [[Implementation Intention]]. Pairing a plan with a small commitment, like a set time and someone expecting the result, is often enough.
