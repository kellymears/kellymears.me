**Social comparison** is the process of evaluating oneself by looking at other people. The psychologist Leon Festinger formalized it in 1954, proposing that people have a drive to evaluate their opinions and abilities, and when there is no objective measure, they compare themselves to others, especially to people similar to themselves.

Comparisons go in two directions:

- **Upward**, to people doing better. It can inspire and teach, showing what is possible, but often lowers mood and self-esteem.
- **Downward**, to people doing worse. It tends to comfort, and people under threat seek it out, but it offers little to learn from.

Context determines the effect. Comparing with someone close and similar, in an area that matters to one's identity, hurts most when they do better.

Notable findings:

- **Bronze beats silver.** A 1995 study of Olympic medalists found bronze winners looked happier than silver winners. Silver medalists compared themselves with gold; bronze medalists with finishing off the podium.
- **The big-fish-little-pond effect.** Herbert Marsh found that students of equal ability have lower academic self-concept in selective schools than in ordinary ones, because they compare themselves with stronger classmates. The effect has been replicated across dozens of countries.
- **Relative income.** Studies of wellbeing find that people's satisfaction depends partly on their income relative to neighbors and peers, not only on its absolute level, which may help explain why rising national incomes don't raise happiness as much as expected; see [[Hedonic Treadmill]]. Spending to keep up with others is part of what drives status goods; see [[Veblen Good]].

Social media intensifies comparison by presenting the curated highlights of many lives at once, compared against one's own unedited experience. Research links passive scrolling and frequent upward comparison to lower wellbeing, though causality is debated; see [[Doomscrolling]]. Influencers and celebrities supply comparison targets that feel personal but aren't peers; see [[Parasocial Relationship]].

Comparison feeds several familiar struggles. People who feel like frauds tend to compare their inner doubts against others' polished exteriors; see [[Impostor Syndrome]]. Sensitivity to rejection makes unfavorable comparisons sting more; see [[Rejection Sensitivity]]. People overestimate how much others notice their own flaws; see [[Spotlight Effect]].

Ways to blunt the harm:

- **Compare with yourself over time** rather than with others; see [[Growth Mindset]].
- **Treat others' success as information**: what did they do that could be learned? Watching similar people succeed can raise belief in one's own ability; see [[Self-Efficacy]].
- **Respond to shortfalls with kindness** rather than self-criticism; see [[Self-Compassion]].

Games and apps exploit comparison deliberately through leaderboards and rankings; see [[Matchmaking Rating]]. Other people's choices also guide behavior directly, which is a different mechanism; see [[Social Proof]].
