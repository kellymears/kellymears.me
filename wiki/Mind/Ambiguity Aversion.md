**Ambiguity aversion** is the preference for known risks over unknown ones. Given a choice between a gamble with clear odds and one whose odds are uncertain, most people pick the clear one, even when they have no reason to think the uncertain gamble is worse. It's different from ordinary risk aversion, which concerns how much variation in outcomes people will accept; ambiguity aversion concerns how much they dislike not knowing the odds.

The economist Frank Knight drew the underlying distinction in 1921. **Risk** describes situations where probabilities are known, like a fair die. **Uncertainty**, often called Knightian uncertainty, describes situations where they aren't, like the chance that a new technology succeeds; see [[Black Swan]].

Daniel Ellsberg demonstrated the preference in 1961 with what became known as the **Ellsberg paradox**. An urn holds 30 red balls and 60 balls that are black or yellow in an unknown mix. Most people would rather bet on drawing red than on drawing black, which implies they think black is less likely than red. Yet most also prefer betting on black-or-yellow, whose combined chance is known to be two in three, over red-or-yellow, which is uncertain. No single belief about the number of black balls makes both choices consistent. People aren't estimating odds and acting on them; they're avoiding the ambiguous bet both times. That breaks the standard model in which rational choices follow from probabilities and values; see [[Expected Value]].

Craig Fox and Amos Tversky found in 1995 that the aversion is strongest when an ambiguous option is directly compared with a clear one, and weak when people judge it on its own. People also become ambiguity seeking when the known probability of a gain is small, preferring a vague chance over a clearly tiny one.

The preference shows up widely. Investors favor familiar domestic stocks over foreign ones they understand less. Patients may stick with a known treatment rather than try a new one with an uncertain but possibly better record. Organizations keep existing systems because their failures are at least familiar; see [[Loss Aversion]]. Some people avoid checking information that might resolve an uncertainty, which is a different response to the same discomfort; see [[Ostrich Effect]]. A strong need for definite answers makes ambiguity especially unpleasant; see [[Need for Closure]].

Ambiguity aversion isn't always a mistake. When odds are truly unknown, a cautious margin protects against having estimated them badly, an argument used for regulation of new hazards; see [[Precautionary Principle]]. Explicit ranges of uncertainty can make ambiguity easier to reason about, as when software estimates are given as spans that narrow over time; see [[Cone of Uncertainty]]. Treating unknown odds as beliefs to be updated with evidence is the core of Bayesian reasoning; see [[Bayesian Inference]].
