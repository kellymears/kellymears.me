**Attention restoration theory** proposes that the kind of attention we use to concentrate on purpose wears out with use, and that certain environments, especially natural ones, let it recover. The psychologists Rachel and Stephen Kaplan developed it in their 1989 book *The Experience of Nature*.

The Kaplans distinguished two kinds of attention. *Directed attention* is effortful: holding focus on a spreadsheet while ignoring the conversation at the next desk. It is closely tied to [[Executive Function]] and to the inhibition side of [[Selective Attention]], and it tires with use, a state they called **directed attention fatigue**: irritability, distractibility, and worse self-control. *Involuntary attention*, or fascination, is captured without effort, by things inherently interesting.

The key idea is **soft fascination**: settings that hold attention gently, like clouds, leaves moving in the wind, or water, leaving room for reflection while directed attention rests. Hard fascination, like a car chase or a feed, also captures attention effortlessly but fills it completely, leaving no room to recover. Restorative environments, in the Kaplans' account, have four qualities:

- **Being away.** A sense of distance from everyday demands, physical or psychological.
- **Extent.** Enough richness and coherence to feel like a whole other world.
- **Soft fascination.** Effortless, undemanding interest.
- **Compatibility.** The setting supports what the person wants to do.

Evidence is encouraging but uneven. In a well-known 2008 study, people performed better on a backward digit-span test after a walk in an arboretum than after a walk through city streets. Meta-analyses find the clearest benefits on working-memory and attention-switching tasks and weaker or inconsistent effects elsewhere, and the field shares the small-sample problems behind the [[Replication Crisis]]. A rival account, stress reduction theory, credits nature with lowering stress directly rather than resting attention.

The framework still offers a practical lens on breaks. A break spent scrolling is hard fascination. It may feel like rest but keeps attention fully occupied, which is part of why [[Doomscrolling]] feels draining. A short walk outside or staring out a window comes closer to restoration. This adds a second argument for timed breaks in the [[Pomodoro Technique]], alongside clearing [[Attention Residue]], and it suggests why wandering thought, associated with the [[Default Mode Network]], is part of recovery rather than wasted time.

For people prone to [[Sensory Overload]] or [[Autistic Burnout]], calm, predictable surroundings matter for the same reason. Every unfiltered stimulus spends directed attention, drawing on the budget [[Spoon Theory]] describes. Lighting that follows the day, as in [[Circadian Lighting]], is one modest attempt to make indoor spaces less draining.
