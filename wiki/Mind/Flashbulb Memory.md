A **flashbulb memory** is a vivid recollection of the circumstances in which someone learned of a surprising, emotionally significant event: where they were, who told them, what they were doing, sometimes what they were wearing. People often say they remember it "as if it were yesterday." The name suggests a photograph taken in a burst of light and preserved intact, ready to be consulted at will.

The psychologists Roger Brown and James Kulik coined the term in 1977. They asked people about the assassination of President Kennedy in 1963 and found that most could describe the moment they heard in rich detail. Brown and Kulik proposed a special memory mechanism, triggered by surprise and personal significance, that recorded the scene wholesale.

Later research cast doubt on the photograph. The morning after the Challenger space shuttle exploded in January 1986, the psychologist Ulric Neisser and a colleague asked students to write down how they had heard the news. About two and a half years later, they asked the same students again. Many of the later accounts differed substantially from the originals, sometimes in where the student had been or who had told them. Yet the students were confident in the later versions, and some, shown their own original notes, preferred their current memory.

A study begun the day after the September 11 attacks in 2001 compared students' memories of hearing the news with memories of an ordinary event from the same days. Over the following months, both kinds of memory became less consistent at about the same rate. What differed was confidence and vividness: the flashbulb memories felt accurate long after they had drifted.

Several processes explain the pattern:

- **Emotional arousal** strengthens memory for the central facts of an event, partly through stress hormones, but does less for peripheral details; see [[Fight-or-Flight Response]] and [[Memory Consolidation]].
- **Rehearsal** through repeated retelling and media coverage reshapes the story each time it's recalled, and details from later coverage blend in; see [[Source Amnesia]] and [[False Memory]].
- **Hindsight** reorganizes the memory around what the event turned out to mean; see [[Hindsight Bias]].

Distinctiveness also matters. Events that stand out sharply from routine are remembered better; see [[Von Restorff Effect]]. Flashbulb memories cluster in youth and early adulthood, when many people form their most lasting recollections; see [[Reminiscence Bump]].

The lesson extends beyond psychology. Vivid, confident recollection is weak evidence of accuracy, a point with consequences for eyewitness testimony. Two people can hold sharply different, equally certain memories of the same moment; see [[Rashomon Effect]] and [[Naive Realism]]. Shared memories of public events also help bind people into communities; see [[Imagined Community]].
