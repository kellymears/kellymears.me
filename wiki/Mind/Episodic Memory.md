**Episodic memory** is memory for specific experiences: what happened, where, and when, recalled as events you lived through. Remembering your last birthday dinner is episodic; knowing that Paris is the capital of France is not. The psychologist Endel Tulving drew the distinction in 1972, separating episodic memory from **semantic memory**, the store of facts and meanings detached from when they were learned.

Both are forms of long-term memory you can consciously recall, as opposed to skills and habits that run without conscious recollection; see [[Motor Learning]] and [[Habit Formation]]. They also feed each other: repeated episodes become general knowledge, so the details of individual meals at a restaurant blur into knowing what the restaurant is like.

The brain's key structure for forming episodic memories is the **hippocampus**. The best-known evidence is the patient Henry Molaison, known for decades as H.M., who had both hippocampi removed in 1953 to treat epilepsy. He could no longer form new episodic memories, though he kept his intelligence and could learn new motor skills without remembering the practice. Memories are first held by the hippocampus and gradually become stored across the cortex, especially during sleep; see [[Memory Consolidation]].

Episodic memory is reconstructive, not a recording. Each recall rebuilds the event from fragments and fills gaps with expectations, so memories shift over time and can be distorted or implanted; see [[False Memory]]. Even vivid "flashbulb" memories of shocking news, which feel exact, change substantially over the years while confidence stays high. Memories return more easily in the setting where they were formed; see [[Context-Dependent Memory]].

Other features:

- **Childhood amnesia.** Adults remember almost nothing from before about age three or four, probably because the hippocampus and language were still developing.
- **Reminiscence bump.** Older adults recall disproportionately many memories from roughly ages 10 to 30, the years when identity forms.
- **Self.** Personal memories make up a life story that supports a sense of who you are.

Tulving called episodic memory **mental time travel**, and the same system runs forward: imagining future events uses largely the same brain regions as remembering past ones. People with hippocampal damage struggle to imagine new scenarios in detail as well as to remember. This suggests a reason memory is reconstructive: it is built for recombining past pieces to simulate what might happen, not for archiving; see [[Prospective Memory]].

Episodic memory also explains why experiences feel the way they do in retrospect. What remains of an episode is a summary weighted toward its peaks and endings; see [[Peak-End Rule]]. And events that are distinctive or emotional get stored more strongly than routine ones; see [[Von Restorff Effect]]. Memory techniques work by turning abstract material into vivid episodes in imagined places; see [[Method of Loci]].
