**The McGurk effect** is an illusion in which what people see changes what they hear. In the classic demonstration, a video shows a face saying "ga" while the soundtrack plays "ba." Many viewers hear "da," a sound that's in neither the picture nor the audio. Closing the eyes restores "ba." Opening them brings "da" back.

The psychologists Harry McGurk and John MacDonald described it in a 1976 paper in Nature titled "Hearing lips and seeing voices." They came upon it by accident while studying how infants perceive speech, when a technician dubbed the wrong sound onto a video.

The illusion shows that speech perception isn't purely auditory. The brain combines the sound with the visible movements of lips, teeth, and tongue, and produces a single best guess about what was said; see [[Predictive Processing]] and [[Bayesian Inference]]. When the two signals conflict, the result can be a compromise. Lip reading helps everyone in noisy rooms, where seeing a speaker's face makes words noticeably easier to understand; see [[Cocktail party effect]].

Part of what makes the effect striking is that it persists after people understand it. Knowing the trick doesn't stop the perception, which suggests the blending happens early and automatically, before conscious reasoning; see [[Dual Process Theory]]. Visual illusions behave the same way.

Not everyone experiences it equally. Some people perceive the blended syllable almost every time, others rarely. Studies have reported differences across individuals, ages, and language backgrounds, though findings across languages are mixed. People who rely more on lip reading, such as many with hearing loss, may weight visual information more heavily.

Related cross-sensory effects:

- **Ventriloquism.** A voice seems to come from a moving mouth rather than its true source, which is why a puppet's mouth or a film actor's lips capture the sound.
- **Body illusions.** Seeing a fake hand touched in sync with one's own can make it feel like part of the body; see [[Rubber Hand Illusion]].
- **Mishearing lyrics**, where expectations reshape ambiguous sounds; see [[Mondegreen]].
- **Editing**, where the same footage takes different meanings from what surrounds it; see [[Kuleshov Effect]].

The effect has practical consequences. Audio and video that drift out of sync become unpleasant to watch, and badly dubbed or poorly lip-synced speech can be harder to follow; see [[Subtitling]]. Synthetic video that misaligns sound and mouth movement can look subtly wrong, one of the cues used to spot manipulated footage; see [[Deepfake]]. Researchers have studied whether people with differences in sensory integration, including some autistic people, experience the effect differently; see [[Sensory Processing]].

More broadly, the McGurk effect is one of the clearest demonstrations that perception is a construction assembled from many sources, not a direct recording of the world; see [[Pareidolia]].
