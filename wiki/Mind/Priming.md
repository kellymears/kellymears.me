**Priming** is the effect of one stimulus on the processing of a later one. After seeing the word *doctor*, people recognize *nurse* a few milliseconds faster than *bread*. After hearing a word once, they recognize it faster the second time. The earlier exposure readies related material, often without awareness.

The classic demonstration is **semantic priming**, reported by David Meyer and Roger Schvaneveldt in 1971. People decided whether letter strings were words, and were faster when a word followed a related one. The usual explanation is **spreading activation**: concepts are linked in memory like a network, and activating one partly activates its neighbors; see [[Knowledge Graph]]. **Repetition priming**, faster processing of something encountered before, is closely related to the ease that makes familiar things feel pleasant and true; see [[Processing Fluency]] and [[Mere-Exposure Effect]].

These basic effects are small, fast, and robust. They have been replicated thousands of times and underpin a great deal of memory research. They fit the view of the brain as constantly predicting what comes next, using recent input to tilt expectations; see [[Predictive Processing]].

The trouble began with **social priming**, the claim that subtle cues could change complex behavior. In a famous 1996 study by John Bargh and colleagues, students who unscrambled sentences containing words associated with old age, like *Florida* and *wrinkle*, supposedly walked more slowly down a corridor afterward. Other studies claimed that money images made people less helpful, or that thinking about professors improved trivia scores.

Many of these findings didn't hold up. A 2012 replication of the walking study found the effect only when experimenters expected it, suggesting their expectations shaped the result. Large coordinated replications of other social priming effects mostly found nothing. Daniel Kahneman, who had featured such studies prominently in *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, wrote an open letter calling the field a "train wreck looming" and later said he had placed too much faith in underpowered studies. Social priming became a central case of the [[Replication Crisis]], with small samples and flexible analyses producing results that looked solid; see [[Statistical Power]] and [[P-Hacking]].

The lesson isn't that context doesn't matter. Some related effects remain well supported: the first number seen still pulls estimates toward it, and the way a choice is worded still shifts decisions; see [[Anchoring]] and [[Framing Effect]]. The lesson is that tiny cues rarely produce large changes in deliberate behavior, and that surprising claims need surprising evidence.

Priming has a direct counterpart in language models, which continue text in the direction its earlier content sets. Examples and phrasing in a prompt prime the output, which is why careful wording and well-chosen examples matter; see [[Prompt Engineering]] and [[In-Context Learning]].
