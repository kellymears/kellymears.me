**Self-efficacy** is a person's belief in their ability to carry out a particular task or handle a particular situation. Albert Bandura introduced the idea in 1977. It is specific: someone can have high self-efficacy for cooking a dinner party and low self-efficacy for public speaking. That makes it different from self-esteem, which is a general sense of worth, and from optimism, which is about how things will turn out rather than what you can do.

It matters because it shapes behavior before any ability is tested. People with higher self-efficacy for a task choose to attempt it, set harder goals, put in more effort, and persist longer after setbacks. Those with low self-efficacy avoid the task, quit early, and read difficulty as proof of incapacity. The belief becomes partly self-fulfilling, since effort and persistence feed actual skill.

Bandura identified four sources, in rough order of strength:

- **Mastery experiences.** Succeeding at the task, especially after effort, is the strongest builder. Easy wins help less than hard-won ones, and early failures before any foundation is laid hurt most.
- **Vicarious experience.** Watching someone similar succeed makes it seem possible. The closer the model is to you, the stronger the effect.
- **Verbal persuasion.** Encouragement helps a little if it is credible, and fades quickly when experience contradicts it.
- **Physiological and emotional states.** A racing heart before a talk can be read as fear or as readiness. How you interpret arousal shapes the belief, which links it to [[Interoception]].

The practical lesson is to engineer mastery. Break a task into steps small enough to succeed at, then raise the challenge, which is how a good [[Difficulty Curve]] teaches a game and how [[Desirable Difficulty]] avoids tipping into defeat. An [[Implementation Intention]] lowers the cost of the first step.

Self-efficacy sits between several neighbors:

- **[[Learned Helplessness]]** is its collapse: after repeated uncontrollable failure, a person stops trying even when success becomes possible.
- **[[Self-Determination Theory]]** names competence as a core psychological need, the felt side of self-efficacy.
- **[[Impostor Syndrome]]** is self-efficacy that does not update: success is attributed to luck, so mastery experiences never register.
- **[[Dunning-Kruger Effect]]** is the opposite miscalibration, belief running ahead of skill.

Low self-efficacy is a quiet driver of [[Procrastination]] and [[Demand Avoidance]], since a task you expect to fail at is painful to start. For neurodivergent people, years of being told they underperform can lower it across the board, regardless of real capacity, which makes accommodations that produce early successes especially valuable.
