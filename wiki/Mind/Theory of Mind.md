**Theory of mind** is the capacity to attribute mental states, such as beliefs, desires, intentions, and knowledge, to other people, and to understand that those states can differ from your own. When a friend searches the wrong drawer for keys you moved, you predict where they will look from what they believe, not from where the keys are. David Premack and Guy Woodruff coined the term in 1978 while asking whether chimpanzees could do this.

The standard test is the **false-belief task**. In the Sally-Anne version, a child watches Sally put a marble in a basket and leave. Anne moves it to a box. Where will Sally look? Most three-year-olds say the box, where the marble is. Most children around four say the basket, where Sally thinks it is. Heinz Wimmer and Josef Perner designed the first version in 1983, and passing it became a milestone in developmental psychology.

Several distinctions matter:

- **Cognitive and affective.** Knowing what someone believes is different from sensing what they feel. A person can be strong at one and weaker at the other.
- **Fast and slow.** Adults track others' perspectives automatically much of the time, but deliberate reasoning about nested beliefs ("she thinks he knows") is effortful and leans on [[Working Memory]] and [[Executive Function]].
- **Projection.** The default model of another mind is your own, adjusted. The [[Curse of Knowledge]] is the failure to adjust: you cannot unsee what you know, so you overestimate what others share. The [[Fundamental Attribution Error]] is a related misreading of situation as character.

In 1985 Simon Baron-Cohen, Alan Leslie, and Uta Frith reported that many autistic children failed the Sally-Anne task, and "theory of mind deficit" became a dominant account of autism. It has aged poorly. Damian Milton's **double empathy problem** (2012) points out that non-autistic people are just as poor at reading autistic minds, and that autistic people often read each other well. The difficulty lives in the mismatch between two kinds of mind, a point central to [[Neurodiversity]]. Much of [[Masking]] is the work of running a deliberate, tiring model of neurotypical expectations to make up for that mismatch.

Theory of mind is also where predictive accounts of the brain meet social life. Under [[Predictive Processing]], other people are among the hardest things to predict, and much social effort goes into reducing that uncertainty.

Machines raised the question again in 2023, when [[Large Language Model]] systems passed classic false-belief vignettes. Small changes to the stories, such as making the container transparent, often broke the success, which suggests pattern matching on familiar test wording. The same habit of modeling a listener drives [[Sycophancy]], telling a reader what the model predicts they want to hear.
