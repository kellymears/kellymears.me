**Time perception** is the sense of how long things take and how fast time passes. Unlike sight or hearing, it has no dedicated sense organ. The brain estimates duration from the flow of events, attention, and memory, and the estimates bend easily.

Psychologists distinguish two kinds of judgment. **Prospective** timing happens when someone knows they'll need to judge an interval, and attends to time passing. **Retrospective** timing happens afterward, reconstructing duration from memory. They often disagree. A boring wait feels long while it lasts, because attention dwells on time itself; see [[Boredom]]. An absorbing afternoon flies by, because attention is elsewhere; see [[Flow]] and [[Hyperfocus]].

Memory reverses some of these effects. A two-week trip full of new places feels short while it's happening but long in retrospect, because it laid down many distinct memories. Two weeks of routine pass slowly in the moment yet leave little behind, so they seem brief later. The writer Claudia Hammond called this the **holiday paradox**; see [[Episodic Memory]].

Several laboratory effects show how malleable the sense is:

- **Oddball effect.** In a sequence of identical images, an unexpected one seems to last longer than the others, as novelty draws extra processing.
- **Chronostasis.** Glancing at a clock with a second hand, the first second sometimes seems to linger, as the brain fills in the gap during the eye's movement.
- **Scale.** People tend to overestimate short durations and underestimate long ones, and errors grow in proportion to the interval; see [[Weber-Fechner Law]].

Fear famously seems to slow time. Accident survivors often describe events unfolding in slow motion. A 2007 study tested this by dropping volunteers into a net from a tall platform while they watched a display flashing numbers too fast to read at normal speed. Participants judged their own falls as longer than other people's, but they couldn't read the numbers. The likely explanation is that frightening moments are recorded more densely, so they seem longer when remembered; see [[Fight-or-Flight Response]].

Time also seems to pass faster with age. One explanation is that adult life holds fewer novel experiences than childhood, so recent years leave sparse memories and seem to have gone quickly. The years of early adulthood, packed with firsts, remain disproportionately vivid; see [[Reminiscence Bump]].

Time perception varies between people. Many with ADHD experience difficulty sensing elapsed time and anticipating future time, a pattern sometimes called time blindness; see [[Time Blindness]]. Daily biological rhythms also shift the internal sense of tempo; see [[Circadian Rhythm]].

Misjudging time has practical costs. Projects routinely take longer than planned; see [[Planning Fallacy]]. Distant rewards feel smaller than near ones partly because the future feels remote; see [[Hyperbolic Discounting]].
