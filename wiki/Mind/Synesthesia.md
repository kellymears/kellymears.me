**Synesthesia** is a condition in which a stimulus in one sense or concept automatically and involuntarily triggers an additional experience. For a grapheme-color synesthete, the letter A may always be red and the number 7 always yellow-green. Others see colors when hearing music, taste words, feel touch when watching someone else being touched, or experience the days of the week arranged in space.

Estimates suggest around 4 percent of people have some form, with grapheme-color the most common. It runs in families. Many synesthetes do not realize their experience is unusual until they mention it and are met with blank looks, a version of the same surprise found with [[Aphantasia]].

Early skepticism held that synesthetes were merely remembering associations, such as letter colors from a childhood toy, or speaking metaphorically. Two lines of evidence showed it is perceptual and real:

- **Consistency.** Asked to pick colors for letters, then retested months or years later without warning, synesthetes choose nearly identical shades. People asked to invent associations cannot match that.
- **Pop-out.** In a 2001 test by V. S. Ramachandran and Edward Hubbard, a triangle of 2s hidden among scattered 5s, all printed in black, was found faster by some synesthetes, because the 2s appeared in a different color. The extra color behaves like a real feature, recruited by [[Preattentive Processing]].

Brain imaging shows color regions activating in response to black letters. One explanation is extra connectivity between neighboring areas, possibly because some connections present in infancy are not pruned during development, a matter of [[Neuroplasticity]]. Another is unusual feedback from higher areas, consistent with [[Predictive Processing]].

Synesthesia is more common among autistic people, roughly three times the general rate in one 2013 study, and both involve differences in sensory perception and detail-focused processing, discussed under [[Sensory Processing]]. Synesthesia is generally experienced as neutral or pleasant, and many synesthetes would not give it up. It can aid memory, since colored digits or spatial calendars provide extra cues, and some synesthetes use their colors to spot typos or remember phone numbers. Mirror-touch synesthesia, feeling touch on one's own body when seeing it on others, is linked to heightened empathy and can be overwhelming in crowds, an edge of [[Sensory Overload]].

Everyone shares milder cross-sensory links. In Wolfgang Köhler's 1929 experiment, revived in 2001, most people match the nonsense word *bouba* to a rounded blob and *kiki* to a spiky shape, across languages. High-pitched sounds are matched with small, bright objects and low pitches with large, dark ones. Designers and composers rely on these shared correspondences, which may be the ordinary end of a spectrum whose far end is synesthesia.

The condition fits the wider picture of [[Neurodiversity]]: perception is constructed, and the construction varies.
