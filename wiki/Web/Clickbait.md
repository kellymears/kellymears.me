**Clickbait** is content whose headline or thumbnail is designed above all to get a click. Its standard techniques are withholding the key fact ("you won't believe what happened next"), numbered lists, exaggeration and emotional language. The term usually implies the content behind the link disappoints, delivering less than the headline promised.

Sensational headlines are much older than the web. Newspapers in 1890s New York competed for street sales with lurid front pages, a style called yellow journalism, and tabloids carried it on. What changed online was measurement and payment. Ads paid by the impression meant revenue rose with every page view, and analytics showed exactly which headlines produced them; see [[Programmatic Advertising]] and [[Pageview]]. Social feeds then put headlines from every publisher side by side, all competing for the same glance; see [[Attention Economy]].

Much of clickbait's power comes from curiosity. The economist George Loewenstein argued in 1994 that curiosity is a feeling of deprivation caused by a gap in knowledge, sharpest when the gap is small and specific. A headline that reveals almost everything but the answer exploits that directly; see [[Curiosity gap]]. Negative and outrage-inducing words help too. A 2023 analysis of thousands of headline experiments run by the viral news site Upworthy found that each additional negative word slightly raised click rates; see [[Negativity Bias]].

Upworthy, launched in 2012, was also famous for its method. Staff wrote many headlines for each story and tested them against each other on live audiences, keeping the winner; see [[A/B testing]]. The approach spread across digital publishing. Optimizing a single number had predictable effects. Headlines got better at earning clicks and worse at describing articles, and readers learned to distrust them; see [[Goodhart's Law]].

Platforms pushed back because clickbait made their feeds feel cheap. In 2014 Facebook began demoting links that people clicked but quickly returned from, treating short visits as a sign of disappointment, and later trained systems to detect clickbait headlines; see [[Recommender System]]. Search engines similarly tried to rank pages that satisfy readers rather than those that merely attract them; see [[Search Engine Optimization]]. Some publishers, including Upworthy, shifted to measuring time spent reading.

Clickbait adapted to each change. Video platforms brought exaggerated thumbnails with shocked faces and arrows, chosen through the same testing. Link previews drawn from page metadata let publishers write separate, punchier headlines for social media; see [[Open Graph]]. Subscription publishers have less need of it, since they're paid by readers who return rather than by single visits; see [[Paywall]].

Critics note that the line is blurry. Every good headline tries to make someone read, and a curiosity-provoking headline is only clickbait when the article fails to pay it off.
