**Adobe Flash** was a browser plugin and authoring tool that, for roughly fifteen years, was how the web did animation, games, interactive sites, and video. At its peak it was installed on nearly every desktop computer. It was discontinued at the end of 2020.

It began as FutureSplash Animator, a small vector animation program released in 1996. Macromedia bought it that year and renamed it Flash; Adobe acquired Macromedia in 2005. Its advantage was size: animations were stored as shapes and motion rather than as pixels, so a minute of cartoon fit through a dial-up modem; see [[Vector Graphics]]. Artists drew on a timeline and let the software fill in motion between key poses; see [[Keyframe Animation]]. A scripting language, ActionScript, added interactivity and grew into a full programming language.

Flash shaped a generation of web culture:

- **Animation.** Independent cartoons and series found audiences without television studios, and portals such as Newgrounds hosted thousands of them.
- **Games.** Browser games made in Flash launched careers and studios, and many later commercial independent games began as Flash prototypes; see [[Game Jam]].
- **Video.** Browsers had no common way to play video, so YouTube, launched in 2005, played everything through Flash; see [[Video Compression]].
- **Sites.** Agencies built whole websites in it, often behind an animated intro with a "skip intro" button.

Its weaknesses came from being a plugin: a rectangle of separately run content inside the page. Search engines couldn't read it, screen readers usually couldn't either, links into it didn't exist, and the back button didn't work; see [[Accessibility]]. It was a proprietary runtime controlled by one company, outside the open process that governs the web; see [[Web Standards]] and [[Browser Wars]]. And it was a constant security liability, with a steady stream of flaws exploited before patches arrived.

Mobile ended it. The iPhone shipped in 2007 without Flash, and in 2010 Steve Jobs published "Thoughts on Flash," arguing that it was closed, insecure, a drain on batteries, and designed for mice rather than touch. Meanwhile browsers gained native video, scriptable drawing, and CSS animation; see [[HTML Canvas]]. Adobe announced the end in 2017, stopped supporting it on December 31, 2020, and the player began refusing to run content days later.

That left a large body of culture unplayable; see [[Digital Preservation]]. Volunteer projects responded. Flashpoint archived well over a hundred thousand games and animations with a launcher to run them offline. Ruffle, a Flash emulator compiled to [[WebAssembly]], plays many old files directly in modern browsers, and the Internet Archive uses it to keep its Flash collection viewable; see [[Emulation]].

Flash's ideas, rich interactive applications in the browser, won even though Flash lost; see [[Web 2.0]].
