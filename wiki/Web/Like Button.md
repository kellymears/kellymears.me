The **like button** reduces a response to a single tap. Earlier sites had experimented with similar ideas: FriendFeed offered a "like" in 2007, and link-sharing sites such as Digg and Reddit let users vote stories up or down to decide what reached the front page. Facebook added its like button in 2009, and it spread to nearly every platform in some form: hearts, thumbs, stars, claps and, from 2016, Facebook's set of emoji reactions; see [[Social Media]].

**Voting** systems such as Reddit's **upvotes** and **downvotes** use the same click differently: as a collective ranking mechanism, more like a crowd editing a newspaper than a friend nodding along. Totals often accumulate into reputation scores shown on profiles, an early form of gamification; see [[Gamification]] and [[Wisdom of the Crowd]]. Downvotes let a community bury spam and off-topic posts quickly, but they can also be used to silence unpopular views, so some sites hide or limit them.

For users, liking is the lowest-cost form of participation, an acknowledgment that doesn't require writing anything. For platforms, likes are the cheapest strong signal of what someone wants to see, and they became central inputs to ranking systems and ad targeting; see [[Recommender System]] and [[Algorithmic Feed]]. Researchers showed in 2013 that a person's Facebook likes alone could predict traits such as political views and sexual orientation with notable accuracy, a result that later fed the Cambridge Analytica controversy.

For posters, the counter is a public scoreboard. Likes arrive unpredictably, which is the reward pattern that most strongly maintains a behavior in conditioning experiments, and people check back to see how a post is doing; see [[Operant Conditioning]]. Visible counts also invite comparison with others; see [[Social Comparison]]. Popular posts attract more likes because they look popular, so early luck compounds; see [[Social Proof]] and [[Matthew Effect]].

Because likes measure success, they get gamed: purchased likes, like-for-like exchanges and engagement bait ("like if you agree") inflate the number without the approval it was meant to show; see [[Goodhart's Law]]. Clickjacking tricks users into liking pages they never saw; see [[Clickjacking]].

The button also spread beyond its home site. Embedded like and share buttons on news sites and stores let platforms record visits to pages across the web, even for people who never clicked; see [[Third-Party Cookie]].

Some platforms have tested hiding public like counts, Instagram most prominently from 2019, hoping to lower the pressure. The result was optional hiding rather than removal, since many users and advertisers wanted the numbers. Several of the button's early designers have since said publicly that they regret aspects of how it shaped behavior; see [[Dark Pattern]].
