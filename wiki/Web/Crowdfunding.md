**Crowdfunding** gathers money from a large number of people, each contributing a little, usually through a website that handles payments and promotion. The idea of public subscription is old: the pedestal of the Statue of Liberty was famously paid for in 1885 after a newspaper campaign drew small donations from more than 100,000 people. The web made it cheap to run, reaching strangers anywhere in the world for the cost of a page and a video.

Several models coexist:

- **Reward-based** platforms such as Kickstarter, launched in 2009, let creators set a goal and a deadline. Backers pledge for rewards, often the product itself, and are charged only if the goal is met. The all-or-nothing rule protects backers from funding a project that can't proceed and encourages them to recruit others as the deadline approaches.
- **Donation-based** sites such as GoFundMe collect gifts for causes, emergencies and, in the United States, a large share of medical bills.
- **Membership** platforms such as Patreon, launched in 2013, fund creators with recurring monthly payments instead of single projects; see [[Influencer]].
- **Equity crowdfunding** sells small stakes in companies to ordinary investors. In the United States it was enabled by the 2012 JOBS Act, with limits on how much non-wealthy investors can put in.

Crowdfunding opened doors that gatekeepers kept shut. Board games, independent video games, comics, gadgets and albums found audiences that publishers doubted existed, and a successful campaign doubles as market research. Some projects raised far more than anyone expected: the Pebble smartwatch drew over $10 million in 2012, and the space game Star Citizen has raised hundreds of millions over more than a decade without a full release. Open source developers use recurring donations to fund maintenance that companies rely on but don't pay for; see [[Open Source Sustainability]] and [[Free Rider Problem]].

Backers carry the risk. A pledge is not a purchase, so products can arrive late, differ from the pitch or never arrive. A study commissioned by Kickstarter in 2015 estimated that about one in ten funded projects failed to deliver rewards, and delays are common. Campaigns that already show momentum attract more pledges, so early support compounds; see [[Information Cascade]] and [[Social Proof]]. Backers who have waited years for a product may keep defending it, partly because of what they've already put in; see [[Sunk Cost Fallacy]].

Platforms sit between creators and backers and take a percentage from each, a classic two-sided market; see [[Two-Sided Market]]. Popular campaigns rely heavily on social sharing and press coverage, and visibility on the platform's own front page can make or break a launch; see [[Social Media]] and [[Slashdot Effect]].
