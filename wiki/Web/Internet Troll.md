An **internet troll** deliberately posts something inflammatory, absurd or off-topic to bait others into angry responses. The term likely comes from fishing, where trolling means dragging a lure behind a boat to see what bites, and it picked up an overlay from the folklore monster lurking under a bridge.

It took hold on Usenet in the late 1980s and early 1990s, where trolling at first meant fairly mild in-jokes: posting a well-worn question to catch newcomers who'd rush to answer it; see [[Eternal September]]. Heated exchanges that spiral into insults, **flame wars**, were common enough that discussion groups developed norms and folk laws about them, such as the observation that any long enough argument eventually compares someone to the Nazis; see [[Godwin's Law]] and [[Internet Forum]].

The standard advice, **don't feed the trolls**, follows from the motive. If the reward is a reaction, ignoring the post removes it. That works against attention seekers but not against everyone, and telling targets to stay silent can leave them absorbing abuse alone.

Trolling covers a wide range:

- **Pranks and absurdism**, such as baiting an argument about a deliberately silly claim. It can be hard to tell sincere extremism from parody, a problem known as Poe's law; see [[Poe's Law]].
- **Concern trolling**, posing as a sympathetic supporter while undermining a cause.
- **Harassment**, including coordinated pile-ons, threats and publishing private information about a target; see [[Doxing]].
- **Sockpuppets**, fake accounts that agree with their creator or evade bans; see [[Sybil Attack]].
- **State and commercial operations**, in which paid teams use troll tactics to sow division or fake public support. Russia's Internet Research Agency, documented in US investigations of the 2016 election, is the best known example; see [[Astroturfing]] and [[Propaganda]].

Why people troll is studied by psychologists. Anonymity, distance and the absence of facial cues lower inhibitions for many people, not just trolls. Studies of self-identified trolls have linked the behavior to traits such as sadism, the enjoyment of others' distress. Other research found that ordinary users troll more when they're in a bad mood and when the discussion they join already contains trolling, suggesting that context matters as much as character; see [[Bystander Effect]].

Platforms respond with blocking and muting tools, reputation systems, rate limits and moderation teams; see [[Content Moderation]]. Ranking that rewards engagement can work against those efforts, since outrage and provocation reliably generate replies; see [[Algorithmic Feed]].

The word has since spread beyond the internet. Politicians and companies are described as trolling when they make provocative statements designed to dominate the news cycle, and it's sometimes used affectionately for teasing between friends.
