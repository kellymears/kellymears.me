A **walled garden** is a platform that keeps its users inside. Content, communication and purchases happen within the service, on its terms, and connections to the outside world are limited or discouraged. The opposite is the open web, where anyone can publish at an address, link to anything and be found by any search engine.

The first great walled gardens predated the web's popularity. In the early 1990s, services like CompuServe, Prodigy and America Online offered email, chat, news and shopping to dial-up subscribers, all inside their own software. AOL mailed out disks by the millions and reached tens of millions of members. As the open web grew richer, the gardens couldn't compete with everything outside, and AOL gradually became a doorway to the web rather than a replacement for it.

The model returned with smartphones. Apple's App Store, opened in 2008, made Apple the gatekeeper for software on the iPhone: apps needed approval, and purchases went through its payment system, which took a commission of up to 30 percent. Critics called this monopolistic; supporters credited it with security and quality. European rules that took effect in 2024 forced Apple to allow other app stores in the EU. Web apps that install like native ones offer a way around app stores, and their support has long been a point of contention; see [[Progressive Web App]].

Social networks built gardens from content. Posts inside them are often hidden from search engines, links leading out are sometimes ranked lower, and publishers have been invited to host articles on the platform itself; see [[Web Crawler]] and [[Deep Linking]]. In 2015 Facebook launched Free Basics, offering free mobile access to a limited set of sites in developing countries. India banned it in 2016 under rules against charging differently for different content; see [[Zero-rating]].

Walls persist because they pay. A platform where users and businesses meet becomes more valuable as each side grows, and leaving means losing contacts, history and purchases; see [[Network Effect]], [[Two-Sided Market]] and [[Lock-in]]. Once users can't easily leave, a platform can shift value from them to itself; see [[Enshittification]]. Restricting outside access is part of the strategy. Several platforms sharply limited their public programming interfaces in 2023, breaking third-party apps and research tools.

Defenders of openness push back with standards and portability. Feeds let readers follow sites without a platform in between, and Google's closure of its popular feed reader in 2013 is often cited as a turning point; see [[RSS]]. Laws increasingly require platforms to let users export their data; see [[Data Portability]]. Federated networks let independent servers interoperate, so no one company owns the garden; see [[Federation]] and [[Web Standards]].
