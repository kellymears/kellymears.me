Any site that lets the public post must decide what stays up. **Content moderation** is that work: writing rules, finding posts that break them, and acting through removal, warning labels, reduced reach or account bans. On a small forum it's a volunteer with a delete button; see [[Internet Forum]]. On the largest platforms it involves tens of thousands of workers, machine learning systems and billions of decisions; see [[Social Media]].

Rules usually cover spam, illegal material such as child sexual abuse imagery and terrorist propaganda, harassment, hate speech, graphic violence, nudity and, increasingly, misinformation on topics such as elections and health; see [[Email Spam]] and [[Propaganda]]. Each category hides hard calls. Satire, news reporting, quotation and reclaimed slurs look like violations out of context, and norms differ between countries and communities.

Moderation runs on several layers:

- **Matching** known illegal images and videos through perceptual hashes, which recognize altered copies; Microsoft's PhotoDNA is widely shared among platforms; see [[Hash Function]].
- **Classifiers** score new posts for likely violations. They catch most spam and nudity before anyone reports it but struggle with context, sarcasm and less common languages; see [[Precision and Recall]].
- **User reports** flag what automation missed.
- **Human reviewers**, often contractors, make the final call on hard cases, sometimes in seconds. Repeated exposure to violent and abusive material has been linked to trauma, and several reviewers have sued over working conditions.

Every system makes both kinds of errors: harmful posts left up and legitimate posts taken down. At scale, even a small error rate means millions of mistakes, so appeals and transparency reports have become standard.

Softer actions avoid deletion. Platforms can demote a post so the feed shows it to fewer people, a practice users call **shadowbanning** when it happens without notice; see [[Algorithmic Feed]]. **Deplatforming** removes a person or group entirely. Studies of several bans found that the removed accounts' audiences shrank, though some followers moved to smaller, less moderated sites.

The legal frame matters. In the United States, Section 230 of the 1996 Communications Decency Act shields platforms from liability for most user posts and protects their right to moderate in good faith. The EU's Digital Services Act instead imposes obligations on how large platforms handle illegal content and explain their decisions.

Moderation is also political. Users across the spectrum accuse platforms of bias, and removals can draw more attention to the content removed; see [[Streisand Effect]]. Volunteer-run communities and federated networks spread the decisions among many moderators rather than one company; see [[Federation]] and [[Wikipedia]]. Generative AI raises the volume of convincing fake content while also offering better tools to review it; see [[Deepfake]] and [[Large Language Model]].
