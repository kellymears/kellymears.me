**Web 2.0** was the name given, in the mid-2000s, to a shift in what websites were: from pages published by their owners to platforms filled by their users, and from documents that reloaded on every click to pages that behaved like applications. The term was coined in 1999 but popularized by a 2004 conference organized by the publisher Tim O'Reilly, whose 2005 essay "What Is Web 2.0" tried to define it.

The defining traits:

- **Users as authors.** The value came from what visitors contributed. Wikipedia (2001), the photo site Flickr (2004), YouTube (2005), blogs with comment threads, and social networks such as MySpace and Facebook all depended on **user-generated content**; see [[Wiki]].
- **Tagging.** Instead of fixed categories, users labeled things with free-form tags, and sites displayed the most popular as a **tag cloud**, words sized by frequency. The resulting bottom-up classification was called a folksonomy; see [[Taxonomy]].
- **Syndication.** Feeds let readers follow many sites from one reader, and let sites republish each other; see [[RSS]].
- **Pages as applications.** Gmail (2004) and Google Maps (2005) updated parts of the page in the background without reloading, a technique named Ajax in 2005; see [[Fetch API]] and [[Single-Page Application]]. Libraries smoothed over browser differences; see [[jQuery]].
- **Open interfaces.** Sites published APIs, and developers combined them into **mashups**. An early example plotted Craigslist rental listings on Google Maps, before Google even offered a public maps API.
- **Perpetual beta.** Software delivered over the web could change weekly, so many products wore a "beta" label for years.

The era had a look: glossy buttons, gradients, reflections, rounded corners, pastel colors, and company names with missing vowels. Time magazine's 2006 Person of the Year was "You," with a mirrored cover.

Critics objected from the start. Tim Berners-Lee called the term jargon, pointing out that the web was always meant to be read and written by everyone; see [[World Wide Web]]. Others noted that users supplied the content, unpaid, while companies kept the value and the data.

The long-term effect was consolidation. Contribution flowed to a few platforms whose value grew with every user, and the open APIs that powered mashups were restricted once platforms no longer needed outside developers; see [[Network Effect]]. Feeds lost ground to algorithmic timelines designed to hold attention; see [[Attention Economy]] and [[Enshittification]]. Movements to reclaim personal sites and open protocols, and later the "Web3" label, define themselves partly against that outcome; see [[IndieWeb]].

Much of Web 2.0's interactivity ran on a plugin before open standards caught up; see [[Adobe Flash]].
