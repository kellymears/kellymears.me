An **internet forum** is a site for public discussion organized into threads. Someone starts a topic with a post, others reply below it, and the whole exchange stays visible and searchable long after. Forums are grouped into boards or subforums by subject, and the conversation is asynchronous: people answer when they arrive, not in real time.

Forums inherited their shape from older systems. Dial-up bulletin boards let callers leave messages for each other on one machine; see [[Bulletin Board System]]. Usenet, begun in 1980, spread threaded newsgroups across many servers worldwide, and email lists carried discussion to subscribers' inboxes; see [[Mailing List]]. Web forums arrived in the mid-1990s, and free software packages around 2000 let anyone run one on cheap hosting; see [[PHP]].

The form developed its own customs. Members had avatars, post counts and signatures appended to every message. Moderators enforced rules, moved off-topic threads and banned troublemakers. Newcomers were told to search before asking and scolded for reviving years-old threads. Text-only conversation also produced conventions for tone, from emoticons to explicit sarcasm markers; see [[Emoticon]]. Several well-known internet laws were first stated on forums and newsgroups, including observations that every long argument eventually reaches a Nazi comparison and that parody of an extreme view can't be told from the real thing; see [[Godwin's Law]] and [[Poe's Law]].

Forum culture had recurring problems. Most visitors read without posting, so a small core wrote nearly everything; see [[Participation Inequality]]. Fake accounts used to win arguments or evade bans were common enough to get their own name, sockpuppets; see [[Sybil Attack]]. Communities that grew too large lost the familiarity that held them together, and waves of newcomers could overwhelm their norms; see [[Dunbar's Number]] and [[Eternal September]].

**Imageboards**, which began in Japan around 1999 and spread with 4chan in 2003, centered threads on images, usually allowed anonymous posting, and deleted old threads automatically. Their mix of anonymity and impermanence made them a major source of internet culture and of harassment campaigns.

Later sites reworked the format. Question-and-answer sites ranked replies by votes and rewarded helpful users with reputation points; see [[Gamification]]. Stack Overflow, launched in 2008, became the best-known example. Link aggregators combined voting with nested comment threads. From the 2010s, many communities moved to social media groups and chat servers, which are easier to join but harder to search. Chat servers in particular keep conversations behind logins, and their scrolling streams are poor at preserving answers for the next person with the same question. Much of the practical knowledge once found in old forum threads now sits in places search engines can't reach, or has disappeared when forums shut down.
