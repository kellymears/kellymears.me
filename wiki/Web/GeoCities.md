**GeoCities** was a free web hosting service where millions of people built personal home pages in the late 1990s. David Bohnett and John Rezner founded it in 1994, and anyone could claim a few megabytes of space and publish whatever they liked. For many, it was their first website.

Its distinctive idea was geography. Sites were grouped into themed **neighborhoods** named after places: Hollywood for film, SiliconValley for computing, Area51 for science fiction, Athens for philosophy and education. A page lived at an address made of its neighborhood and a house number, and users were called homesteaders. Volunteer community leaders helped newcomers in each area. It made a sprawling network feel like a town you could wander.

The pages had a recognizable look, assembled by people learning [[HTML]] as they went:

- tiled backgrounds and blinking text;
- "under construction" signs, often animated; see [[Animated GIF]];
- hit counters, guestbooks, and background music;
- links to similar sites through a [[Webring]].

Pages were built in a browser-based editor or uploaded by FTP, and the editor's templates explain why so many pages shared a look. Topics ranged from fan shrines and family photos to poetry, and for many people it was the first place they wrote for strangers.

By 1999 it was among the most visited sites on the web, and Yahoo bought it that January in a stock deal worth about $3.6 billion at the time; see [[Economic bubble]]. Yahoo soon changed the terms of service to claim broad rights over everything users had posted. After an outcry it backed down, but the episode showed who owned a free host's content.

Free hosting came with limits. Pages carried ads, and accounts had caps on traffic, so an image that other sites embedded could shut a page down for the day; see [[Hotlinking]] and [[Bandwidth]]. As commercial social networks and blogging tools made publishing easier, homesteaders moved on; see [[Blog]] and [[Web 2.0]].

Yahoo shut down the service in the United States in October 2009; the Japanese version lasted until 2019. A volunteer group called Archive Team raced to copy as much as it could before the deadline and later released a torrent of several hundred gigabytes. Other collectors saved what they could too, but much was lost; see [[Link Rot]] and [[Digital Preservation]].

The closure became a standard warning about building on a platform you don't control. If the host decides the pages aren't worth keeping, they go; see [[Enshittification]].

GeoCities is now remembered with some fondness. Its amateur pages, made by people with no commercial goal, are often contrasted with feeds built around engagement, and its aesthetic is copied on purpose by personal sites that want to feel handmade; see [[IndieWeb]].
