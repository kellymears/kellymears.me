A **hashtag** is a word or run-together phrase marked with the # symbol, such as #FridayFeeling, that makes a post findable alongside every other post using the same tag. Click it and you see the conversation, assembled from strangers who never coordinated beyond picking the same few characters.

The convention came from users, not from a company. Internet Relay Chat had long used the # sign for channel names; see [[Internet Relay Chat]]. In August 2007 Chris Messina, an early Twitter user, proposed using it to group posts by topic. Twitter didn't build support at first, but people adopted it anyway, notably during wildfires in San Diego that autumn, when the tag #sandiegofire collected updates. Twitter began linking hashtags in 2009 and added trending topics, a list of tags rising fastest. Instagram, Facebook, TikTok and others followed; see [[Social Media]].

Hashtags are a **folksonomy**: a classification system built bottom-up by users rather than designed by librarians, flexible and messy at once. Different groups invent different tags for the same thing, and the same tag can mean unrelated things; see [[Taxonomy]] and [[Metadata]].

They serve several purposes:

- **Discovery.** Following an event, a show or a conference as it happens.
- **Identity and humor.** Many tags carry no search value at all. A tag like #blessed or #fail works as a punchline or a tone marker, closer to an emoji than an index term; see [[Emoji]] and [[Meme]].
- **Marketing.** Brands launch campaign tags, and posts stuffed with popular tags chase reach, a cousin of keyword stuffing on the web; see [[Search Engine Optimization]].
- **Activism.** Tags such as #BlackLivesMatter, #MeToo and the 2014 #BringBackOurGirls condensed movements into a shareable phrase and made their scale visible; see [[Social Identity Theory]].

Critics call some of this **hashtag activism** or slacktivism, arguing that posting a tag costs little and can substitute for action. Researchers have found cases both ways: some campaigns faded quickly, while others built lasting networks and shifted coverage.

Because trending lists amplify whatever is rising, they're targets for manipulation. Coordinated accounts, bots and paid campaigns push tags to manufacture the appearance of grassroots momentum, and a trend's visibility then pulls in real users, a self-reinforcing loop; see [[Astroturfing]] and [[Information Cascade]]. Tags can also be hijacked when opponents flood them with mockery, and a brand's cheerful campaign tag can become a collection point for complaints; see [[Streisand Effect]].

Platforms that rank posts by predicted engagement have made tags less central to discovery, since the feed decides what spreads; see [[Algorithmic Feed]]. The symbol itself has become generic enough to appear on packaging, protest signs and spoken aloud.
