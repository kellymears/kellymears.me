An **influencer** is someone whose audience on social platforms is valuable enough that their recommendations move purchases or opinions. The broader term **content creator** covers anyone making videos, posts, streams, podcasts or newsletters for an online audience, whether or not brands are involved; see [[Social Media]].

Celebrity endorsement is old, but influencers differ in where their pull comes from. They usually built an audience themselves, often in a niche such as makeup, gaming, fitness, parenting or cooking, and they speak directly into followers' phones in a casual first-person voice, often from their own bedrooms and kitchens. Followers feel they know them, a one-sided intimacy researchers call a **parasocial relationship**; see [[Parasocial Relationship]]. That perceived authenticity is what advertisers pay for: a recommendation from someone who seems like a friend feels more trustworthy than an advertisement; see [[Social Proof]].

The path from bloggers and early YouTubers in the 2000s to a full industry ran through Instagram and later TikTok, Twitch and others; see [[Blog]]. Creators earn in several ways:

- **Sponsorships** and paid posts, often priced by follower count and engagement.
- **Platform revenue sharing**, such as a cut of ads shown on videos.
- **Direct support** from fans through subscriptions, tips and memberships; see [[Crowdfunding]].
- **Merchandise and their own products**, with many large creators launching brands.

Income is extremely uneven. A small fraction of creators earn most of the money while the long tail earns little or nothing, a power-law pattern that compounds because platforms show popular accounts to more people; see [[Pareto Principle]] and [[Matthew Effect]]. Creators also depend on ranking systems they don't control, and a change to a platform's feed can halve someone's reach overnight; see [[Algorithmic Feed]].

The arrangement raises consumer protection questions. When a post is paid, followers may not know. The US Federal Trade Commission and regulators elsewhere require clear disclosure, such as an #ad label, though enforcement has been patchy; see [[Hashtag]]. Influencers have promoted products that later proved fraudulent, including the 2017 Fyre Festival and a series of cryptocurrency schemes. Fake followers and purchased engagement can inflate a creator's apparent reach to sell sponsorships; see [[Astroturfing]].

Audiences feel effects too. Curated images of bodies, homes and lifestyles invite unfavorable comparison, a concern raised especially about teenagers; see [[Social Comparison]]. Children who appear in family vlogs have prompted some jurisdictions to pass laws setting aside a share of earnings for them.

The model has spread to unexpected places. Companies now hire employees to post as creators, politicians court influencers as campaign surrogates, and virtual influencers, computer-generated characters run by agencies, have attracted real sponsorships; see [[Attention Economy]].
