A **web directory** is a catalog of websites sorted into categories by people rather than found by software. Instead of typing a query, a visitor drills down through a hierarchy, from Recreation to Outdoors to Hiking to a list of trail sites, each with a short description written by an editor.

Directories dominated the early web; see [[World Wide Web]]. In 1994, two Stanford graduate students, Jerry Yang and David Filo, kept a list of their favorite sites called "Jerry and David's Guide to the World Wide Web." It grew into Yahoo, which for several years was the front door to the web for millions of people. Search engines of the time ranked results poorly, and a human-chosen list of a few good sites per topic was often more useful than thousands of unranked matches; see [[Web Search Engine]].

The model borrowed from libraries. Like a classification scheme, a directory places each item in one spot in a tree, with cross-references for items that belong in several; see [[Library Classification]] and [[Taxonomy]]. That brought the same problems: deciding where an item belongs, keeping categories balanced as some topics explode, and the fact that any hierarchy reflects the views of whoever built it.

Scale was the deeper problem. The web was growing far faster than editors could review submissions, and listings decayed as sites moved or vanished; see [[Link Rot]]. The **Open Directory Project**, started in 1998 and soon bought by Netscape, tried to solve this with volunteers, eventually tens of thousands of editors, each responsible for a small category. Its data was free to reuse, and search engines, including Google, used it to seed crawls and to display category information. Volunteer editing brought its own trouble, including accusations that some editors favored their own sites or blocked competitors.

Search engines that ranked by links made directories less necessary. Being listed in a directory still mattered for a time, because a directory listing was a link from a trusted page; see [[PageRank]]. Paid directories sprang up to sell such links, and search engines learned to discount them; see [[Search Engine Optimization]]. Yahoo closed its directory in 2014, and the Open Directory Project shut down in 2017.

The idea persists in smaller forms. Webrings and blogrolls let site owners vouch for each other directly; see [[Webring]]. Curated link lists on code-hosting sites, community-maintained wikis, and personal link pages play a directory's role for narrow subjects; see [[Wiki]]. Tag-based systems traded the single tree for labels anyone could add. Interest in human curation has revived partly because search results have filled with spam and generated text.
