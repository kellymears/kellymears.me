**Wikipedia** is a free encyclopedia written and maintained by volunteers, any of whom can edit almost any article. It launched in January 2001, founded by Jimmy Wales and Larry Sanger, and grew into one of the most visited sites on the web, with editions in over three hundred languages and around seven million articles in English alone.

It began as an accident. Wales and Sanger were running Nupedia, an online encyclopedia whose articles went through expert review. The process was so slow that it published only a few dozen articles in its first year. They added a [[Wiki]] as a feeder where drafts could be written quickly. The feeder outgrew its parent within weeks, and Nupedia was shut down.

Wikipedia works because of a few principles rather than credentials. Articles should take a **neutral point of view**, presenting significant views fairly rather than arguing for one. Claims must be verifiable through published, reliable sources, and editors may not publish their own original research. Disputes are settled through discussion pages and a thick body of policy. Every change is kept, so any version can be compared with any other and vandalism reverted in seconds; see [[Diff]].

The result is a leading example of large-scale volunteer production, coordinated without managers or pay; see [[Commons-Based Peer Production]] and [[Gift Economy]]. Content is released under a license requiring that copies stay equally free, so it can be reused anywhere; see [[Copyleft]]. A nonprofit foundation, the Wikimedia Foundation, runs the servers on donations and carries no ads. A sister project stores facts as structured data shared across languages; see [[Wikidata]].

Its weaknesses are well known. A small fraction of editors make most edits; see [[Participation Inequality]]. Surveys have found editors to be mostly male and concentrated in wealthy countries, and coverage reflects it. Contentious topics produce **edit wars**, with editors repeatedly undoing each other, and organized campaigns use sockpuppet accounts to push a view; see [[Sybil Attack]]. Errors can become circular: a mistake in an article gets repeated by a journalist, who is then cited as a source for it, a process sometimes called citogenesis; see [[Source Amnesia]]. Cited pages also disappear over time; see [[Link Rot]].

Still, early fears that an encyclopedia anyone could edit would be worthless largely didn't come true. A 2005 study in Nature found science articles had error rates comparable to those in Britannica, a comparison Britannica disputed; see [[Wisdom of the Crowd]].

Its reach now extends well beyond its own pages. Search engines quote it in answer boxes, and it is among the most heavily used sources in training language models, which raises questions about whether the volunteer work behind it will keep being rewarded; see [[Web Search Engine]] and [[Pretraining]].
