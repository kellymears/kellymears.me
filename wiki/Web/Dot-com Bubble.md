The **dot-com bubble** was a stock market frenzy around internet companies that peaked in March 2000. The belief behind it, that the web would transform commerce and media, turned out largely right. The prices paid for it, and the timing, did not.

The boom is often dated to August 1995, when Netscape, maker of the leading web browser, went public. Its shares roughly doubled on the first day even though the company wasn't yet profitable; see [[Browser Wars]] and [[Mosaic]]. Over the next five years, venture capital and public investors funded hundreds of internet startups, many with business plans that amounted to growing fast and working out profits later. A company could see its stock jump simply by adding ".com" to its name. Founders and analysts argued that old valuation measures didn't apply, a claim that accompanies most bubbles; see [[Speculative Bubble]].

The logic had some basis. Investors believed the web had strong **network effects** and that the first company to win a market would hold it, so they paid for growth at any cost; see [[Network Effect]]. Advertising-funded sites chased page views and free services, a model later social platforms would refine; see [[Attention Economy]]. Free hosting sites such as GeoCities were bought for billions; see [[GeoCities]]. In December 1996 Federal Reserve chair Alan Greenspan warned of "irrational exuberance," yet prices kept climbing for three more years.

The Nasdaq Composite index, heavy with technology stocks, peaked on March 10, 2000, at just over 5,000. Over the next two and a half years it lost nearly 80 percent of its value. Rising interest rates, disappointing earnings and a rush of companies selling stock contributed to the turn. Startups that depended on fresh funding ran out of cash. Pets.com, with its sock-puppet mascot, shut down months after its public offering, and the grocery-delivery company Webvan collapsed after spending heavily on warehouses. Accounting scandals at Enron and WorldCom in 2001 and 2002 deepened the loss of trust.

Infrastructure was overbuilt too. Telecom companies laid far more fiber-optic cable than demand required and many went bankrupt. That cheap leftover capacity later helped make streaming video and broadband affordable; see [[Content Delivery Network]].

The survivors defined the next era. Amazon's stock fell more than 90 percent but the company endured, and Google, founded in 1998, grew through the downturn. The rebuilding web of the mid-2000s, with its emphasis on user contributions and sustainable advertising, was branded partly in contrast to the bust; see [[Web 2.0]]. The Nasdaq didn't regain its 2000 peak until 2015.

The episode remains the standard comparison whenever a new technology draws a wave of investment, from cryptocurrency to artificial intelligence; see [[Information Cascade]].
