**Social media** are services where the content is made by users and arrives through their connections. The defining parts are a profile, a list of people or accounts one follows, and a stream of their posts, along with ways to respond: replies, shares and likes; see [[Like Button]].

The pieces existed before the term. Bulletin boards, Usenet groups and web forums let strangers talk in public, and blogs gave individuals a publishing outlet with comments; see [[Internet Forum]]. Early friend-network sites such as SixDegrees in 1997 and Friendster in 2002 added explicit connections. The mid-2000s brought MySpace, Facebook, YouTube and Twitter, part of the shift toward user-generated pages that commentators called Web 2.0; see [[Web 2.0]]. Smartphones then put these services in every pocket, and photo, video and short-form apps followed.

The economics follow from **network effects**: a service is worth more the more people already use it, so markets tend toward a few giants that are hard to leave, because a user's friends, followers and history don't move with them; see [[Network Effect]]. Most are free to use and paid for by advertising, which makes the user's attention the product being sold. Platforms collect detailed behavioral data to target ads and to decide what each person sees; see [[Attention Economy]]. Advertisers on one side and users on the other make them classic two-sided markets; see [[Two-Sided Market]].

Since the early 2010s, most large platforms have replaced the simple chronological stream with a ranked one, chosen by models trained to maximize engagement; see [[Algorithmic Feed]]. That shift made reach unpredictable for posters and turned careers into a matter of pleasing a ranking system; see [[Influencer]].

Social media's effects are fiercely debated:

- **Connection.** Diaspora families, isolated people and niche communities find each other, and movements organize quickly, often around a shared tag; see [[Hashtag]].
- **Information.** Falsehoods and propaganda can spread faster than corrections, and people may see mostly views they already hold, though research on how strong that effect is remains mixed; see [[Filter Bubble]].
- **Wellbeing.** Researchers disagree about how much heavy use harms mental health, especially for teenagers. Constant comparison with curated lives and compulsive scrolling are the commonly cited mechanisms; see [[Social Comparison]].
- **Governance.** Platforms decide what may be said at enormous scale, making private companies arbiters of public speech; see [[Content Moderation]].

Alternatives keep appearing. Federated networks let independent servers interoperate so no single company owns the network, an echo of how email works; see [[Federation]]. Others bet on smaller circles: group chats and private communities now carry much of the conversation that once happened in public.
