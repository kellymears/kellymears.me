**Moravec's paradox** is the observation that, for computers, the hard problems are easy and the easy problems are hard. Abstract reasoning that people find demanding, such as chess, algebra, or formal logic, turned out to need relatively little computation. Skills any small child has, recognizing a face, walking across a cluttered room, picking up an egg without crushing it, turned out to be enormously difficult.

The roboticist Hans Moravec put it this way in 1988: it is comparatively easy to make computers play checkers at an adult level, and difficult or impossible to give them the perception and mobility of a one-year-old. Rodney Brooks and Marvin Minsky made similar points around the same time.

The usual explanation is evolution. Perception and movement have been refined over hundreds of millions of years, and the brain devotes huge resources to them, running beneath awareness. Because they feel effortless, people underestimate them; see [[Dual Process Theory]] and [[Predictive Processing]]. Deliberate abstract reasoning is evolutionarily recent and runs on a thin layer of slow, conscious effort, which is why it feels hard, and why its difficulty is misleading. Effort, in other words, is a poor guide to computational difficulty; see [[Cognitive Load]].

The paradox shaped AI's history. Early researchers, impressed that programs could prove theorems and play chess, expected vision and robotics to follow within years. Both took decades; see [[Expert System]]. In 1997 Deep Blue beat the world chess champion, while robots still struggled to fold laundry.

Deep learning moved the line. Image and speech recognition, once the textbook hard cases, became routine around 2012–2017 by learning from enormous amounts of data; see [[Neural Network]] and [[Automatic Speech Recognition]]. [[Large Language Model]]s then showed a twist: they write fluent prose and pass professional exams, yet can stumble on counting letters or simple spatial reasoning, partly because they see text as [[Token]]s rather than characters.

Physical manipulation remains the clearest case. Robots that can reliably handle arbitrary household objects are still an open problem in the mid-2020s, despite progress from learning by demonstration and simulation. Software agents operating computers face a milder version: reading a screen and clicking the right button is easy for people and still error-prone for models; see [[Computer Use]].

The practical lesson is to distrust intuitions about which tasks will be automated first. The tasks that feel skilled to people, like analysis and writing, have proved easier to automate than many that feel menial, like cleaning, cooking, or plumbing; see [[Planning Fallacy]].

The paradox also reframes intelligence: much of what people call "common sense" is perception and motor skill that never reaches words; see [[Interoception]] and [[Expert Intuition]].
