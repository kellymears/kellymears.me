**Artificial general intelligence**, or AGI, refers to an AI system that can handle the wide range of intellectual work people do, learning new tasks as needed, rather than excelling at one narrow job. The phrase spread in the early 2000s, used by researchers who felt the field had drifted into narrow applications and wanted a name for its original ambition.

That ambition dates from the field's founding. The 1956 proposal for the [[Dartmouth Workshop]] proposed that every aspect of intelligence could in principle be described precisely enough for a machine to simulate it. In 1965 Herbert Simon predicted machines would within twenty years be able to do any work a person could. Repeated over-promising helped bring on the funding collapses known as AI winters.

Definitions vary widely, which makes claims of arrival hard to judge. Some focus on outcomes, such as a system that outperforms people at most economically valuable work. Others focus on breadth and learning speed. François Chollet argued in 2019 that intelligence is the efficiency of acquiring new skills, not skill itself, and built a puzzle benchmark that memorization can't solve. The [[Turing Test]] is an older candidate, now widely seen as too easy to game. Benchmarks keep being beaten without settling the question, partly because measured scores become targets; see [[Goodhart's Law]].

Large language models reopened the debate. One camp, often citing [[Scaling Laws]] and the [[Bitter Lesson]], expects general ability to keep emerging from more compute and data, with agents and planning added on top; see [[Large Language Model]] and [[Reasoning Model]]. Another argues that something is still missing, such as grounding in the physical world, durable memory or reliable reasoning, and points to [[Moravec's Paradox]]: skills effortless for animals remain hard for machines; see [[Symbol Grounding Problem]].

**Superintelligence** names a system far beyond human ability in most domains. In 1965 the statistician I. J. Good described an **intelligence explosion**, in which a machine able to improve its own design triggers rapid cycles of improvement, and called the first such machine the last invention people would need to make. Vernor Vinge later popularized the **technological singularity**, a point past which prediction fails. Nick Bostrom's 2014 book Superintelligence argued that controlling such a system would be extremely hard; see [[Paperclip Maximizer]].

These ideas now shape real decisions. Companies state AGI as an explicit goal and raise money on it, while researchers work on keeping more capable systems controllable and honest; see [[AI Alignment]], [[Corrigibility]] and [[Scalable Oversight]]. Forecasts of when, if ever, it arrives range from a few years to never, and track records for such forecasts are poor.
