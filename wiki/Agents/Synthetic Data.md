**Synthetic data** is training data produced by a program or a model rather than collected from the real world. It's used when real data is scarce, expensive to label, private, or missing the rare cases that matter most. A growing share of the data used to train AI systems is synthetic.

The approaches range from mild to total:

- **Data augmentation** creates variations of real examples: flipped, cropped, recolored, or noisier images, or reworded sentences. It's the oldest and safest form, teaching a model that these changes don't alter the answer and reducing overfitting; see [[Regularization]].
- **Simulation** renders scenes where every label comes for free, since the program knows where each object is. In 2016 researchers trained image-labeling models on frames from an open-world video game; see [[Procedural Generation]].
- **Model-generated data** uses one model to produce examples for another.

Simulation has a gap with reality: models trained on clean renders often fail on messy camera images. **Domain randomization**, introduced around 2017, randomizes textures, lighting, and camera positions so wildly that the real world looks like just one more variation, helping robots trained in simulation work on real hardware; see [[Distribution Shift]].

For language models, model-generated data is now central. A large model can write instructions and answers that train a smaller one, a form of [[Knowledge Distillation]]. In 2023, Stanford's Alpaca was fine-tuned on 52,000 examples generated by a larger model for a few hundred dollars; see [[Fine-Tuning]]. Small models trained mainly on generated textbook-style material showed that quality could outweigh quantity.

Synthetic data works best when outputs can be checked. For math and code, a model can generate many attempted solutions, and only those that reach the right answer or pass tests are kept for training. This loop underlies much of the progress in models that reason step by step; see [[Reasoning Model]]. When answers can't be checked directly, another model often grades them, inheriting its biases; see [[LLM-as-Judge]]. Games offer a closed version of the same loop; see [[Self-Play]].

The risks:

- **Model collapse.** A 2024 study showed that models trained repeatedly on their predecessors' output lose the rare, unusual parts of the original distribution, converging on bland averages; see [[Model collapse]].
- **Amplified bias.** A generator's blind spots and errors pass into everything trained on its output.
- **Contamination.** Generated data can quietly include test questions the generator memorized; see [[Benchmark Contamination]].

Synthetic data is also promoted for privacy, replacing real records with statistically similar fake ones. Without formal guarantees, though, generators can memorize and leak real records; see [[Differential Privacy]] and [[Personal Data]].
