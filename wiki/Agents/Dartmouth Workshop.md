The **Dartmouth Workshop** was a summer research meeting held at Dartmouth College in 1956, usually treated as the founding event of artificial intelligence as a field. It gave the field its name and many of its first leaders, and its founding proposal set a pattern of ambitious forecasts that has repeated ever since.

The proposal, dated August 31, 1955, was written by John McCarthy, then a young mathematics professor at Dartmouth, with Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester of IBM, and Claude Shannon of Bell Labs; see [[Information Theory]]. McCarthy chose the term "artificial intelligence" partly to set the project apart from cybernetics, the existing study of feedback and control in animals and machines, and from its dominant figure, Norbert Wiener; see [[Feedback Loop]].

The proposal's central claim was sweeping: "every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it." It proposed a two-month, ten-person study and predicted that "a significant advance can be made in one or more of these problems if a carefully selected group of scientists work on it together for a summer." The listed topics were language, neural networks, abstraction, self-improvement, and creativity, most of which remain open research areas; see [[Planning Fallacy]] and [[Hofstadter's Law]].

The workshop itself was loose. Participants came and went over the summer rather than working together throughout, and no joint result emerged. The most concrete work came from Allen Newell and Herbert Simon, who brought the Logic Theorist, a program that proved theorems from Whitehead and Russell's *Principia Mathematica*, arguably the first program designed to reason. Arthur Samuel, whose checkers program learned from experience, also attended; see [[Self-Play]].

The meeting's real effect was social. It gathered the people who would found the major AI laboratories at MIT, Carnegie Mellon, and Stanford, and it set the early direction toward symbolic reasoning: representing knowledge as explicit symbols and rules and searching through them. McCarthy designed the programming language Lisp in 1958 for this work; see [[Lisp]]. Neural approaches, which had their own early champions, were sidelined for decades, returning only with more data and computation; see [[Neural Network]] and [[Bitter Lesson]].

The optimism set a cycle. Researchers predicted human-level machines within a generation; funders believed them, results lagged, and funding collapsed in periods later called AI winters, notably in the mid-1970s and late 1980s; see [[Expert System]].

The workshop's enduring legacies are its name and its question. Whether intelligence can be precisely described, and what a machine that simulated it would be, remain contested. Some of the hardest problems turned out to be the ones humans find easiest, such as seeing and moving; see [[Moravec's Paradox]] and [[Turing Test]].
