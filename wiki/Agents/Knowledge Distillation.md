**Knowledge distillation** trains a smaller *student* model to imitate a larger *teacher* model. Rather than learning only from the original data, the student learns from the teacher's outputs, which carry more information than raw labels. The result can retain much of the teacher's capability while being far cheaper and faster to run.

Geoffrey Hinton, Oriol Vinyals, and Jeff Dean popularized the technique in a 2015 paper. Their insight was that a trained model's full output distribution is informative. An image classifier shown a picture of a dog might give 90 percent to "dog," 8 percent to "wolf," and almost nothing to "car." Those *soft targets* tell the student that dogs resemble wolves far more than cars, knowledge a hard label of "dog" doesn't contain. Raising the [[Temperature Sampling]] temperature of the teacher's outputs during distillation makes these small probabilities more visible.

Distillation became standard for deploying language models. DistilBERT, released in 2019, was 40 percent smaller and 60 percent faster than the BERT model it learned from while keeping about 97 percent of its language understanding performance. Today, many small and mid-sized models are trained partly on text generated by larger ones:

- **Output imitation.** The student trains on the teacher's responses to large sets of prompts, a form of [[Fine-Tuning]] on synthetic data.
- **Logit matching.** When the teacher's full probability distribution over tokens is available, the student trains to match it directly, which transfers more per example.
- **Reasoning transfer.** In January 2025, DeepSeek released small models distilled from the reasoning traces of its large R1 model, which outperformed much larger models on math benchmarks; see [[Reasoning Model]].

The economics drive it. A frontier model may be too slow and expensive for high-volume tasks, and a distilled model serves most requests well enough. That makes distillation the companion to [[Model Routing]], where cheap models handle easy work and expensive ones are reserved for hard cases. It combines with [[Quantization]], which shrinks a model's numbers rather than its size, to make capable models run on laptops and phones. [[Speculative Decoding]] uses a related pairing, with a small draft model proposing tokens that a large model verifies.

Distillation has limits and controversies. A student copies its teacher's errors and biases along with its skills, and it tends to match the teacher on tasks resembling its training prompts while falling further behind on unusual ones. As described under [[Scaling Laws]], some capability seems to require size. Most commercial model providers prohibit using their outputs to train competing models, and accusations that rivals distilled from proprietary models have become a recurring dispute in the industry.

The idea echoes human teaching: a learner gains from seeing how close the alternatives were, not just the expert's answer.
