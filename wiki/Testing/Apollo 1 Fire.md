On 27 January 1967, the crew of what was to be the first crewed Apollo mission, Virgil "Gus" Grissom, Edward White and Roger Chaffee, were sealed inside their command module on the launch pad at Cape Kennedy for a **plugs-out test**, a rehearsal in which the spacecraft ran on its own internal power as it would during a countdown. The rocket beneath it held no fuel. Because of that, the test was not classified as hazardous, and emergency crews and equipment were not standing by as they would have been for a launch.

Five and a half hours into the test, after hours of communication problems, a crew member reported a fire. Within about twenty seconds the cabin had ruptured from the pressure of the flames, and all three astronauts died, mainly from inhaling toxic gases. Pad workers struggled for about five minutes to open the hatch through smoke and heat.

The investigation found a set of conditions that had each seemed acceptable:

- **Pure oxygen at high pressure.** The cabin was filled with 100 percent oxygen at slightly above sea-level pressure to check for leaks. In that atmosphere, materials that barely burn in air ignite readily and burn fiercely; see [[Combustion]] and [[Atmospheric Pressure]].
- **Flammable materials.** Nylon netting, foam padding and Velcro patches added by crews for convenience filled the cabin, and many burned easily in pure oxygen.
- **Ignition.** The likely source was an electrical arc from damaged wiring near the floor, though the exact spark was never identified. The spacecraft had many known wiring and workmanship defects.
- **The hatch.** The inner hatch opened inward and was held shut by the cabin pressure. Even with no fire, opening it took at least ninety seconds.

No single factor was decisive on its own. The disaster came from their combination, and from a test that was treated as routine because one hazard, rocket fuel, was absent while others went unexamined; see [[Risk-Based Testing]] and [[Failure Mode and Effects Analysis]].

NASA grounded crewed flights for twenty months and redesigned the command module. It got a single hatch that opened outward in seconds, a nitrogen-oxygen atmosphere on the pad, fireproof fabrics and rerouted, protected wiring. The redesign is often credited with making the later Moon landings possible; see [[Apollo 11 Program Alarms]] and [[Apollo 13]].

Flight director Gene Kranz responded with a talk to the flight control team that became part of NASA culture, arguing that the team had failed by not speaking up about problems everyone could see, and that from then on mission control would be "tough and competent." The lesson that a safety-critical organization can drift into accepting known defects would recur in later disasters; see [[Normalization of Deviance]] and [[Space Shuttle Challenger Disaster]].
