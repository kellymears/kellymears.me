The **Fukushima Daiichi nuclear disaster** began on March 11, 2011, when a magnitude 9.0 earthquake off northeastern Japan was followed by a tsunami that struck the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear power plant. Three reactors melted down, hydrogen explosions destroyed reactor buildings, and radioactive material spread over a wide area, forcing well over a hundred thousand people to evacuate. It's the only accident besides Chernobyl rated at the highest level on the international scale; see [[Chernobyl Disaster]].

The three operating reactors shut down automatically as designed, inserting control rods to stop the chain reaction; see [[Nuclear Fission]]. But a shut-down reactor still produces heat from radioactive decay and must be cooled for days. The quake cut the plant's connection to the grid, and emergency diesel generators started as designed to run the cooling pumps.

About fifty minutes later, the tsunami arrived. The site's seawall was built for a wave of under six meters; the tsunami that struck was around thirteen to fifteen meters; see [[Earthquake]]. Water flooded the turbine buildings, where most of the backup generators and the electrical switchgear sat in basements. Nearly all power was lost, a condition called a **station blackout**. Cooling stopped, and fuel in reactors one, two and three overheated and melted.

The core failure was that redundant backups shared a single exposure. Multiple generators offered no protection against one flood that reached all of them; see [[Common-Mode Failure]] and [[Single Point of Failure]]. Failure analyses that treat each generator as an independent chance of failing miss exactly this case; see [[Fault Tree Analysis]].

The size of the possible tsunami wasn't unknown. Geologists had found evidence of a huge tsunami in the region in the year 869, and an internal estimate by the plant's operator around 2008 suggested waves near fifteen meters were possible. The estimate wasn't acted on. A commission appointed by Japan's parliament concluded in 2012 that the disaster was preventable and largely man-made, rooted in collusion between the operator and regulators; see [[Normalization of Deviance]].

The nearby Onagawa plant, closer to the earthquake's center, survived the same tsunami largely intact. It had been built on higher ground after its designers took historical tsunamis into account; see [[Margin of Safety]].

Deaths from radiation itself appear to have been very few. The evacuation took a much larger toll, especially among elderly and hospitalized people moved in haste, which makes Fukushima a hard case for weighing one risk against another; see [[Precautionary Principle]].

Afterward, regulators worldwide required plants to plan for losing all power for extended periods, with portable generators stored away from the plant; see [[Safety Case]]. Like the accident at Three Mile Island, it showed operators struggling with failed instruments in the dark; see [[Three Mile Island Accident]] and [[Normal Accidents]].
