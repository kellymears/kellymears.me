The **Three Mile Island accident** was a partial meltdown of Unit 2 of the Three Mile Island nuclear plant near Harrisburg, Pennsylvania, on March 28, 1979. It's the most serious accident in the history of commercial nuclear power in the United States. Little radiation escaped and no deaths have been firmly attributed to it, but it changed how nuclear plants, and complex systems generally, are designed and understood.

The accident began at about four in the morning with a routine fault. A pump failure in the plant's secondary cooling loop caused the reactor to shut down automatically, as designed. The reactor kept producing heat from the decay of its radioactive fuel, so pressure rose, and a relief valve opened to release it; see [[Radioactive Decay]] and [[Nuclear Fission]]. When pressure fell, the valve should have closed. It stuck open, and cooling water began escaping.

In the control room, a light indicated the valve was closed. In fact, the light showed only that the signal to close it had been sent, not that the valve had moved. Operators believed the valve was shut, and nothing else on the crowded panel clearly told them otherwise. The instrument reported intention, not state; see [[Instrumentation]] and [[Mental Model]].

Other instruments seemed to show the reactor had too much water, not too little. Operators had been trained above all to avoid overfilling the system, so they cut back the emergency cooling water that was automatically flowing in. For more than two hours the core was partly uncovered, and about half of it melted. Evidence that contradicted the operators' picture was interpreted to fit it; see [[Confirmation Bias]]. Meanwhile, more than a hundred alarms went off in the first minutes, overwhelming anyone trying to understand what was happening; see [[Alarm fatigue]] and [[Cognitive Load]].

The sociologist Charles Perrow studied the accident and concluded that the operators weren't primarily to blame. In systems whose parts interact in unexpected ways and are tightly coupled, small failures combine in ways no one anticipated, and accidents are an inevitable property of the design; see [[Normal Accidents]].

Several kinds of change followed. Control rooms were redesigned with human factors in mind, operator training moved toward simulators, and regulators required plants to analyze more combinations of failures; see [[Fault Tree Analysis]]. Public fear of nuclear power rose sharply, and orders for new American reactors, already slowing, largely stopped for decades; see [[Precautionary Principle]].

Seven years later a far worse accident happened in the Soviet Union, with a very different cause; see [[Chernobyl Disaster]]. The undamaged Unit 1 at Three Mile Island kept operating until 2019.
