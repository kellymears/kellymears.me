The **Citicorp Center** is a 59-story skyscraper in Manhattan, finished in 1977. A church owned the corner of the block and would only allow a tower built over it, so the building stands on four massive columns placed at the middle of each side rather than at the corners, lifting the tower about nine stories above the ground. A system of diagonal braces in a chevron pattern carried the loads down to those columns. To limit swaying, it was among the first tall buildings with a tuned mass damper, a huge concrete block near the top that slides to counter the building's motion.

The structural engineer was William LeMessurier. In 1978, prompted in part by questions from an engineering student, LeMessurier checked a case not usually required by the building code: winds striking the building diagonally, on its corners, called **quartering winds**. For most buildings, perpendicular winds are the worst case. For this one, with its columns at the middle of each side, the diagonal case raised loads in some braces by around forty percent.

That alone was within the margins. But LeMessurier then learned that the steel contractor had replaced the welded joints in the braces with bolted joints, a cheaper change approved as meeting the design. The bolts had been checked only against the perpendicular-wind loads. Under quartering winds, some joints would be stressed far beyond what the bolts could take; see [[Margin of Safety]] and [[Boundary Value Analysis]].

By LeMessurier's estimate, a storm strong enough to topple the building could be expected about once every sixteen years on average, and the damper, which needed electric power, might fail in exactly such a storm; see [[Single Point of Failure]]. LeMessurier went to the building's owners and told them. Over the summer of 1978, crews worked at night, welding heavy steel plates over more than two hundred joints while the offices stayed open by day. In early September a hurricane threatened the city but turned out to sea. The repairs were finished in October.

The public was never told at the time. A press strike in New York that summer helped keep the story quiet, and it became widely known only through an article in The New Yorker in 1995. It's now a staple of engineering ethics courses, praised for the engineer's willingness to report a personal error and criticized for the secrecy toward people working in and near the building; see [[Blameless Postmortem]].

The case shows how a design can be correct at review and become unsafe through an unexamined substitution, and how a load case missing from the code can go untested; see [[Verification and Validation]], [[Requirements Traceability]] and [[Hyatt Regency Walkway Collapse]], where another connection change proved fatal.
