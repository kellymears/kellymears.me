The **de Havilland Comet** was the world's first commercial jet airliner. It entered service with the British Overseas Airways Corporation in May 1952, flying higher and faster than any propeller aircraft. Then, in January 1954, a Comet broke up in the air over the Mediterranean near the island of Elba shortly after leaving Rome. Flights resumed after modifications aimed at possible fire causes. In April 1954 a second Comet disintegrated near Naples, and the entire fleet was grounded.

Nobody knew why. Both aircraft had come apart at cruising altitude without warning, and wreckage lay at the bottom of the sea. The Royal Navy recovered much of the Elba aircraft from the seabed, an unprecedented salvage effort, while the Royal Aircraft Establishment at Farnborough ran a test that has become a classic.

Investigators took a complete Comet fuselage, built a water tank around it, and repeatedly pressurized and depressurized the cabin with water to imitate the cycle of climbing to altitude and descending, while also flexing the wings. Water was used rather than air because a rupture under water releases far less stored energy. After the equivalent of about 3,000 flights, counting those it had already flown in service, the fuselage split open. The crack began at the corner of a cutout in the skin, matching evidence found in the recovered wreckage of the Elba aircraft.

The cause was **metal fatigue**: tiny cracks that grow a little with every load cycle, at stresses far below what would break the metal in a single pull; see [[Material Fatigue]]. Sharp corners of openings concentrated the stress, and rivet holes near them gave cracks a place to start. The popular story blames square passenger windows, but the decisive failure started at a smaller cutout in the roof.

An earlier test had misled the designers. A test section of fuselage had first been pressurized to well above its working level as a strength proof, which left it with residual stresses that made it unusually resistant to fatigue, so it survived far more cycles than an untouched production fuselage would. The test was answering a different question from the one that mattered; see [[Test Oracle]] and [[Verification and Validation]].

The lessons reshaped aviation. Airframes are now tested at full scale through many times their expected lifetime of load cycles; see [[Accelerated life testing]]. Openings have rounded corners. Structures are designed so that a crack in one part does not bring down the whole, and they are inspected on a schedule for cracks before they grow critical; see [[Fail-Safe]] and [[Non-Destructive Testing]].

The redesigned Comet 4 flew from 1958, but by then American jets such as the Boeing 707 had taken the market. The investigation remains a model of [[Root Cause Analysis]].
