The **Tacoma Narrows Bridge** was a suspension bridge across Puget Sound in Washington State that opened on July 1, 1940 and collapsed on November 7 of the same year. Its fall, caught on film, became one of the most widely shown engineering failures ever recorded.

The design pushed slenderness further than any bridge before it. Earlier suspension bridges stiffened their decks with deep open trusses. The Tacoma Narrows deck instead used solid plate girders only about eight feet deep along a main span of roughly 2,800 feet, which made it light, cheap and flexible. From the first weeks, it rose and fell in waves of several feet in ordinary winds, and drivers nicknamed it **Galloping Gertie**.

Engineers knew it moved and were studying it. A team at the University of Washington was testing a scale model in a wind tunnel and proposing fixes, and some temporary cables had been added. The real bridge failed before the work finished; see [[Verification and Validation]].

On the morning of the collapse, a steady wind of around forty miles an hour set off a new kind of motion. The deck began twisting about its centerline, one side rising as the other fell, with the roadway tilting dramatically. After about an hour, suspenders snapped and the center span tore away and dropped into the water. No person died. A dog left in a stranded car was lost.

The collapse is often taught as a textbook case of resonance, with wind gusts pushing the bridge at its natural frequency like a child on a swing; see [[Resonance]]. Engineers have argued since the 1990s that this is misleading. A steady wind has no rhythm to match. The better explanation is **aeroelastic flutter**: once the deck twisted slightly, the airflow over it pushed it further in the same direction, so the motion fed itself and grew. Vortices peeling off the deck probably drove in the earlier vertical galloping but weren't the main cause of the final twisting; see [[Vortex Shedding]] and [[Feedback Loop]].

The lesson for testing was that a structure can fail through a mode that no one checked. Designers had calculated static wind loads carefully but not how the deck's shape interacted dynamically with moving air. Afterward, wind-tunnel tests on scale models of bridge decks became standard, and the replacement bridge, opened in 1950, had deep stiffening trusses and openings to let air through; see [[Margin of Safety]].

The same pattern shows up in other failures: a design reaching beyond experience, with physics outside the model; see [[Hyatt Regency Walkway Collapse]] and [[Hubble Mirror Flaw]]. Small oscillations tolerated as normal until they become catastrophic echo a better-known drift; see [[Normalization of Deviance]].
