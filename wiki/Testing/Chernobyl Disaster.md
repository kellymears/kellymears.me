The **Chernobyl disaster** was the explosion and fire at Reactor 4 of the Chernobyl nuclear plant in northern Ukraine, then part of the Soviet Union, on April 26, 1986. It's the worst accident in the history of nuclear power. And it happened during a safety test.

The test was meant to answer a practical question. If the plant lost outside power, the reactor's cooling pumps would stop until backup diesel generators started, which took about a minute. Engineers wanted to know whether the spinning turbine, coasting down after its steam was cut off, could generate enough electricity to keep the pumps running through that gap. Earlier attempts hadn't given a satisfactory answer.

The test went wrong before it started. It was delayed by about ten hours because the grid needed the plant's power, so it was handed to a night shift that hadn't prepared for it. While the reactor was being brought to low power, its output fell much lower than planned, partly because of a buildup of neutron-absorbing byproducts of fission; see [[Nuclear Fission]]. To raise power again, operators withdrew most of the control rods, leaving far less margin than regulations required. They went ahead with the test anyway; see [[Testing in Production]].

The reactor's design made the situation deadly. In this type of reactor, water absorbs neutrons as well as cooling the core, so when water boiled into steam, the reaction sped up, producing more heat and more steam. This is a positive feedback loop; see [[Positive feedback]]. When the test began and cooling flow fell, power surged. An operator pressed the emergency shutdown button, but a design flaw in the control rods meant their first moments of insertion briefly increased reactivity. Within seconds, the reactor exploded.

Two workers died that night, and around thirty more, mostly firefighters and plant staff, died of acute radiation sickness within months. Radioactive material spread across Europe; see [[Ionizing Radiation]]. The long-term death toll from cancer remains disputed, with estimates ranging from thousands to far more. Some contamination will take centuries to decay; see [[Half-life]]. An exclusion zone of around thirty kilometers still surrounds the site.

Soviet authorities initially blamed the operators. A later international review placed much more weight on the reactor's design and on a safety culture that let known flaws go unaddressed; see [[Just Culture]] and [[Safety Case]]. The accident is also an argument for testing risky procedures in simulation first; see [[Staging Environment]].

Chernobyl followed seven years after an American accident that shared some features; see [[Three Mile Island Accident]]. Both are central to the theory that complex, tightly coupled systems produce accidents no one can foresee; see [[Normal Accidents]].
