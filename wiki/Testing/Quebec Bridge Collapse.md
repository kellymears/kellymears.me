The **Quebec Bridge** crosses the St. Lawrence River just upstream of Quebec City. It was designed as the world's longest cantilever bridge, with steel arms reaching from each pier toward a central span. On 29 August 1907, with the south arm partly built, the structure collapsed into the river in about fifteen seconds, killing 75 of the workers on it. Many were Mohawk ironworkers from the Kahnawake community near Montreal, which lost 33 men in a single day.

The causes were technical and organizational:

- **A longer span, unchecked.** To reduce the cost and risk of building piers in deep water, the main span was lengthened from about 1,600 to 1,800 feet. The bridge's own weight was not properly recalculated after the change. When it finally was, after construction had started, the real dead load was much higher than the figure used in the design, leaving the key members with far less margin than intended; see [[Margin of Safety]].
- **Members that buckled.** The lower chords near the main pier carried enormous compression. Their built-up plates and lattice bracing were too weak to keep them straight, and they began to bend.
- **Remote oversight.** The consulting engineer, an eminent bridge designer in poor health, supervised largely from New York and rarely visited the site. Few people involved had the authority or the independence to check the design.
- **Ignored warnings.** In the final weeks, field inspectors measured growing deflections in the lower chords and reported them. A message to stop adding load was sent but did not reach the site in time to halt work.

A Royal Commission concluded that the design's errors could have been caught with competent review, and that responsibility had been diffuse; see [[Software Inspection]] and [[Requirements Traceability]]. The warnings, by contrast, were explained away while work continued, a pattern seen in many later disasters; see [[Normalization of Deviance]].

The bridge was redesigned with much heavier members. In 1916 the center span fell during hoisting when a casting in the lifting gear failed, killing 13 more workers. The bridge was finally completed in 1917, and its 549-meter main span remains the longest of any cantilever bridge.

The disaster left a lasting cultural mark. In 1925 Canadian engineering schools began the Ritual of the Calling of an Engineer, in which graduates receive an **Iron Ring** worn on the little finger of the working hand as a reminder of their obligations; see [[Rite of Passage]]. A popular story holds that the first rings were made from steel of the collapsed bridge, a claim with no documentary support.

It is often taught alongside other failures of calculation rather than materials; see [[Hyatt Regency Walkway Collapse]] and [[Sinking of the Vasa]].
