The **Space Shuttle Columbia** broke apart over Texas on February 1, 2003, as it returned from a sixteen-day mission, killing all seven crew. The damage that destroyed it had happened more than two weeks earlier, about 82 seconds after launch.

During the climb, a piece of insulating foam broke off the external fuel tank and struck the leading edge of the left wing. The foam was light, under a kilogram, but the shuttle was moving fast relative to it, so it hit hard; see [[Kinetic energy]]. The wing's leading edge was made of reinforced carbon panels designed to withstand the extreme heat of reentry. The strike punched a hole in one of them. When Columbia reentered the atmosphere, superheated gas flowed through the breach and melted the wing's internal structure; see [[Heat Transfer]].

Foam shedding was a known problem. Foam had struck shuttles on many earlier flights. Originally treated as a safety issue, foam strikes had gradually been reclassified as a maintenance matter because no strike had yet caused a disaster; see [[Normalization of Deviance]].

After the launch, engineers reviewing film saw the strike and asked for satellite or ground imaging of the wing in orbit. Managers declined. An analysis using a mathematical model concluded the damage was probably not serious, but the model had been built and checked against tests with small debris, and this piece of foam was hundreds of times larger than anything in that data. The result was an extrapolation dressed up as a conclusion; see [[Verification and Validation]]. Some engineers raised concerns by email but didn't push them up the chain; see [[Psychological Safety]].

The **Columbia Accident Investigation Board** reported in August 2003. To settle doubts that soft foam could break a reinforced wing panel, investigators fired a block of foam from a compressed-gas gun at a real leading-edge panel. It blew open a hole about forty centimeters across. The test settled the question; see [[Load Testing]].

The board concluded that the organizational causes were as important as the physical one, and that NASA had repeated patterns from the Challenger accident seventeen years earlier: schedule pressure, tolerance of anomalies, and a culture where engineers had to prove danger rather than managers having to prove safety; see [[Space Shuttle Challenger Disaster]] and [[Just Culture]].

The shuttle program changed afterward. Every subsequent flight included an inspection of the heat shield in orbit, using a camera on an extended boom, and a plan for a rescue mission if damage was found; see [[Non-Destructive Testing]]. The program was retired in 2011. Whether the crew could have been saved had the damage been found in time remains uncertain, though the board described a possible rescue as challenging but feasible; see [[Precautionary Principle]].
