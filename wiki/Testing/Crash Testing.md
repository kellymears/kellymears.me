**Crash testing** is the deliberate destruction of vehicles under controlled conditions to measure how well they protect the people inside. Cars are driven or pulled into barriers, struck from the side by moving carts, and rolled over, while high-speed cameras and onboard sensors record the event. It is the opposite of [[Non-Destructive Testing]]: the specimen is used up so that the answer can be measured.

The occupants are **crash test dummies**, formally anthropomorphic test devices. Before reliable dummies existed, researchers learned about human tolerance from cadavers, animals and volunteers. The most famous volunteer, the Air Force flight surgeon John Paul Stapp, rode rocket sleds in the 1940s and 1950s, in 1954 stopping from over 1,000 kilometers per hour in under a second and a half and enduring 46 times the force of gravity; see [[Newton's Laws of Motion]].

Modern dummies are precision instruments. The Hybrid III, introduced by General Motors in 1976 and still the most widely used frontal dummy, has a steel skeleton, a flesh-like skin, a neck designed to bend like a human one, and accelerometers and load cells throughout, measuring forces on the head, neck, chest and legs. Those readings are converted into estimates of injury risk, such as the likelihood of a head injury from a given acceleration; see [[Instrumentation]] and [[Concussion]].

Public ratings turned testing into a market force. The United States began its New Car Assessment Program in 1979, and Euro NCAP started in 1997 with star ratings. One early model, the Rover 100, earned a single star in Euro NCAP's frontal test and was withdrawn from sale soon after. Manufacturers quickly learned to design for the ratings, and deaths per mile driven fell substantially over the following decades; see [[Benchmarking]].

Designing for a test also means designing for its blind spots; see [[Goodhart's Law]] and [[Pesticide Paradox]]. In 2012 the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety introduced a small overlap test, striking only the outer quarter of the front of the car, and many highly rated vehicles performed poorly. Manufacturers improved the driver's side. When the institute later tested the passenger side, some models did markedly worse there, suggesting the fixes had targeted the test rather than the hazard.

The dummies themselves encode assumptions. The standard frontal dummy represents an average adult man, and the common small female dummy has largely been a scaled-down version of it. A 2019 study from the University of Virginia found that belted women in frontal crashes had about 73 percent higher odds of serious injury than belted men, after adjusting for other factors. Researchers in Sweden have since developed a dummy based on an average woman's body; see [[Selection Bias]].

Much testing now happens first in simulation, using computer models of the human body; see [[Model-Based Testing]] and [[Acceptance Testing]].
