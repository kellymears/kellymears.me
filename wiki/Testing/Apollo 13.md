**Apollo 13** was the third NASA mission meant to land on the Moon. It launched on April 11, 1970 with James Lovell, Jack Swigert and Fred Haise aboard. About fifty-six hours into the flight, an oxygen tank in the service module exploded, crippling the spacecraft's power and air supply. The crew abandoned the landing, swung around the Moon, and returned to Earth on April 17. The crew's radio call, "Houston, we've had a problem," became one of the best-known lines of the space program.

The explosion was the end of a chain of small problems going back years. The tank had heaters and a fan inside to keep the liquid oxygen at the right pressure. The heaters had thermostatic switches designed to open if the tank got too hot. The original specification called for 28-volt switches. When the requirements changed to let ground equipment run the heaters at 65 volts, the change didn't reach the switches; see [[Requirements Traceability]].

In 1968, the tank was dropped about two inches during maintenance at the factory, probably damaging a tube inside. During a countdown test weeks before launch, the tank wouldn't empty normally, so technicians switched on the heaters for hours to boil the oxygen off. At the higher voltage, the switches welded shut instead of opening. Temperatures inside climbed far beyond design limits and charred the insulation on the fan wiring. No one saw it, because the temperature gauge on the ground equipment only read up to 80 degrees Fahrenheit. A thermometer that cannot show the failure cannot warn of it; see [[Instrumentation]] and [[Boundary Value Analysis]].

In flight, when the crew stirred the tanks, a spark from the damaged wiring ignited the insulation in pure oxygen. The tank burst and damaged the second tank.

The recovery depended on the lunar module, built to support two people on the Moon for under two days. It became a lifeboat for three for four days. Engineers on the ground improvised procedures and tested them in simulators before reading them up to the crew; see [[Hardware-in-the-Loop]]. The command module's square carbon dioxide filters didn't fit the lunar module's round sockets, and the ground team designed an adapter from hoses, cardboard, plastic bags and tape using only items aboard.

The review board found a mix of design, testing and procedure failures, none catastrophic alone. Each had passed its own checks, and the danger existed only in their combination; see [[Normal Accidents]] and [[Root Cause Analysis]].

Apollo 13 is often set beside Apollo 11's computer alarms, where a design had margins that let the crew continue; see [[Apollo 11 Program Alarms]]. It also stands in contrast to later shuttle disasters, where warnings were known but discounted; see [[Space Shuttle Challenger Disaster]].
