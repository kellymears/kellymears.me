The **Space Shuttle Challenger** broke apart 73 seconds after launch on January 28, 1986, killing all seven people aboard. Millions watched it live, including schoolchildren tuned in because one of the crew was a teacher selected to be the first in space.

The physical cause was a seal. Each solid rocket booster was built in segments, and the joints between them were sealed by rubber O-rings. On launch, hot gas pressure briefly flexed the joint open, and the O-rings had to spring back fast enough to keep the seal. Cold rubber is stiff and slow to recover; see [[Elasticity]] and [[Glass transition]]. The morning of the launch was far colder than any previous shuttle launch, near freezing. A joint on the right booster leaked flame, which burned through to the external fuel tank.

The flaw was not a surprise. Inspections after earlier flights had found O-rings partly eroded or scorched by gas that got past them. Each time, the damage was analyzed, judged acceptable, and the next flight went ahead. A seal never designed to erode at all came to be treated as having a safety margin because erosion had not yet caused a failure; see [[Margin of Safety]]. The sociologist Diane Vaughan later named this pattern, where a deviation becomes routine through repeated survival; see [[Normalization of Deviance]].

The night before launch, engineers at the booster's contractor argued against flying in the cold. In a teleconference, managers pushed back, and the contractor's management reversed its engineers' recommendation. The burden of proof had quietly flipped: instead of having to show the shuttle was safe to fly, the engineers were asked to prove it was unsafe; see [[Groupthink]] and [[Psychological Safety]].

The presidential commission that investigated, known as the **Rogers Commission**, is best remembered for a moment at a televised hearing in which the physicist Richard Feynman dropped a piece of O-ring material into a glass of ice water and showed that it lost its resilience. Feynman's appended report noted that engineers estimated the chance of losing a shuttle at roughly one in a hundred, while management claimed figures closer to one in a hundred thousand, and closed with the line that reality must take precedence over public relations, for nature cannot be fooled.

Challenger became a standard case study in engineering ethics and safety management, alongside the [[Citicorp Center Engineering Crisis]]. It shows how risk analysis can be bent by schedule pressure, and how a formal argument for safety is only as good as the evidence behind it; see [[Safety Case]] and [[Failure Mode and Effects Analysis]]. It's easy to see the warning signs afterward; see [[Hindsight Bias]]. Seventeen years later, a second shuttle was lost to a similar pattern of tolerated anomalies; see [[Space Shuttle Columbia Disaster]].
