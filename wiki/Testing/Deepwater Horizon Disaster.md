On the night of 20 April 2010, gas and oil surged up the Macondo well in the Gulf of Mexico and onto the drilling rig **Deepwater Horizon**, off the coast of Louisiana. The gas ignited, the explosion and fire killed eleven workers, and the rig sank two days later. Oil flowed from the seabed, about 1,500 meters down, for 87 days before the well was capped. The government's estimate of roughly 4.9 million barrels released made it the largest marine oil spill in history.

A deepwater well is supposed to be protected by several independent barriers. All of them failed in sequence:

- **The cement.** The bottom of the well was sealed with cement that, investigators concluded, failed to isolate the oil and gas reservoir. Lab tests of the mixture had been worrying, and a full evaluation was skipped.
- **The pressure test.** Before removing heavy drilling fluid from the well, the crew ran a **negative pressure test**, lowering the pressure inside the well to see whether anything leaked in. Pressure built up on the drill pipe when it should have stayed at zero, a clear sign that the well was not sealed. After discussion, the crew accepted an explanation, a so-called bladder effect, that has no basis in engineering, and judged the test a success; see [[Test Oracle]] and [[Confirmation Bias]].
- **Monitoring.** As fluid was removed, signs of oil and gas flowing into the well appeared in the data for roughly forty minutes, while crews were busy with other operations, and were not recognized; see [[Alarm fatigue]].
- **The blowout preventer.** The massive valve stack on the seabed was the last line of defense. Its blind shear ram, designed to cut through the drill pipe and seal the well, activated but failed to close fully, probably because the pipe had buckled sideways and was off-center in the cutter.; see [[Fail-Safe]] and [[Single Point of Failure]].

The National Commission on the spill reported in January 2011 that the failures were not the work of one rogue actor but the product of systemic management problems at the operator BP, the rig owner Transocean and the cementing contractor Halliburton. Decisions that saved time and money added risk, and nobody had a full picture of how those risks combined; see [[Normalization of Deviance]] and [[Common-Mode Failure]].

The disaster is a case study in how a test is only useful if its failure is accepted as a failure. The negative pressure test did exactly what it was meant to do. Its readers, under pressure, found a story that let them see success; see [[Safety Case]] and [[Fault Tree Analysis]]. It is often compared with other system accidents in which layered defenses fail together; see [[Normal Accidents]].
