On 27 March 1977, two Boeing 747s collided on the fog-covered runway of Los Rodeos airport on the island of Tenerife in the Canary Islands. KLM Flight 4805 began its takeoff roll while Pan Am Flight 1736 was still taxiing toward it on the same runway. 583 people died, and it remains the deadliest accident in aviation history.

Neither plane was meant to be there. A bomb at the main airport on Gran Canaria had diverted dozens of flights to small Los Rodeos. Parked aircraft filled the taxiway, so departing planes had to taxi down the runway itself and turn around at the end. The KLM crew also faced strict duty-time limits that a long delay could exceed.

A chain of small problems lined up:

- **Fog.** Visibility fell to a few hundred meters, so neither crew could see the other plane, and the tower could not see either. The airport had no ground radar.
- **A missed exit.** The Pan Am crew was told to leave the runway at the third exit but missed the sharply angled turn in the fog and continued on.
- **Ambiguous words.** The KLM copilot read back a route clearance and added "we are now at takeoff," while the plane was already starting to roll. The controller replied "OK," then "stand by for takeoff, I will call you." The controller meant to approve only the route; the KLM crew heard permission to go; see [[Plain Language]].
- **Blocked transmission.** Pan Am radioed at the same moment that it was still on the runway. The two transmissions overlapped and produced a loud squeal on the KLM frequency, blocking the crucial "stand by" and most of the Pan Am warning; see [[Wave Interference]].

The KLM flight engineer asked whether Pan Am was clear of the runway. The captain, a senior pilot who ran the airline's training program, answered emphatically that it was. Nobody challenged the decision again. The aircraft collided seconds later.

The aftermath changed aviation in two ways. First, phraseology was standardized. The word "takeoff" is now used only in an actual takeoff clearance, and "departure" is used otherwise, so that no phrase can be read both ways; see [[Two Generals Problem]].

Second, the accident became a founding case for **crew resource management**, training that teaches flight crews to share information, challenge decisions and speak up regardless of rank. Airlines adopted it widely in the 1980s, and surgery later borrowed it; see [[Psychological Safety]] and [[Checklist]].

Investigators described a failure that no single person or device caused, the layered failure pattern that safety researchers call the [[Swiss cheese model]]. Much of aviation safety since then aims to keep the holes from aligning; see [[Normal Accidents]].
