**Solitaire** is the family of card games played by one person, usually called **patience** in British English. Descriptions appear in Europe from the late eighteenth century, and books of patience games multiplied through the nineteenth. Most share a shape: cards are dealt into a layout, and the player moves them according to rules that build ordered sequences, aiming to sort the whole deck into foundation piles by suit.

The best-known variant in North America is **Klondike**, probably named after the Canadian gold rush of the 1890s. Seven columns are dealt with only their top cards face up. Cards are built down in alternating colors, and the rest of the deck is turned through in ones or threes. Spider, Pyramid and Golf are other common variants.

Klondike's fame comes from computers. Windows 3.0 included it in 1990, in a version written by Wes Cherry, then a Microsoft intern, with card designs by Susan Kare; see [[Skeuomorphism]]. Microsoft saw it partly as training, since dragging cards taught new users to point, click and hold with a mouse; see [[Drag and drop]]. It became one of the most-used programs on the system, prompting periodic complaints about lost office hours. In 2006 New York's mayor fired a city employee after seeing the game on a work screen. Windows Solitaire entered the World Video Game Hall of Fame in 2019.

**FreeCell**, written by Jim Horne and bundled with later versions of Windows, offers nearly pure skill. All cards are dealt face up, and four free cells hold cards temporarily. Of the 32,000 numbered deals in the original version, only one, deal 11982, is known to be unwinnable; see [[Perfect Information]]. Klondike is different. Cards stay hidden, so a player can make every reasonable move and still lose. Researchers estimate that most deals, roughly four in five, could be won by someone who could see every card, but actual win rates are far lower, and estimates have come from computer search and simulation rather than proof; see [[Solved Game]] and [[Monte Carlo Method]]. Digital versions shuffle with algorithms such as the [[Fisher-Yates Shuffle]].

Part of solitaire's appeal is its low stakes. A game takes a few minutes, needs no opponent and offers steady small decisions with a satisfying cascade at the end, which puts it in the same niche as [[Sudoku]], the [[Crossword]] and [[Tetris]]. Many players describe it as a way to occupy the hands while the mind settles, close to the absorption of [[Flow]]. Unlimited undo turns some variants into puzzles solved by trial and error; see [[Save Scumming]].

The tile game **mahjong solitaire** follows the same single-player template with different pieces; see [[Mahjong]]. Its mobile-era descendants include the many match-and-clear games that fill phone app stores; see [[Match-Three]].
