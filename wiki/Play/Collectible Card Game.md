A **collectible card game** (CCG), also called a trading card game, is a game in which each player builds their own deck from a personal collection of cards, acquired mostly in randomized booster packs. Two players bring different decks to the table, so every match pits two players' choices against each other before the first card is drawn.

The form began with *Magic: The Gathering*, designed by Richard Garfield and released by Wizards of the Coast in 1993. Its first print run sold out almost immediately. Players spend **mana**, generated by land cards, to cast spells and summon creatures, and much of deck construction is about the **mana curve**: having enough cheap cards to act early and enough powerful cards to win late. *Pokémon* (1996) and *Yu-Gi-Oh!* (1999) followed and reached enormous audiences of children and adults.

The genre's appeal rests on a few layers:

- **Deck building.** Players choose usually sixty cards from thousands, looking for combinations that work together; see [[Deckbuilder]] and [[Engine Building]].
- **The metagame.** At any given time, a few strong decks dominate, and players build to beat or exploit them; see [[Metagame]] and [[Tier List]].
- **Collecting.** Rare cards are printed in lower quantities, so opening packs carries the excitement of a lottery; see [[Variable-Ratio Reinforcement]].
- **Trading.** Players swap cards, and a secondary market sets prices, with a few rare *Magic* cards selling for hundreds of thousands of dollars; see [[Veblen Good]].

The business model shapes the design. Publishers release new sets regularly, and new cards have to be appealing enough to buy, which tends to make them stronger than old ones; see [[Power Creep]]. To keep the game manageable, most CCGs rotate older sets out of their main competitive formats, which frustrates players who invested in them. Critics have long argued that randomized packs are a form of gambling, especially for children.

Digital CCGs such as *Hearthstone* (2014) moved the genre online. Without physical cards, they could add effects that generate random cards or transform them mid-game, impossible to track on a table; see [[Pseudorandom Number Generator]]. Most are [[Free-to-Play]], selling packs with in-game currency and often guaranteeing a rare card after a run of bad luck; see [[Pity Timer]] and [[Gacha Monetization]]. Digital cards usually can't be traded or resold, which removes the secondary market but keeps the spending.

Living card games and other fixed-distribution formats respond to these criticisms by selling expansions with known contents. Draft formats, where players open packs and pick cards one at a time to build a deck on the spot, even out collection size.

The genre's influence spread widely: roguelike deckbuilders such as *Slay the Spire* borrow its card design while discarding the collection entirely; see [[Roguelike Deckbuilder]].
