**Power creep** is the gradual increase in the strength of new content in a game that keeps releasing it. Each new card, character, weapon, or expansion is a little stronger than what came before, until older content is obsolete and the whole game is played at a higher power level than it was designed for.

It's driven by incentives more than by mistakes. New content has to sell or get played, and content weaker than what players already own has no reason to exist. Making new things merely different, without being stronger, is hard to do well and even harder to market to existing players. So designers lean slightly stronger each time, and the small steps compound. The result resembles the [[Hedonic Treadmill]]: each release raises the baseline that the next must beat.

The pattern is clearest in:

- **Collectible card games.** Magic: The Gathering, around since 1993, has seen creatures grow steadily stronger for their cost. Its designers have openly and repeatedly discussed managing creep.
- **Gacha and live-service games.** New characters are what players pay for, so they must be appealing, which usually means powerful; see [[Gacha Monetization]] and [[Live Service Game]].
- **MMORPG expansions.** Each raises the level cap, making the previous expansion's best gear worthless.

Consequences:

- **Old content dies.** Players stop using it, and new players never encounter it.
- **Barriers rise.** New and returning players face a steep climb, since the game assumes everything recent.
- **The metagame narrows.** A few newest options dominate; see [[Metagame]] and [[Tier List]].
- **Numbers inflate.** Health and damage grow into the millions, sometimes forcing a "stat squish" that rescales everything downward.

Designers manage it with several tools:

- **Rotation.** Formats where only recent sets are legal, so older cards leave play intentionally rather than becoming useless.
- **Balance patches.** In digital games, strengthening weak options (a *buff*) and weakening strong ones (a *nerf*). Nerfs prevent runaway creep, but players who spent money on something resent seeing it weakened.
- **Sidegrades.** New options that are different rather than better, solving new problems instead of the same ones harder.
- **Resets.** Seasonal structures where everyone starts fresh.

Power creep has parallels outside games. Software accumulates features each release because new versions must justify upgrades; see [[Feature Creep]]. Credentials inflate as more people hold them, and metrics drift upward once they become targets; see [[Goodhart's Law]]. The common mechanism is a ratchet: each step is easy to take and hard to undo, the same asymmetry that makes [[Technical Debt]] grow.
