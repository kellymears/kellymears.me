**A Let's Play** is a recording of someone playing a video game while talking about it. Live versions, broadcast as they happen, are called game streaming. Millions of people now watch others play games, often more than they play themselves.

The term came from the forums of the website Something Awful in the mid-2000s, where members posted playthroughs, first as screenshots with captions and then as videos with spoken commentary. YouTube carried the format to a mass audience in the early 2010s, and the most popular channels drew audiences in the tens of millions. Live streaming grew alongside it. Justin.tv, a general live video site, spun off its gaming section as Twitch in 2011, and Amazon bought Twitch in 2014 for nearly a billion dollars.

People watch for different reasons:

- **Personality.** Many viewers come for the person, not the game, and feel they know them; see [[Parasocial Relationship]].
- **Company.** A stream in the background provides a sense of others around while working or relaxing; see [[Body Doubling]] and [[Loneliness]].
- **Skill.** Top players and competitions draw audiences like sports; see [[Esports]] and [[Speedrun]]. Charity speedrun marathons have raised millions of dollars.
- **Experience by proxy.** Viewers see games they can't afford, can't play, or would find too frightening.

Streams also produced new forms. In 2014, Twitch Plays Pokémon let tens of thousands of viewers control one game through chat commands at once, and finished it after more than two weeks of chaos, with the crowd voting between anarchy and democracy modes.

Streaming changed the games business. Games that are fun to watch, with surprising moments, social drama, or spectacular failure, can be lifted by streams; see [[Social Deduction Game]] and [[Emergent Gameplay]]. The social deduction game Among Us, released in 2018, became a phenomenon two years later largely through streams. Story-driven games face the opposite risk: once viewers have seen the story, some feel no need to buy the game; see [[Walking Simulator]] and [[Spoiler Etiquette]].

Ownership is unsettled, and law in most countries hasn't directly answered whether a commentated playthrough is a new work. Publishers hold the copyright in their games' footage and music, and most tolerate streams as free marketing, but they can and sometimes do issue takedowns. Copyrighted music played in the background has been a frequent cause of removed videos.

Streaming turned attention into income for players, with subscriptions, donations, and sponsorships, which rewards constant presence and can push streamers toward exhausting schedules; see [[Attention Economy]] and [[Burnout]].

Recorded playthroughs have also become a record of how games looked and played, useful when the games themselves are lost; see [[Game Preservation]].
