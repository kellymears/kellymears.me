**Esports** are organized video game competitions, from local tournaments to professional leagues with salaried players, coaches, sponsors, and audiences of millions. Unlike traditional sports, every esport is a product owned by a company, which shapes how the scene works.

Competitive play is as old as video games. In 1972 Stanford University hosted a tournament of *Spacewar!* whose prize was a magazine subscription. Atari's *Space Invaders* championship in 1980 drew about ten thousand entrants. High-score tables in arcades made rankings public, and early networked shooters like *Quake* in the 1990s brought online and LAN tournaments; see [[Arcade Game]].

South Korea built the first professional scene. In the late 1990s cheap internet cafes spread after the Asian financial crisis, and *StarCraft* became a national pastime. Cable channels broadcast matches, sponsors paid salaries, and top players became celebrities. The scene also produced esports' first major match-fixing scandal in 2010.

Online streaming from about 2011 let anyone watch, and audiences grew enormously; see [[Parasocial Relationship]]. Publishers built world championships for games like *League of Legends* and *Dota 2*, whose prize pool, crowdfunded through in-game purchases, passed 40 million dollars in 2021; see [[Battle Pass]]. Fighting games kept a grassroots scene, built around the open tournament now called Evo, whose roots go back to 1996, where anyone who pays the entry fee can face the best; see [[Fighting Game]] and [[Rollback Netcode]].

What distinguishes esports:

- **Publisher ownership.** No one owns football, but a publisher controls its game, its rules, and who may run tournaments. Leagues have been launched and closed at a company's discretion, as with franchised leagues that charged teams large fees and later folded.
- **Patches.** Balance updates change the game mid-season, reshaping which strategies dominate; see [[Metagame]]. Players must relearn a game they had mastered, sometimes weeks before a championship.
- **Spectating.** Games must be readable to viewers, with observer modes, replays, and casters who explain what is happening; see [[Visual Hierarchy]].
- **Integrity.** Competition requires defenses against cheating software, match-fixing, and account sharing; see [[Anti-Cheat]].
- **Short careers.** Reaction speed peaks early, and long practice schedules lead many players to retire in their twenties; see [[Burnout]].

Many genres now have scenes: real-time strategy, team shooters, battle royales, card games, racing simulators, and speedruns; see [[Battle Royale]] and [[Speedrun]]. Ranked online ladders feed the top of the pyramid, using skill ratings descended from chess; see [[Elo Rating System]].

Whether esports are sports remains debated, mostly over physical exertion. They share the essentials of rules, training, competition, and spectators, and the boundary is a matter of convention; see [[Magic Circle]]. Some countries already grant professional players the visas given to traditional athletes.
