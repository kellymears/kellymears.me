**Fast travel** lets a player jump instantly to a location they have already visited, usually by choosing it on a map. It is standard in large open-world games, where walking back across a map for the fifth time would be tedious. It is also one of the clearest trade-offs in game design: every shortcut saves the player time and, in exchange, makes the world feel a little smaller.

The tension shows in a long-running series. The Elder Scrolls III: Morrowind, in 2002, offered travel through the fiction: hired giant insects called silt striders, boats, guild teleporters and spells that could mark a spot and recall to it. Using them required knowing the routes. Its sequel, Oblivion, in 2006, let players click any discovered location on the map and arrive there. That was more convenient, and players still argue over whether it emptied out the journey.

Other designers withhold fast travel to make the world itself the reward. Dark Souls, in 2011, didn't allow warping between its rest points until well into the game. Until then players crossed the world on foot and discovered shortcuts that looped back to places they knew, which made the map memorable; see [[Soulslike]] and [[Level Design]]. A [[Metroidvania]] usually adds teleport rooms late, once the player has learned the layout and backtracking has become chores. Survival modes in several open-world games remove fast travel entirely to restore danger and distance.

A common compromise is to make discovery the key. A location becomes a destination only once the player has reached it on foot, so the map fills in as the player explores and learns it; see [[Cognitive Map]] and [[Wayfinding]].

Fast travel shapes the content around it. It makes errands across the map bearable, and so encourages them; see [[Fetch quest]]. It can strand a game's [[Environmental Storytelling]] in places players skip after the first visit. It also interacts with [[Time as Resource]]: games with day-night cycles or hunger often make travel cost in-game hours, so the convenience still has a price. Some games are built around the trip itself, such as Death Stranding, in 2019, which makes route planning and carrying cargo the main activity, a distant relative of the [[Walking Simulator]].

Players also find unintended ways to travel quickly. Speedrunners hunt for glitches that warp them to places they shouldn't reach yet; see [[Sequence Breaking]]. And designers use the same machinery to return a player to a safe spot after failure; see [[Checkpoint]].

The question for each game is what the walking is for. If travel offers discovery, danger or decisions, fast travel removes something valuable. If it's dead time, fast travel removes only boredom.
