**Procedural rhetoric** is the practice of persuading through rules and processes rather than through words or images. Ian Bogost introduced the term in his 2007 book *Persuasive Games*. Every game models some part of the world, and in deciding what players can do, what is rewarded, and what is left out, the model makes claims about how that part of the world works.

Bogost's examples included political and commercial games, but the principle applies to any game. *SimCity* lets players zone land, build roads, and set taxes, and rewards growth; crime falls when more police stations are built, and public transit reduces traffic. Whatever its designers intended, the game argues that cities are engineering problems solved from above; see [[City Builder]]. *Monopoly* began in 1904 as Lizzie Magie's *The Landlord's Game*, designed to show how land ownership concentrated wealth, and its rules still produce that outcome: one player ends up owning nearly everything.

Several kinds of games use procedural rhetoric deliberately:

- **Newsgames.** Small games about current events, such as *September 12th* (2003), in which bombing terrorists in a village also kills civilians, whose mourners then become new terrorists. No strategy wins.
- **Serious games.** Games for training, education, or health, which teach through doing.
- **Art games.** Games built to provoke reflection. *Papers, Please* (2013) makes the player a border inspector forced to choose between rules, bribes, and the family's survival; the pressure of the job's systems is the point.

The concept explains why some games feel at odds with their stories. If a narrative presents a character as gentle while the rules reward killing hundreds of people, the two arguments conflict; see [[Ludonarrative Dissonance]]. It also explains why choices in a story-driven game can feel meaningless when the rules give every path the same result; see [[Branching Narrative]].

Procedural rhetoric extends beyond games. Software interfaces encode views about how people should behave: defaults, required fields, and what a form allows all argue for particular choices; see [[Choice Architecture]] and [[Dark Pattern]]. Economic simulations, epidemic models, and climate models persuade through what they include and leave out; see [[Map and Territory]]. And gamified apps teach that what counts is what gets points; see [[Gamification]].

Critics argue that players often don't read rules as arguments at all, playing to win rather than to understand, and that the same mechanics can carry opposite meanings to different players. Bogost's reply is that the persuasion works whether or not it is noticed, much as an interface shapes behavior without users thinking about it; see [[Affordance]].

For designers, the lesson is to ask what a system implies when someone plays it optimally. Rules, rewards, and failure states say what matters more forcefully than any text; see [[MDA Framework]] and [[Compulsion Loop]].
