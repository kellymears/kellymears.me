**Free-to-play** is a pricing model where entry costs nothing and revenue is collected afterward, through purchases layered on top of a game a player already owns for free.

The economics run on a small paying minority. **Conversion rate** — the share of players who ever pay — sits in the low single digits for most titles, and revenue within that group is whale-weighted: a small fraction of payers accounts for most of the income, so the decisions with the largest financial stake are aimed at a handful of players, a skew that any average-revenue-per-user figure conceals by construction. **Battle passes** are a subscription rebuilt as a track: a fixed term that makes [[Time as Resource]] the lever, rewards gated behind play or payment, recurring revenue without the word, its curve ordinary [[Meta-Progression]] with a price attached to skipping it.

The load-bearing distinction is **cosmetic versus competitive monetisation**. Selling appearance leaves the underlying game intact and is broadly tolerated; selling power — stronger gear, better odds — changes what skill means, and most backlash traces to games that blurred that line rather than to the price of entry. Both lean on the same machinery: the [[Compulsion Loop]] a session is built around, its intermittent payouts running on the same [[Variable-Ratio Reinforcement]] schedule that makes gambling hard to walk away from, and [[Gacha Monetization]], where randomized rewards replace a fixed price entirely.

Design bends around the paying minority in ways non-paying players absorb without noticing — a bundle's [[Anchoring Effect]] against which a smaller purchase reads as restraint. Once revenue per player becomes the metric a studio optimises, [[Goodhart's Law]] takes hold, and the constant tuning that requires is what the [[Live Service Game]] cadence exists to support.
