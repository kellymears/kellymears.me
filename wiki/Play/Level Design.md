**Level design** is the craft of building the spaces where a game is played: the layout of platforms and rooms, where enemies and items sit, what the player sees first, and the order challenges arrive in. Mechanics define what a player can do; levels decide when, where, and in what combination they have to do it. A strong mechanic in a weak level feels thin, and good levels can make a few simple verbs last a whole game.

The most studied example is **World 1-1** of *Super Mario Bros.* (1985). The player starts on the left side of an empty screen, which suggests moving right. The first Goomba walks toward Mario in a spot where jumping over it, or failing and learning, is nearly unavoidable. A block marked with a question mark hangs overhead, inviting a jump that reveals a mushroom, which then slides into the player even if they try to avoid it. Without text, the level teaches running, jumping, enemies, and power-ups in the first few seconds.

Common principles:

- **Teach, test, twist.** Introduce a mechanic safely, test it with some danger, then combine it with something else. Nintendo designers have described a four-step version borrowed from **kishōtenketsu**, a structure from East Asian storytelling: introduction, development, twist, conclusion.
- **Guiding the eye.** Light, color, contrast, and lines of architecture pull attention toward the path forward; see [[Visual Hierarchy]] and [[Wayfinding]]. Yellow paint on climbable ledges is a modern, much-debated version of the same idea; see [[Signifier]].
- **Pacing.** Tense stretches alternate with quiet ones so players can recover, and difficulty generally rises over a level and across the game; see [[Difficulty Curve]] and [[Flow]].
- **Landmarks and sightlines.** A distant tower visible from many places orients players and gives them a goal, as in [[Open World Design]].
- **Gating.** Locked doors, abilities, and keys control the order in which areas open up; see [[Metroidvania]] and [[Sequence Breaking]].

Competitive multiplayer maps follow different rules. They must be fair to both sides, offer several routes so no single chokepoint dominates, and give players cover and sightlines that reward positioning; see [[Cover System]]. Map designers watch heat maps of where players die to find spots that are too strong.

Level design overlaps heavily with storytelling. Rooms can tell a story through what is left in them, such as a barricaded door or a half-eaten meal; see [[Environmental Storytelling]]. Architecture can show what kind of place this is before anyone speaks.

Levels can also be generated by algorithms, trading hand-tuned pacing for variety; see [[Procedural Generation]] and [[Roguelike]]. Most generators still work from hand-built pieces, because designers struggle to encode the judgment that makes a room feel right.
