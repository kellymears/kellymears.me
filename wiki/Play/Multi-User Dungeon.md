A **multi-user dungeon** (MUD) is a text-based virtual world shared by many players at once over a network. Players type commands such as "go north" or "attack troll," read descriptions of rooms, and talk with others present in the same place. MUDs began in 1978 and were the direct ancestors of today's massively multiplayer online games.

The first, MUD1, was written by Roy Trubshaw and Richard Bartle at the University of Essex in 1978, inspired by text adventures like *Zork* and by tabletop role-playing; see [[Interactive Fiction]] and [[Tabletop Role-Playing Game]]. In 1980 Essex connected to the network that would become the internet, letting players join from abroad; see [[ARPANET]]. Bartle later drew on years of watching MUD players to describe four types of player motivation; see [[Bartle Taxonomy]].

MUDs spread through universities in the late 1980s and 1990s, with codebases freely copied and modified. Several families emerged:

- **Combat-focused MUDs** such as DikuMUD (1990), with classes, levels, and experience from killing monsters. Their design passed almost directly into *EverQuest* (1999) and then *World of Warcraft* (2004); see [[Computer Role-Playing Game]].
- **Social MUDs** such as TinyMUD and **MOOs** (MUD, object-oriented), where players could build rooms and program objects. LambdaMOO, run from Xerox PARC from 1990, became a famous online community.
- **Role-play MUDs**, where staying in character was a requirement.

LambdaMOO produced an early debate about online harm. In 1993 a player used a programming trick to make other characters perform sexual acts in public text. The journalist Julian Dibbell wrote about it in "A Rape in Cyberspace," and the community's struggle to respond led it to create its own petition and voting system. MUDs also first met the problems of [[Griefing]] and runaway in-game inflation; see [[Faucets and Sinks]].

Graphics arrived through games such as *Meridian 59* (1996) and *Ultima Online* (1997), whose creator Richard Garriott popularized the term MMORPG. *World of Warcraft* passed 12 million subscribers in 2010. These games kept the MUD structure of zones, quests, guilds, and continuous worlds, and ran as ongoing services updated for years; see [[Live Service Game]].

Researchers Constance Steinkuehler and Dmitri Williams argued in 2006 that online games serve as informal gathering places, neither home nor work, where people meet regulars and form loose ties; see [[Third Place]]. Guild sizes often settle near the limit of stable social relationships; see [[Dunbar's Number]].

MUDs connected to the same culture as other text-era community spaces; see [[Bulletin Board System]] and [[Internet Relay Chat]]. A few hundred are still running, played through clients that look much like a [[Terminal User Interface]].
