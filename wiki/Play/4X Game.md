**4X Game** names itself after its own verbs, coined by *Alan Emrich* in a 1993 *Civilization II* preview: explore the map, expand onto it, exploit its resources, exterminate whoever else was doing the same thing. The genre's shape follows from those four verbs in order — a slow, low-information opening gives way to a midgame of parallel economic engines, which gives way to a late game where those engines convert into armies and the map's remaining ambiguity resolves into a winner.

*Civilization* is the reference case: a symmetric start on an unrevealed map, [[Fog of War]] converting exploration into a real resource race, and a victory condition (military, cultural, scientific, diplomatic) that gives the whole arc a finish line. *Master of Orion* ran the same arc in space; *Stellaris* dresses 4X's opening in the same clothes before drifting into [[Grand Strategy Game]] territory once its midgame narrative systems (crises, ethics, internal politics) start generating story that outlives the explore-expand arc.

The genre's known failure mode is the "empire ceiling": a 4X that scales well through expansion tends to collapse under its own bookkeeping once expansion stops, because the same systems that made early turns exciting (managing ten cities, ten unit types) become tedious at fifty. Most of the genre's late-history design effort — automation toggles, city-state vassals, streamlined late-game tech — is really an argument about which turns of a 4X are worth a human's attention and which should be handed to the AI.

4X is frequently mistaken for [[City Builder]] because both involve zoning and growth, but a city builder has no exterminate verb and usually no other empire to race against — its opponent is entropy inside a single city, not a rival civilization on the same map.
