An **asymmetric multiplayer** game gives its players or teams different abilities, goals, resources or information, rather than mirror-image starting positions. Chess and most sports are symmetric, so the sides differ only in who moves first. In an asymmetric game, one side might be a single powerful monster and the other a team of fragile humans, or each player might follow an entirely separate rulebook.

The idea is old. Medieval Scandinavian tafl games pitted a king and a few defenders against a larger attacking force with a different objective. Historical [[Wargame]] scenarios routinely give the sides different forces and victory conditions. Board games developed **hidden movement**: in Scotland Yard, from 1983, one player moves the fugitive Mister X in secret while the others hunt, getting only occasional glimpses; see [[Hidden information]] and [[Fog of War]]. Root, from 2018, gave each of its four factions a distinct set of rules, so players learn different games that interlock on one board.

Video games embraced the **one-versus-many** format in particular. Natural Selection, a 2002 mod, set commanders and marines against aliens with completely different abilities; see [[Game Modding]]. Evolve, in 2015, pitted four hunters against a monster that grew stronger as it fed, and its servers closed in 2018. Dead by Daylight, from 2016, set one killer against four survivors and became one of the genre's longest-running successes, borrowing its mood from [[Survival Horror]] and asking survivors to rely on [[Stealth Mechanics]]. Nintendo used the Wii U's tablet controller for local versions in which the player holding the tablet sees things the others can't; see [[Local Multiplayer]]. Hidden-role games such as Among Us add asymmetry of information without different abilities; see [[Social Deduction Game]].

The appeal is variety and tension. Each side faces a different problem and feels a different kind of pressure, the lone player outnumbered and the team needing coordination; see [[Cooperative Game]]. Playing the other side is almost a new game.

The cost is balance. With symmetric sides, fairness is built in. With asymmetric ones, designers must make different things roughly equal, and nothing short of extensive [[Playtesting]] and win-rate data reveals whether they are. One side may develop a [[Dominant Strategy]] that the other can't answer, and as players improve the balance can shift, since a coordinated team often gains more from skill than a lone opponent does. Matchmaking adds a practical problem. Games need the right ratio of roles, and if far more players want one role than the other, queue times grow for the popular side; see [[Matchmaking Rating]].

A [[Boss Battle]] in a single-player game is a scripted relative of the one-versus-many format, with the computer playing the overwhelming side.
