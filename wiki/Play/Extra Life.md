An **extra life** is an additional attempt granted to a player, usually as a reward for reaching a score or finding a special item. It belongs to the broader convention of **lives**: a game starts with a few, loses one each time the player fails, and ends with "game over" when none remain.

The idea came from arcades and pinball. A pinball game gives a fixed number of balls, traditionally three or five, and skilled play can win more; see [[Pinball]]. Video arcade games adopted the same structure because it suited their business: each coin bought a few lives, so a game lasted minutes for most players and longer for experts, and an operator could estimate earnings per machine; see [[Arcade Game]] and [[Bushnell's Law]]. Space Invaders (1978) and many games after it awarded a bonus life at a score threshold, giving players a reason to chase points.

**Continues** extended the bargain. Inserting another coin before a countdown ended let a player resume where they died, often with the score reset, so a determined or wealthy player could see the whole game. Home consoles had no coins, and many early home games kept lives mainly from habit, while adding password systems or limited continues to make long games finishable. Nintendo's Super Mario Bros. (1985) popularized "1-Up," its green mushroom that grants a life and a name for the reward in general; see [[Platformer]]. Konami's famous button sequence gave Contra players thirty lives; see [[Cheat Code]].

Lives shape how a game feels. A shared pool of attempts makes each mistake matter, raises tension near the end, and turns a hard stretch into a resource problem. They also punish players by sending them back to the start, a cost designers once used to stretch short games; see [[Difficulty Curve]] and [[Loss Aversion]].

As games grew longer and saving became standard, lives often became vestigial. A game over might return the player to the last checkpoint, which the checkpoint alone would do; see [[Checkpoint]]. Many later games removed lives entirely. Prince of Persia (2008) had a companion pull the hero back from every fall, and Super Mario Odyssey (2017) replaced lives with a loss of coins. Others keep failure costly in other forms, such as losing progress or dropped items; see [[Soulslike]].

Some genres made lives central instead. Roguelikes give one life per run, and death ends it, though progress may carry over; see [[Roguelike]] and [[Permadeath]]. Players sometimes bypass limited lives by reloading saves; see [[Save Scumming]].

Accessibility options now let players add lives, slow the game, or skip sections, recognizing that the old arcade economy no longer has to set the terms; see [[Assist Mode]].
