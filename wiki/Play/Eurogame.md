A **Eurogame**, or German-style board game, is a strategy board game emphasizing economic and planning decisions over conflict and luck. The style grew in West Germany in the 1980s and 1990s and reached a worldwide audience with *The Settlers of Catan* (1995), whose success began the modern boom in hobby board games.

Germany had a strong culture of family games, supported by the **Spiel des Jahres**, a game-of-the-year award founded by critics in 1979. Winning it could sell hundreds of thousands of copies, which encouraged games that families could learn in minutes and finish in an hour, yet that rewarded skill. A second award for more complex games was added in 2011.

Typical features:

- **Indirect competition.** Players compete for resources, spaces, and turn order rather than attacking each other. Taking the spot someone needs is the main form of interaction; see [[Worker Placement]].
- **No player elimination.** Everyone plays until the end, so no one sits out for an hour.
- **Low luck.** Randomness is limited or happens before decisions, so players plan around it; see [[Input and Output Randomness]].
- **Victory points.** Many paths lead to points, and the winner is often unclear until the final count.
- **Economic engines.** Players build systems that produce more over time; see [[Engine Building]] and [[Faucets and Sinks]].
- **Designer credit.** Designers' names appear on the box, a German publishing practice that made designers known.

Influential titles include *Carcassonne* (2000), which builds a shared map from tiles; *Puerto Rico* (2002), whose role selection lets each player's choice affect all; *Agricola* (2007), which made worker placement central; and *Dominion* (2008), which invented the deckbuilder; see [[Deckbuilder]]. *Pandemic* (2008) turned similar mechanics toward cooperation; see [[Cooperative Game]].

The contrasting American style, which enthusiasts labeled **Ameritrash**, first as an insult and then with pride, favors dramatic themes, dice, direct conflict, and elimination, as in *Risk* and many science fiction epics. Modern design mixes both freely.

Criticisms of Eurogames are that themes are thin, so a game about trading spices could as easily be about anything; that players often play solitaire side by side; and that tight point races reward calculation over fun, causing long pauses, sometimes called analysis paralysis. Games that let a losing player decide the winner, or let the leader run away, need balancing mechanics; see [[Catch-Up Mechanic]].

The ideal Eurogame offers a series of real choices between valuable options with no single best answer; see [[Interesting Decisions]] and [[Dominant Strategy]]. Online communities that rank, review, and discuss games helped spread the style worldwide from about 2000, and their rankings have long been dominated by heavy Eurogames and hybrids. Crowdfunding later let small publishers reach these players directly.
