An **alternate reality game** (ARG) is a story told through real-world media, such as websites, emails, phone numbers, videos, and physical objects, that players discover, piece together, and sometimes influence. Its defining pose is "this is not a game": the fiction never admits to being fiction, and the line between play and ordinary life is deliberately blurred; see [[Magic Circle]] and [[Fourth Wall]].

The entry point is called the **rabbit hole**, an odd detail that invites a closer look; see [[Rabbit Hole]]. The designers who run the game from behind the scenes are called puppetmasters, and they adjust the story as players respond.

Landmark examples:

- **The Beast (2001).** Built to promote the film *A.I. Artificial Intelligence*, it began with a credit in the trailer for a "sentient machine therapist" named Jeanine Salla. Searching the name led to a web of fictional sites about a murder in 2142. An online group called the Cloudmakers grew to over 7,000 members and solved puzzles faster than the designers had planned.
- **I Love Bees (2004).** A promotion for *Halo 2* that hid its story in a hacked beekeeping website and sent players to ring specific payphones across the US at set times.
- **Year Zero (2007).** Nine Inch Nails left USB drives in concert venue bathrooms that held songs and clues to a dystopian future, released alongside the album.

ARGs are designed for crowds. Puzzles often require skills no single player has, such as translating an obscure language, decoding audio, or traveling to a location, so solving depends on a community dividing the labor and remembering who knows what; see [[Transactive Memory]] and [[Wisdom of the Crowd]]. Players build shared wikis and guides that track every clue; see [[Wiki]] and [[Commons-Based Peer Production]]. Some fans dig into game files and site source for hidden hints, a practice that overlaps with [[Datamining]].

The form draws on older traditions. **Puzzle hunts** such as the MIT Mystery Hunt, held every January since 1981, send teams through hundreds of interlocking puzzles. The storytelling owes much to [[Interactive Fiction]] and to stories told through objects and places rather than narration; see [[Environmental Storytelling]]. Games built around insight rather than mechanics share the same pleasure of understanding; see [[Metroidbrainia]].

The "not a game" pose has costs. Players have mistaken real people and real sites for clues, and some have harassed strangers who turned out to be uninvolved. Fiction that hides its frame can be hard to tell from a hoax, and when an ARG ends, its sites go offline and much of the experience disappears. Commercial ARGs also faded as marketing budgets moved to social media, though fan-made and indie ARGs on video platforms remain active.

The performed reality resembles professional wrestling's practice of never breaking character; see [[Kayfabe]].
