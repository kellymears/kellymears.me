An **indie game** is made without the financial backing of a large publisher, typically by a single person or a small team. The label describes a business arrangement first, but it has come to suggest a sensibility too: personal, experimental, small in scope, and willing to try ideas that big-budget games avoid.

Independent development is as old as the medium. In the early home computer era, individuals wrote and sold games by mail order. The 1990s brought shareware, where the first part of a game was given away free and the rest sold to players who wanted more, a model that funded several studios that later became famous. In the 2000s, free browser games built in Flash gave a generation of developers an audience, and online portals let anyone publish.

Digital distribution made the modern indie scene. Download services on consoles and PCs in the mid-to-late 2000s let small teams reach players without manufacturing discs or negotiating shelf space. A run of breakout games around 2008 to 2012, several of them profiled in a 2012 documentary about their makers, showed that tiny teams could produce critically acclaimed, commercially successful work. A block-building game released in early unfinished form in 2009 by one developer became one of the best-selling games ever made; see [[Early access]] and [[Sandbox Game]].

Indie games have shaped the medium out of proportion to their budgets:

- **Revived genres.** Small teams brought back forms that big studios had abandoned, from exploration platformers to hard dungeon crawls; see [[Metroidvania]] and [[Roguelike]].
- **New genres.** Narrative exploration, puzzle games built on knowledge, and relaxed farming and crafting games grew mainly from independent work; see [[Walking Simulator]], [[Metroidbrainia]], and [[Cozy Game]].
- **Visual styles.** Low-resolution art became an aesthetic choice instead of a technical limit; see [[Pixel Art]].

Tools lowered the barriers further. Free and cheap engines, open marketplaces where anyone can publish, and short creative sprints that seed many projects all help; see [[Godot]] and [[Game Jam]]. Crowdfunding campaigns, which broke records for games in 2012, let developers raise money directly from future players.

Success is unevenly distributed. Tens of thousands of games are released on major PC storefronts each year, and most earn very little while a few earn fortunes; see [[Long tail]]. The visible hits create a misleading impression of the odds; see [[Survivorship Bias]]. Discovery depends heavily on streamers and video creators, whose attention can make a small game's career; see [[Let's Play]].

The line between indie and mainstream has blurred. Some "indie" studios have dozens of staff and publisher funding, while large companies release small games under separate labels. The term persists mostly as shorthand for games that feel authored by particular people.
