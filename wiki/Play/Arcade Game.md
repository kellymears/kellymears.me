An **arcade game** is a coin-operated game in a public place, such as an arcade, bar, or cinema lobby. Arcades were where video games first reached a mass audience, and their economics shaped game design in ways that persist long after most arcades closed. They also made games a social activity, played in front of strangers who waited their turn.

The first commercial video arcade game was *Computer Space* (1971), designed by Nolan Bushnell and Ted Dabney, which proved too complicated for bar patrons. Their next company, Atari, released *Pong* in 1972, a game simple enough to need almost no instructions; see [[Bushnell's Law]]. The golden age followed in the late 1970s and early 1980s with *Space Invaders* (1978), *Asteroids* (1979), *Pac-Man* (1980), and *Donkey Kong* (1981). Some games drew lines on vector screens rather than pixels; see [[Vector Display]].

The business model was a coin per play, and it shaped everything:

- **Short sessions.** A machine earned by turning players over, so games were designed to last a few minutes for beginners while letting experts play much longer.
- **Rising difficulty.** Waves sped up until any player lost, giving no ending but the end of the player's skill; see [[Difficulty Curve]].
- **Instant clarity.** A game had seconds to make sense to a stranger walking past, so rules were visible and controls few; see [[Game Feel]].
- **Attract mode.** Between players, machines ran demos of themselves to lure the next coin.
- **High scores.** Tables of initials turned solitary play into public competition, an early form of [[Esports]]. The *King of Kong* documentary (2007) showed how fierce competition over *Donkey Kong* records still was decades later.
- **Continues.** Later games let players add a coin to keep going, trading money for progress, a forerunner of [[Free-to-Play]] monetization.

Home consoles undercut arcades by the mid-1980s, and the market crash briefly hurt both; see [[Video Game Crash of 1983]]. *Street Fighter II* (1991) revived Western arcades by making them the place to find human opponents, and arcade-born genres kept going: shooters, racers, rhythm games, and fighting games; see [[Shoot 'em Up]], [[Rhythm Game]], and [[Fighting Game]]. Japanese game centers stayed busy longer, with prize cranes, photo booths, and rhythm cabinets.

As home hardware matched arcade boards and online play replaced the need to meet in person, most Western arcades closed in the 2000s. The games themselves survive mainly through emulators that run original arcade code, a major preservation effort since the late 1990s; see [[Emulation]] and [[Game Preservation]].

Arcade design still echoes in modern games: short loops of play and reward, score attack modes, and the tension between fair challenge and extracting money; see [[Compulsion Loop]].
