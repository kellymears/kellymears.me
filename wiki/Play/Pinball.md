**Pinball** is a game in which a player keeps a steel ball in play on a sloped, glass-covered table, using flippers to send it at targets, ramps, and bumpers that score points. It's one of the few arcade games whose core is real physics rather than a simulation, so no two balls ever travel exactly the same path.

Its ancestor was bagatelle, an eighteenth-century table game in which players shot balls into holes surrounded by pins. Coin-operated versions appeared in the United States in the early 1930s and spread quickly during the Depression as cheap entertainment. Early machines had no flippers; players launched the ball and watched it fall, which made them games of chance. Flippers arrived in 1947, on a Gottlieb machine called Humpty Dumpty, and turned pinball into a game of aim and timing.

The reputation for chance lingered. New York City banned pinball in 1942 as a form of gambling, with the mayor smashing confiscated machines for the press, and cities including Chicago and Los Angeles also restricted them; see [[Moral Panic]]. The New York ban lasted until 1976, when a player demonstrated skill before the city council by calling a shot and making it, an event later accounts suggest owed something to luck.

The physical ball gives pinball its character. Players nudge and shake the cabinet to influence the ball, which is legal up to a point. Too much violence trips a tilt sensor, traditionally a pendulum inside the cabinet, and ends the turn. The phrase "on tilt," for a frustrated player making reckless choices, spread from here to card games; see [[Poker]].

Pinball's history tracks the arcade's:

- **Solid-state electronics** in the late 1970s added digital scoring, sound, and complex rules.
- **Video games** competed for arcade space from the late 1970s; see [[Arcade Game]].
- **The 1990s** saw licensed machines with ramps, toys, and modes, and the best-selling modern table, The Addams Family, released in 1992.
- **Decline.** By around 2000, only one major manufacturer remained.
- **Revival.** From the 2010s, new manufacturers, competitive leagues, and bars dedicated to pinball brought it back as a niche hobby.

Its design wisdom is old and widely borrowed: a game easy to start and deep to master; see [[Bushnell's Law]]. Its feedback, the clack of the flippers and the jolt of the bumpers, is a reference point for [[Game Feel]].

Digital pinball recreates the physics in software; see [[Rigid Body Dynamics]] and [[Collision Detection]]. Old machines wear out as rubber, coils, and boards fail, and enthusiasts restore them and preserve their code; see [[Game Preservation]] and [[Emulation]].
