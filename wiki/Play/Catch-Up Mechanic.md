A **catch-up mechanic** is a rule that helps players who are behind close the gap with the leader. It answers a common flaw in game design: an early advantage that keeps growing, so the winner is clear long before the game ends and everyone else plays out a lost cause.

The flaw is called **snowballing** or the **runaway leader** problem. It comes from positive feedback: resources produce more resources, so whoever gets ahead gets ahead faster. Monopoly is the classic case. Once one player owns developed properties, their income drains the others, and the last hour of the game is a slow formality. The same dynamic appears in strategy games where territory produces armies, and in team games where early kills give gold for better equipment; see [[4X Game]] and [[Engine Building]].

Designers counter it in several ways:

- **Direct help.** Trailing players get extra resources or better odds. *Mario Kart* gives players near the back stronger items, most famously the blue shell, which strikes whoever is in first place.
- **Leader penalties.** The player in front pays more, faces more opposition, or loses some efficiency.
- **Diminishing returns.** Each extra unit of an advantage is worth less, so leads level off; see [[Power Creep]].
- **Bounties.** In many team games, defeating a player on a streak earns a bonus, so a dominant player becomes a valuable target.
- **Scoring late.** Saving much of the scoring for the end, or hiding scores, keeps outcomes uncertain; see [[Perfect Information]].
- **Negative feedback in AI.** Racing games slow down leading computer opponents and speed up trailing ones, called rubber banding; see [[Dynamic Difficulty Adjustment]].

Catch-up mechanics have costs. Players who built a lead through skill feel robbed when the game takes it away, and if help for trailing players is strong enough, deliberately falling behind becomes a strategy. Competitive players often see them as rewarding luck over skill, which is why tournament rules sometimes turn random items off. The goal is to keep the result in doubt without making early play pointless.

A related problem in multiplayer games is **kingmaking**: a player who can no longer win decides who does, by choosing whom to attack or help. It makes the outcome feel arbitrary and is hard to design away, since any game with three or more players allows it. Common remedies include hidden scores, ending the game as soon as anyone can no longer win, and scoring systems where losing players still care about their placement.

The tension mirrors a debate outside games, between rewarding success and limiting how much early advantage compounds; see [[Matthew Effect]]. Board games settle it by choice, in rules, which is part of why designers study them as small models of economies; see [[Procedural Rhetoric]].
