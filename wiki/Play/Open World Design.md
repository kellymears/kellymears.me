**Open World Design** is a structural claim about a game's space: it's large, contiguous, and its objectives can be tackled in a non-linear order, as opposed to a level-based game that gates progress behind a fixed sequence. The claim is about geography and sequencing only: an authored quest layer almost always still runs through the space, which is what separates the term from [[Sandbox Game]], where it's the authored goals themselves that are missing. *Elden Ring* makes the boundary vivid — radically non-linear space, fully authored purpose — open world to its bones and nothing like a sandbox.

The genre's known failure mode is density-of-interest collapse — the "Ubisoft tower" critique, where a map fills with repeated icon-types (collect this, clear that camp, climb this tower to reveal more icons) faster than the design team can make any individual one worth doing. A big map is easy to generate; a big map where every screen has a reason to be there is not, and the gap between the two is most of what separates a beloved open world from a tedious one.

Open world design shares [[Fog of War]]'s reveal-the-map instinct at a meta level — tower-climbing to unfog the map is fog of war borrowed wholesale from strategy games and repurposed as a traversal reward. It's a looser, more permissive structure than [[Metroidvania]], which shares the "one contiguous map" property but gates large portions of it behind specific abilities, converting free roam into a controlled backtracking loop; an open world usually lets the player simply walk there, difficulty permitting.
