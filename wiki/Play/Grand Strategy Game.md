**Grand Strategy Game** describes the Paradox-defined lineage — *Europa Universalis*, *Crusader Kings*, *Hearts of Iron*, *Victoria* — in which the player governs a nation or dynasty across decades or centuries, juggling abstracted diplomacy, economy, succession, and war as simultaneous, ongoing systems rather than a sequence of discrete battles to win. There usually is no clean endgame. The game keeps running; the player's job is to keep steering.

The defining mechanical choice is pausable real time rather than turns: the world advances on its own tick, and the player interrupts it to issue orders, which puts the emphasis on *when* to act as much as *what* to do. This is where [[Time as Resource]] becomes literal rather than metaphorical — a Crusader Kings player who fails to act before a character dies has lost an option permanently, the way a chess clock punishes hesitation.

The genre is routinely conflated with [[4X Game]], and the distinction is load-bearing: a 4X game runs a symmetric race — explore, expand, exploit, exterminate — toward a defined victory condition on a map that starts blank and is progressively revealed, usually through [[Fog of War]]. A grand strategy game instead simulates a persistent, asymmetric world that was never blank — nations have history, succession law, and existing borders before the player arrives — and its narrative comes from emergent events (a succession crisis, a rebellion, an assassination) rather than from crossing a finish line. Civilization sits closer to 4X for exactly this reason even though it borrows grand strategy's diplomacy vocabulary; Stellaris sits closer to grand strategy despite its 4X-shaped opening because its midgame narrative machinery (crises, ethics drift, internal politics) keeps generating story after the map is full.

The genre's real risk is scope: a system detailed enough to be a genuine simulation of statecraft is also detailed enough to bury a new player before the interesting decisions surface.
