The **Bartle taxonomy** sorts players by what they enjoy in a game into four types. Richard Bartle, co-creator of MUD1, the first multi-user dungeon, proposed it in a 1996 paper titled "Hearts, Clubs, Diamonds, Spades: Players Who Suit MUDs." It came from a long debate among the game's experienced players about what people wanted from it.

The four types fall on two axes: whether a player prefers *acting on* things or *interacting with* them, and whether their focus is the *world* or other *players*:

- **Achievers** (diamonds) act on the world. They want points, levels, gear, and completion; see [[Achievement Hunting]].
- **Explorers** (spades) interact with the world. They want to find hidden areas, understand how systems work, and discover secrets; see [[Datamining]] and [[Sequence Breaking]].
- **Socializers** (hearts) interact with players. They come for conversation, community, and relationships.
- **Killers** (clubs) act on players. They want to compete with, dominate, or disrupt others.

Bartle's key observation was about balance between the groups. The types depend on each other: killers need victims, often achievers; socializers need a stable community, which too many killers destroy; explorers supply the knowledge achievers use. Changing one population shifts the others, so a designer adjusting a game for one type changes the whole community. He described the dynamics almost like an ecosystem.

The taxonomy became the most cited model of player motivation and spread far beyond online games, into game design teaching, marketing, and the "gamification" of apps and workplaces.

Its limits are well known, including to Bartle:

- **One game, one era.** It was drawn from the players of a text-based multiplayer game, not from games generally, and not from systematic research.
- **People are mixtures.** Most players enjoy several things, and the same person plays differently in different games or moods.
- **Not a personality test.** Online "Bartle tests" turned the four types into labels people assign themselves, which the original paper never intended.
- **Missing motivations.** It says little about story, mastery for its own sake, or creativity.

Later models built on survey data. Nick Yee's research on online players found three broad motivation groups, achievement, social, and immersion, with sub-components. Quantic Foundry's model uses six. Bartle himself later added a second dimension, implicit versus explicit, giving eight types.

The broader lesson holds: players don't all want the same thing, and design choices that delight one group can drive another away. The same logic underlies a [[Persona]] in product design and the idea that motivation depends on which psychological needs an activity meets; see [[Self-Determination Theory]].
