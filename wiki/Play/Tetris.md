**Tetris** is a puzzle game in which shapes made of four squares fall into a well, and the player rotates and slides them to complete horizontal lines, which then vanish. It was written in 1984 by Alexey Pajitnov, a researcher at the Soviet Academy of Sciences' computing center in Moscow, on an Electronika 60 computer that could display only text. The name combines *tetra*, Greek for four, with tennis, Pajitnov's favorite sport.

The seven pieces, called tetrominoes, are every way of joining four squares edge to edge. Pajitnov was inspired by pentomino puzzles, which use five. The game never ends: pieces fall faster as levels rise until the stack reaches the top. There is no winning, only how long and how well you last, a structure shared with many arcade games; see [[Arcade Game]].

Copies spread across Soviet computers and then to the West, where its rights became a Cold War tangle. Western companies sold versions under licenses whose validity the Soviet state agency holding the rights disputed, and Pajitnov, as a state employee, earned nothing. In 1989 the designer Henk Rogers secured handheld rights for Nintendo, and *Tetris* was bundled with the Game Boy, selling about 35 million copies and defining the console. Pajitnov received royalties only after he and Rogers formed The Tetris Company in 1996.

Its design rewards study:

- **Randomness.** Early versions picked pieces at random, so long droughts of the straight piece were possible. Modern versions deal the seven pieces in shuffled sets, guaranteeing each appears regularly; see [[Input and Output Randomness]] and [[Pity Timer]].
- **Forgiveness.** Many versions add a brief lock delay before a landed piece sets, letting players slide it at the last moment; see [[Coyote Time]].
- **Absorption.** Steady speed increases and constant small decisions hold players at the edge of their ability; see [[Flow]].

Researchers have used the game often. A 1992 brain imaging study found that players' brains used less energy as they improved, an early demonstration of practice making the brain more efficient. Players report seeing falling blocks when they close their eyes, the **Tetris effect**, and a 2000 study found that even people with amnesia dreamed of the game after playing, despite not remembering it; see [[Dream]] and [[Memory Consolidation]]. Mathematicians showed in 2002 that playing optimally with known pieces is NP-complete; see [[NP-complete]].

The original Nintendo version became a competitive scene, with a world championship held since 2010. Players developed faster techniques for tapping buttons, including rolling the controller against the fingers, and reached levels the designers never anticipated. In December 2023 a thirteen-year-old player reached a level where the game's code crashed, the first known time a human had done so; see [[Kill screen]] and [[Esports]].
