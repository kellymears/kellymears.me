A **boss battle** is a fight against an enemy far stronger and more elaborate than the ordinary ones, usually placed at the end of a level, a dungeon, or the whole game. The name borrows from gang slang for the one in charge. A **mini-boss** guards the midpoint, and the **final boss** caps the story.

The form appeared early. Computer role-playing games on university mainframes in the mid-1970s placed a powerful dragon at the bottom of their dungeons, and arcade shooters around 1980 ended waves with a giant mothership; see [[Arcade Game]]. By the late 1980s, bosses were a fixture of action games, complete with large health bars, dramatic music, and arenas built for a single fight.

A good boss works as an exam. It tests the skills and tools the preceding level taught, often in combination and under pressure. Adventure games famously make each dungeon's boss vulnerable to the item found in that dungeon, so the fight confirms that the player has learned to use it; see [[Level Design]].

Common design ingredients:

- **Patterns.** Bosses cycle through attacks that can be learned and countered, turning an apparently overwhelming fight into a solvable puzzle.
- **Warnings.** Each attack is signaled by a windup, sound, or animation, giving attentive players time to respond; see [[Telegraphing]].
- **Phases.** The fight changes partway through, as the boss gets angrier, loses armor, or transforms, keeping the encounter from going stale.
- **Openings.** Windows after an attack where the boss can be damaged, rewarding patience over button mashing; see [[Parry and Riposte]].
- **Spectacle.** Scale, music, and staging that make the fight feel like an event; see [[Adaptive Music]].

Some genres center on bosses. Many shooters are remembered for their screen-filling final bosses; see [[Bullet hell]]. A whole family of action games built its identity on punishing fights that take dozens of attempts to learn; see [[Soulslike]]. A few games consist of almost nothing else, and players have built challenges of fighting every boss back to back; see [[Boss Rush]].

Bosses can also go wrong. A sudden spike in difficulty can stall players who were otherwise enjoying the game, and placing a save point far before the fight makes each attempt expensive; see [[Difficulty Curve]] and [[Checkpoint]]. Games that let players solve problems through stealth or conversation sometimes force them into a straight fight at a boss, contradicting everything the game had encouraged; see [[Immersive Sim]] and [[Ludonarrative Dissonance]].

Memorable bosses often break the rules instead of just being stronger: a fight that depends on the console's clock, an enemy who reads the player's save file, or a final confrontation that can be avoided entirely. Because the boss usually comes last, it's what players remember the level by; see [[Peak-End Rule]].
