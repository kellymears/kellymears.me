**Grinding** is repeating the same tasks in a game, such as defeating the same monsters or running the same dungeon, to accumulate experience points, items, or currency. The activity itself isn't the point; the reward is. Players speak of "the grind" as the stretch of repetitive work between them and the next goal.

Grinding is as old as role-playing games with levels. Early computer and Japanese role-playing games often made the next area too hard for characters at the expected level, so players fought weak enemies near town until they were strong enough; see [[Computer Role-Playing Game]]. Online role-playing games made grinding central, since a game meant to be played for years needs goals that take years; see [[MMORPG]]. Players nicknamed one popular early title, EverQuest, EverCrack, because of how many hours its players sank into leveling and camping for rare items.

Designers have reasons to include grind:

- **Pacing.** Repetition stretches content that is expensive to make into many hours of play; see [[Time as Resource]].
- **Flexible difficulty.** Players who struggle can grind to become stronger instead of improving their skill, which works as an informal easy mode; see [[Difficulty Curve]].
- **Rare rewards.** Items with low drop rates keep players repeating in hope of a lucky result; see [[Variable-Ratio Reinforcement]].

Some players genuinely enjoy it, and many games are loved partly because of the grind rather than in spite of it. Repetitive, low-stakes tasks with clear progress can be calming, almost meditative, especially with a podcast running; see [[Flow]]. Idle games turned the grind into the entire game, often as satire; see [[Idle Game]].

Others resent it, and businesses formed on both sides of that resentment. In the 2000s, gold farming operations, many reported in China, paid workers to play online games for long shifts, collecting in-game currency to sell to wealthier players for real money; see [[Real-money trading]]. Developers banned it as damaging to their in-game economies, since farmed currency floods the market and drives up prices for everyone else, and automated grinding by bots became a major target for [[Anti-Cheat]] systems.

Free-to-play games turned the shortcut into a product. A game can make progress slow by design and then sell boosts, so the grind becomes the pressure that drives purchases, and the game's pacing is tuned less for fun than for the moment a tired player reaches for a wallet; see [[Free-to-Play]] and [[Battle Pass]].

The psychology behind grinding has well-studied parts. Once players have invested many hours, quitting feels like wasting them; see [[Sunk Cost Fallacy]]. And as rewards accumulate, each new one satisfies less, so the target keeps moving; see [[Hedonic Treadmill]].
