**A crossword** is a puzzle in which the answers to a set of clues are written into a grid, reading across and down, so that answers share letters where they cross. Each crossing letter is a check: a wrong answer in one direction clashes with an answer in the other.

The journalist Arthur Wynne published the first recognized crossword, a diamond-shaped "word-cross," in the New York World on December 21, 1913. A decade later, in 1924, a new publishing firm called Simon & Schuster launched itself with a book of crosswords, and a craze followed. Newspapers ran stories about people solving on trains and in offices, and some commentators worried about the nation's wasted time; see [[Moral Panic]]. The New York Times called crosswords a passing fad, then started its own in 1942, after the attack on Pearl Harbor, partly as a distraction for readers in wartime.

Two main traditions developed:

- **American style.** Every letter is checked by both an across and a down answer, the grid is symmetric when rotated half a turn, and clues are usually definitions or puns. Puzzles grow harder through the week, and many have a theme linking the longest answers.
- **British cryptic.** Each clue contains a straight definition plus wordplay, such as an anagram, a hidden word, or a pun, that leads to the same answer. Grids have more unchecked letters, since the clues give more to work with.

Cryptic crosswords have a famous footnote. In 1942, the Daily Telegraph ran a timed solving contest, and several fast solvers were reportedly approached afterward about codebreaking work at [[Bletchley Park]].

Solving draws on several kinds of memory and reasoning. A clue can produce the frustrating sense of knowing an answer without being able to retrieve it; see [[Tip of the Tongue]]. Cryptic clues often resolve in a sudden flash once the solver sees the trick; see [[Insight]]. Crossing letters narrow the possibilities for other answers, which narrow others in turn; see [[Constraint Propagation]]. Regular solvers learn short words with friendly letter patterns that constructors use to fill tight corners, a vocabulary called crosswordese.

Constructing a grid is a search problem. Software tries word after word from a list and backs up when a corner can't be filled; see [[Backtracking]]. Computer-assisted construction is now standard, though editors still judge which fills are fresh and fair.

Crosswords depend so heavily on a language's words and spelling that they can't really be translated; each language develops its own conventions; see [[Video Game Localization]]. Many solvers keep a daily solving habit for decades; see [[Habit Formation]].
