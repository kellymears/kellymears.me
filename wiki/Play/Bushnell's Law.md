**Bushnell's law** is the principle that the best games are easy to learn and difficult to master. Nolan Bushnell, co-founder of Atari, put it in arcade terms: a good game should reward the first quarter and the hundredth.

*Pong* (1972) is the model. Its instructions were one line, "Avoid missing ball for high score," and anyone could play within seconds, yet the angles and speed rewarded practice for years. Arcade economics enforced the rule. A game that confused new players earned no first quarter, and one that ran out of depth earned no hundredth.

Designers talk about it as two separate measures:

- **Skill floor**: how much a new player must learn before the game is playable and fun.
- **Skill ceiling**: how far improvement can go before there is nothing left to get better at.

A low floor and a high ceiling is the goal; the two are independent. Go has a handful of rules and a ceiling no human has reached. *Tetris* can be explained in a sentence, and its top players were still discovering new techniques four decades after release. Many complex strategy games have a high floor and a high ceiling, and many casual games have a low floor and a low ceiling. Neither is wrong, but Bushnell's combination reaches the widest audience.

The depth comes from interactions rather than from rules. A few simple pieces that combine in many ways give more to master than many special cases; see [[Emergent Gameplay]]. Depth disappears when one approach beats everything, since mastery collapses into executing it; see [[Dominant Strategy]]. A game whose best play has been fully computed has reached its ceiling; see [[Solved Game]].

Ways games lower the floor without lowering the ceiling:

- **Simple inputs, deep timing.** Fighting games and platformers often have few buttons but reward precise timing measured in single frames; see [[Frame Data]].
- **Gradual introduction.** New mechanics arrive one at a time and are tested before the next; see [[Difficulty Curve]] and [[Skill Check]].
- **Fair opponents.** Matching players of similar skill keeps beginners from facing experts; see [[Matchmaking Rating]].
- **Optional help.** Settings that slow the game or soften punishment let more people in while leaving the full challenge intact; see [[Assist Mode]].

Speedrunners keep pushing the ceiling past anything the designers imagined; see [[Speedrun]].

The idea extends beyond games. The educator Seymour Papert described good learning tools as having a low floor and a high ceiling, and his colleague Mitchel Resnick added wide walls, many different paths to explore. Interface designers pursue the same goal by keeping advanced features available but out of the way; see [[Progressive Disclosure]]. Learning research suggests the climb itself, with challenge just beyond current skill, is where engagement lives; see [[Flow]] and [[Desirable Difficulty]].
