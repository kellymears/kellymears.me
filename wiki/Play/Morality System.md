A **morality system** is a set of mechanics that tracks the ethical character of a player's choices and responds to it. The common form is a meter running from good to evil, or a pair of scores, adjusted by actions such as sparing an enemy or stealing from a shop. The game then changes dialogue, endings, powers, or how characters treat the player; see [[Branching Narrative]] and [[Multiple Endings]].

An early influential example was Ultima IV (1985), whose goal was not to defeat a villain but to become a moral exemplar by living up to eight virtues, tracked by hidden values that the game measured through ordinary actions. Black & White (2001) shaped a creature and a landscape that looked kinder or crueler according to the player's conduct. Star Wars: Knights of the Old Republic (2003) mapped choices onto the light and dark sides of the Force, and Fable (2004) changed the hero's appearance with their reputation. Mass Effect (2007) used separate Paragon and Renegade scores so that one didn't cancel the other; see [[Dialogue Tree]].

The systems have a well-known flaw. When good and evil each unlock rewards at the extremes, players stop asking what is right and start asking which path pays better, picking one side and staying there to max out the meter. The measure meant to reflect character becomes a target to optimize; see [[Goodhart's Law]] and [[Dominant Strategy]]. Choices are often obvious, too, between saint and cartoon villain, leaving no real dilemma.

BioShock (2007) became a famous case. Players could rescue or harvest the Little Sisters, characters who carried a valuable resource, and the reward structure was close enough to balanced that the choice felt more like accounting than ethics. Critics argued that what the game's systems rewarded clashed with what its story claimed to be about; see [[Ludonarrative Dissonance]].

Designers have tried several alternatives. Some hide the meter, so players act without seeing a score. Some, like The Witcher series, give choices delayed and ambiguous consequences, so no option is clearly correct at the time. Undertale (2015) lets players finish without killing anyone, and treats violence as something the game and its characters remember. Games such as Papers, Please and Frostpunk build dilemmas into the rules themselves, forcing trade-offs between survival and decency rather than labeling actions; see [[Procedural Rhetoric]] and [[Trolley Problem]].

Reputation systems are a related approach. Instead of a single moral axis, factions keep their own opinion of the player, so the same act pleases one group and angers another; see [[Non-Player Character]].

The best moral choices tend to be ones where a thoughtful player would hesitate, with costs on each side and no score to settle the matter; see [[Interesting Decisions]].
