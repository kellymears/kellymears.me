In a **cooperative game**, all players are on the same side, and they win or lose together against the game itself. With no human opponent, the system provides the opposition, through decks that escalate threats, timers, or automated enemies.

Reiner Knizia's *Lord of the Rings* board game (2000) popularized the form in modern board games. Matt Leacock's *Pandemic* (2008), in which players race to cure four diseases spreading across a world map, made it mainstream. The game's challenge comes from an event deck that grows more dangerous as play continues; see [[Difficulty Curve]].

The central design problem is the **alpha player**, or **quarterbacking**: if all information is open, the most experienced or confident player can direct everyone else's turns, and the others become spectators. Groups may defer to that player even when they're wrong; see [[Groupthink]]. Designers have several responses:

- **Hidden information.** In *Hanabi* (2010), winner of the Spiel des Jahres in 2013, players hold their cards facing outward, seeing everyone's hand but their own, and may give only limited hints; see [[Perfect Information]].
- **Real-time play.** Games with a running timer leave no time for one person to plan everyone's moves.
- **Limited communication.** Some games ban table talk entirely, forcing players to read each other's actions.
- **A traitor.** One player may secretly work against the group, turning cooperation into suspicion; see [[Social Deduction Game]].
- **Semi-cooperation.** Everyone loses if the group fails, but only one player can win, which builds in a tension between team and self; see [[Prisoner's Dilemma]].

Players who let others do the work, and get credit when the group wins, are a familiar problem; see [[Free Rider Problem]]. Good co-op design gives every role something only it can do, so each player matters, and rewards communication over command; see [[Transactive Memory]] and [[Collective Action]].

Video games approached cooperation differently, often with each player controlling their own character in real time. *Left 4 Dead* (2008) used an "AI Director" that adjusts the number of enemies to the team's state, pressing harder when they are doing well and easing off after a hard fight; see [[Dynamic Difficulty Adjustment]]. *It Takes Two* (2021) was built only for two players, with each level giving the two characters different abilities that must be combined. Single-player games sometimes simulate a partner; see [[Companion AI]] and [[Escort Mission]].

Co-op games are a common choice for mixed groups because no one has to beat a friend, and losses are shared. Groups play more freely when no one fears blame for a mistake; see [[Psychological Safety]].
