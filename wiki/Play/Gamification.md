**Gamification** is the use of game elements in non-game settings: points for completing tasks, badges for milestones, streaks for daily use, and leaderboards that rank people against each other. The term spread around 2010, when startups and consultants promoted it as a way to make work, learning, fitness, and shopping more engaging. Loyalty cards, sales contests, and school gold stars did versions of it long before.

The most common toolkit is sometimes called **points, badges, and leaderboards**. Critics noted early that these are the least interesting parts of games. What makes games compelling is meaningful choice, clear feedback, challenges matched to skill, and the freedom to fail safely; see [[Flow]] and [[MDA Framework]]. Points attached to an otherwise unchanged task leave the task as it was.

Where it works, gamification usually does a few things well:

- **Visible progress.** Progress bars and levels make slow improvement noticeable, which helps with long-term goals.
- **Streaks.** Counting consecutive days of activity uses the reluctance to break a chain; language-learning apps rely heavily on it, sometimes to the point of anxiety; see [[Habit Formation]].
- **Social comparison.** Leaderboards motivate people near the top and can discourage everyone else.
- **Variable rewards.** Unpredictable bonuses keep people checking back; see [[Variable-Ratio Reinforcement]] and [[Compulsion Loop]].

The deeper concern comes from research on motivation. When people are rewarded for an activity they already enjoy, their interest can drop once the rewards stop, because the reason for doing it shifts from enjoyment to reward; see [[Overjustification effect]]. Points can replace curiosity and mastery with a score to chase.

Measured targets distort behavior too. When a system rewards a number, people optimize the number: logging minimal activity to keep a streak, taking easy courses for badges, or gaming whatever counts; see [[Goodhart's Law]]. In workplaces, leaderboards for call volume or tickets closed can push speed over quality.

Gamification also blurs into manipulation. Many apps use game mechanics mainly to maximize time spent and purchases, through daily rewards, artificial scarcity, and loss aversion; see [[Dark Pattern]] and [[Gacha Monetization]]. The systems teach what matters by what they reward, which is not always what the user wanted; see [[Procedural Rhetoric]].

Some designers prefer making activities genuinely game-like: giving people real choices, interesting problems, and room to play, rather than adding scores on top. Citizen science projects such as Foldit, where players solve protein-folding puzzles, are often cited as successes because the play is the work. Others argue that a little structure, used honestly and optionally, can help people stick with things they already want to do; see [[Achievement Hunting]] and [[Bartle Taxonomy]].
