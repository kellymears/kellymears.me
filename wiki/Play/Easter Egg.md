**An Easter egg** is a hidden message, joke, or feature that its creators put into a game, program, or film for people to discover. Like eggs hidden for an Easter hunt, it's meant to be found, but only by people who look. Films have them too, from props referencing earlier films to messages hidden in the background of a single frame.

The best-known origin is Adventure, a 1980 game for the Atari 2600. Atari didn't credit its designers, partly so competitors couldn't recruit them. The game's designer, Warren Robinett, hid a room containing the words "Created by Warren Robinett," reachable only by finding an invisible single-pixel object and carrying it to a particular wall. A player discovered it and wrote to Atari, and the company decided to treat it as a bonus and encourage more, reportedly calling it an Easter egg. Hidden credits and messages in software existed earlier, but this one gave the practice its name.

Easter eggs come in several kinds:

- **Hidden credits** for the people who made something.
- **Secret content**, such as rooms, levels, or modes reachable by unusual actions; see [[Sequence Breaking]].
- **Cheat codes.** The most famous, the Konami Code, a sequence of up, up, down, down, left, right, left, right, B, A, began in 1985 as a developer's way to make testing easier and spread across dozens of games.
- **References and in-jokes** to other works, which reward attention to the [[Fourth Wall]].
- **Whole programs**, such as hidden games inside productivity software in the 1990s.

Finding them became its own pastime. Players share discoveries online, and dataminers dig through game files for unused or hidden content; see [[Datamining]]. Some designers hide secrets deliberately to reward exploration, and whole games are built around finding hidden knowledge; see [[Metroidbrainia]] and [[Environmental Storytelling]]. Elaborate hidden puzzles can grow into community-wide hunts; see [[Alternate Reality Game]].

Hidden code has a downside. An Easter egg is software that no one asked for, that often skips review, and that could carry bugs or security flaws; see [[Code Review]] and [[Attack surface]]. Around the early 2000s, Microsoft, which had shipped well-known eggs in its office software, adopted policies against undocumented features as part of a push on security and reliability. Many large companies followed suit, though playful hidden features, like a game in a browser's offline page, persist where they can be reviewed like any other feature.

Once found, an egg's status is fragile. Spreading the secret spoils the discovery for others; see [[Spoiler Etiquette]]. And once people know about a hidden behavior, they come to depend on it; see [[Hyrum's Law]].
