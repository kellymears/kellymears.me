**Experience points**, usually shortened to XP, measure a character's accumulated achievement in a game. Defeating enemies, finishing quests, or solving problems earns points, and reaching set thresholds raises the character's **level**, bringing more health, stronger abilities, or new options. The moment of crossing a threshold, the level up, is often marked with a fanfare.

The system comes from Dungeons & Dragons, published in 1974; see [[Dungeons & Dragons]]. In its first edition, characters earned experience mostly for treasure, one point per gold piece brought back, with smaller amounts for defeating monsters. That rule shaped play around careful looting more than fighting. Thresholds roughly doubled from level to level, so each new level took about as long as all the previous ones combined.

Early computer role-playing games adopted the system almost immediately, since a computer could do the bookkeeping; see [[Computer Role-Playing Game]]. Japanese role-playing games of the 1980s built whole structures around it, including the practice of fighting weak enemies repeatedly to gain levels before moving on; see [[Grinding]].

Experience systems do several things for a game:

- **Pacing.** Required experience gates access to harder areas, smoothing the rise in challenge; see [[Difficulty Curve]].
- **A safety valve.** A player stuck on a hard fight can get stronger by playing more, trading time for skill.
- **Visible progress.** A bar that fills steadily gives a sense of movement even when the story stalls; see [[Progress bar]].
- **Choice.** Levels often come with points to spend, letting players shape their character; see [[Skill Tree]].

The system spread far beyond role-playing games. Competitive shooters in the late 2000s added levels and unlocks to multiplayer modes, and players kept returning to earn the next one. Seasonal reward tracks and long-term unlocks across many play sessions use the same structure; see [[Battle Pass]] and [[Meta-Progression]]. Language-learning apps, fitness trackers, and workplace software borrowed XP to encourage habits; see [[Gamification]].

Critics argue that experience can become a substitute for meaningful play. When the numbers rise whether or not the player learns anything, progress becomes a treadmill that keeps people engaged without satisfying them; see [[Compulsion Loop]]. Psychological research on motivation suggests rewards work best when they reflect genuine growth in competence instead of time spent; see [[Self-Determination Theory]].

Designers also face a balancing problem. If enemies stay the same while the player grows, the game becomes trivial. If enemies grow alongside the player, leveling feels pointless. Some games scale enemies to the player's level, which can mean that bandits in rags appear in elite armor; see [[Dynamic Difficulty Adjustment]]. Ever-rising numbers also invite inflation, where each update needs bigger figures than the last; see [[Power Creep]].
