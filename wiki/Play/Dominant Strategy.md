A **dominant strategy** is one that does at least as well as every alternative, whatever the opponent does. In [[Game theory]] the term describes a precise property, and a rational player should always play such a strategy when one exists. In game design the term usually names a flaw: if one approach always wins, the game collapses into repeating it, and every other option becomes a trap for players who don't know better.

Players call such strategies **degenerate** or, in fighting and action games, **cheese**: a tactic that wins reliably while skipping the play the designers intended, such as an attack that can't be countered or an enemy that can be attacked from a spot where it can't respond. Communities find them fast. Thousands of players experimenting in parallel will locate any dominant option within days of a release, and the competitive scene reorganizes around it; see [[Metagame]] and [[Tier List]].

The standard cure is **intransitivity**: making options beat each other in a cycle, so nothing is best against everything. Rock-paper-scissors is the purest example: each throw beats one and loses to another, so the only sound play is to mix them unpredictably. Strategy games build the same structure from units, as in the classic cavalry beats archers, archers beat infantry, infantry with spears beats cavalry. Fighting games balance attacks, blocks, and throws in a similar triangle; see [[Fighting Game]].

Other tools designers use:

- **Costs and trade-offs.** A powerful option costs more, takes longer, or leaves the player exposed afterward.
- **Situational value.** Options strong in one map, matchup, or phase of the game and weak in others; see [[Level Design]].
- **Counterplay.** Every strong tactic has a visible tell and a response, so it rewards reading the opponent; see [[Telegraphing]].
- **Patches.** In online games, designers watch play data and weaken overperforming options, the source of the gamer's term "nerf"; see [[Live Service Game]].

Dominance isn't always bad. A simple dominant line in a puzzle is its solution. Some single-player games allow overpowered builds as a reward for experimentation, and speedrunners prize degenerate tricks; see [[Sequence Breaking]]. The concern is mainly in competitive play, where a dominant strategy shrinks the space of interesting choices, and in games meant to be replayed, where it makes repeat play monotonous.

The concept also helps explain why players optimize the fun out of games. Given a reliable but boring way to win, many players will use it and then blame the game for being dull. Designers often take this as their responsibility, aligning the most effective strategy with the most enjoyable one, since players can't be expected to avoid it; see [[Goodhart's Law]] and [[MDA Framework]].
