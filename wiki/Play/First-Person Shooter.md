A **first-person shooter** is a game played from the viewpoint of the character, who is usually holding a weapon at the bottom of the screen. Moving, aiming, and shooting happen in real time, and the player sees the world as the character would. It is one of the most commercially important genres in games.

Precursors appeared in the 1970s, including Maze War, a networked maze game where players shot at one another's eyeball avatars. The genre took shape with id Software's Wolfenstein 3D (1992) and Doom (1993). Wolfenstein drew its corridors by casting a ray for each column of the screen, a technique fast enough for ordinary PCs; see [[Ray Casting]]. Doom added varying heights, lighting, and complex levels organized for speed with a binary space partition; see [[Spatial Partitioning]]. Released as shareware with a free first episode, Doom spread widely, and its open level format invited an enormous modding community. Its four-player network mode popularized **deathmatch**; see [[Game Modding]].

Quake (1996) moved to fully 3D worlds and made mouse aiming standard, and its internet play drove new networking techniques; see [[Lag Compensation]]. Half-Life (1998) told its story through scripted events without taking away control, avoiding conventional cutscenes; see [[Cutscene]] and [[Environmental Storytelling]]. Counter-Strike, a fan modification of Half-Life, became one of the most enduring competitive games; see [[Esports]].

Consoles needed different controls. GoldenEye 007 (1997) showed the genre could work on a gamepad, and Halo (2001) refined twin-stick aiming, regenerating shields, and a two-weapon limit that forced choices. Controllers usually include aim assistance, which gently slows or nudges the crosshair near targets to offset the lack of a mouse. Call of Duty 4 (2007) made persistent unlocks and ranks central to online play, so every match fed a longer progression.

Design priorities shifted over time. Early shooters favored labyrinthine mazes, colored keys, secret areas, and very fast movement. Later ones added cover, squad mechanics, and set-piece levels; see [[Level Design]]. Some games fused shooting with role-playing and simulation, letting players solve problems several ways; see [[Immersive Sim]]. The battle royale, born from mods, became a dominant online form in the late 2010s; see [[Battle Royale]].

The genre places heavy demands on hardware and connections. Players notice small delays between input and response, and high refresh rates matter; see [[Input Latency]]. Precise hit detection depends on carefully defined collision shapes around each character's head, body, and limbs. Competitive shooters also attract cheats such as automatic aiming, making detection an ongoing contest; see [[Anti-Cheat]].

Shooters have also been central to public arguments about violence in media, which recur with each new generation of games; see [[Moral Panic]].
