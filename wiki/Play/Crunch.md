**Crunch** is the game industry's term for extended periods of heavy overtime, often weeks or months of sixty- to eighty-hour weeks, before a project's deadline. It's sometimes mandatory and sometimes framed as voluntary while being expected. Software development more broadly has a similar term, the death march, for a project whose schedule can only be met through unsustainable effort.

Public attention began in 2004, when an anonymous blog post signed "EA Spouse" described the hours demanded at Electronic Arts, where the writer's partner worked. The post spread widely, and class-action suits over unpaid overtime followed and were settled. Since then, reporting has documented crunch at many large studios, including on games celebrated for their polish. In 2020 CD Projekt Red required six-day weeks before Cyberpunk 2077's release, despite earlier saying it wouldn't, and the game launched with serious technical problems anyway.

The causes are structural. Release dates are set early for marketing, retail, and holiday seasons, and estimates for creative and technical work are notoriously optimistic; see [[Planning Fallacy]] and [[Hofstadter's Law]]. Features keep being added as the game takes shape, and the last stretch of polish takes far longer than expected; see [[Feature Creep]] and [[Ninety-Ninety Rule]]. Adding staff late rarely helps, since newcomers need time to learn the project; see [[Brooks's Law]]. Many developers love their work, and studios have relied on that passion, along with a steady supply of young applicants, to ask for sacrifice.

Evidence suggests crunch often backfires. Productivity per hour falls with sustained long hours, error rates rise, and the bugs created late in a project must be fixed by the same tired people; see [[Sleep Deprivation]] and [[Technical Debt]]. Industry surveys by the International Game Developers Association have repeatedly found crunch common and linked it to people leaving the industry, which drains experience and makes the next project's schedule worse; see [[Burnout]].

Pushback has grown. Game Workers Unite formed in 2018 to promote organizing, and since the early 2020s several groups of developers and testers at large studios have won union recognition, with quality assurance staff, often the lowest paid and most exposed to crunch, among the first; see [[Playtesting]]. Some studios have publicly committed to avoiding crunch, with delays as the alternative, and players have grown more accepting of delayed games.

Live games keep the pressure going, since updates arrive on fixed schedules with no clear finish line; see [[Live Service Game]]. Small independent teams face their own version, with founders working unpaid hours to finish before money runs out; see [[Indie Game]].

Crunch isn't the same as a short, chosen burst of effort. Game jams compress development into a weekend by design, with a scope matched to the time; see [[Game Jam]].
