A **point-and-click adventure** is a story-driven game played by clicking on a scene: walking to a spot, examining objects, talking to characters, picking things up, and using items on each other to solve puzzles. It grew out of text adventures, which used typed commands, when graphics and the mouse made it possible to show the world and point at it directly; see [[Interactive Fiction]].

Sierra On-Line's *King's Quest* (1984) put an animated character in illustrated scenes, though players still typed commands. LucasArts' *Maniac Mansion* (1987) replaced typing with a list of verbs on screen, such as "open," "pick up," and "use," combined with clicking on objects; its engine, SCUMM, powered *The Secret of Monkey Island* (1990), *Day of the Tentacle* (1993), and *Grim Fandango* (1998). Later games condensed the verbs into a single smart cursor. The genre peaked in the early-to-mid 1990s, when CD-ROMs allowed voice acting and detailed [[Pixel Art]].

The two big studios had different philosophies. Sierra games often let players die suddenly or reach unwinnable states without knowing it, such as forgetting an item hours earlier, which made saving constantly essential; see [[Save Scumming]]. LucasArts designer Ron Gilbert argued against this in a 1989 essay, and LucasArts games mostly avoided death and dead ends, which became the genre's standard.

The genre is also known for **moon logic**: puzzles whose solutions make sense only to their designers. The notorious example is from *Gabriel Knight 3* (1999), where the player builds a fake mustache from cat hair and tape. Designers knowingly or not rely on the player thinking like them, and players get stuck trying each item on everything; see [[Functional Fixedness]] and [[Curse of Knowledge]]. Hint lines charging by the minute and printed strategy guides once made good money from this.

Adventure games declined commercially in the late 1990s as 3D action games rose, though the form never disappeared. Telltale's episodic games of the 2010s shifted focus from puzzles to choices and consequences; see [[Branching Narrative]] and [[Visual Novel]]. Independent developers revived classic styles, and [[Disco Elysium]] carried the genre's dialogue-heavy approach into a role-playing game.

The genre's influence shows in games built around understanding rather than skill. Puzzles that hinge on noticing details in the environment survive in exploration games; see [[Environmental Storytelling]] and [[Metroidbrainia]]. Good modern puzzle design gives players enough clues to reason their way to an answer rather than trying everything; see [[Information Scent]].

Many classics have been preserved through ScummVM, an interpreter that reimplemented the old engines so the games run on modern systems; see [[Emulation]] and [[Game Preservation]].
