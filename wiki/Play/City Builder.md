**City Builder** is a genre in which the player places infrastructure and zones land, and the game's central labor is keeping several interdependent systems — traffic, power, water, tax revenue, land value, pollution — within tolerance at once. There is usually no scoreboard opponent. The city itself is the adversary, in the sense that it will find the load-bearing mistake in your grid faster than you will.

*SimCity* set the template with its demand-and-zone model: residential, commercial, and industrial zones each track their own demand curve, and the player's job is to read those curves and correct imbalance before it compounds. *Cities: Skylines* pushed the simulation further into traffic specifically, modeling individual vehicle pathing rather than an aggregate flow number, which turned "my city is failing" into a legible, diagnosable traffic-engineering problem rather than a vague malaise.

The genre is easy to confuse with [[Colony Sim]], and the distinction is worth holding onto: a colony sim tracks named individuals with needs and moods, while a city builder abstracts its population into statistics — average happiness, not Greg's happiness. That abstraction is a design choice, not a limitation; it's what lets a city builder scale to tens of thousands of residents without the interface collapsing.

The genre's real tension is between simulation depth and legibility. A model detailed enough to be *true* is often too opaque to be *readable* — a city can fail for reasons buried three systems deep, and a player who can't trace the cause learns nothing from the failure, the same legibility problem that [[Soulslike]] design solves for combat. The best city builders expose enough of their internal graph (overlays, per-building tooltips, historical charts) that a bad decision remains diagnosable years after it was made.
