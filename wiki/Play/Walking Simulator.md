A **walking simulator** is a game in which the player mostly explores an environment and uncovers a story, with little or no combat, puzzle-solving, or failure. The term started as an insult in online forums, implying that such games were just walking, and that walking was all a player could do. Developers and players gradually adopted it, and the genre is now also called narrative exploration.

The genre's founding work is *Dear Esther*, made in 2008 as a modification of *Half-Life 2* by The Chinese Room, a team led by the researcher Dan Pinchbeck at the University of Portsmouth, as part of research into game storytelling; see [[Game Modding]]. The player walks across a Hebridean island while a narrator reads fragments of letters. It was remade as a commercial game in 2012. *Gone Home* (2013) followed, in which the player explores an empty family house in 1995 and learns what happened through notes, objects, and voice-over. Later examples include *Firewatch* (2016) and *What Remains of Edith Finch* (2017).

Design techniques:

- **Environmental storytelling.** Rooms, objects, and their arrangement tell the story; a stack of unopened mail says time has passed; see [[Environmental Storytelling]].
- **Found documents and voice-over** reveal events in fragments, letting players assemble them out of order, sometimes from an unreliable source; see [[Unreliable Narrator]].
- **Pacing through space.** The layout controls the order of discoveries, so level design does the work of editing; see [[Level Design]].
- **Sound and music** carry mood that combat would otherwise provide; see [[Adaptive Music]].
- **Diegetic presentation.** Little interface sits between player and world; see [[Diegetic Interface]].

Removing combat removes a familiar source of tension between story and play, where a character's violence clashes with the story's tone; see [[Ludonarrative Dissonance]]. The genre borrows from older forms: text adventures that told stories through rooms, point-and-click adventures with their exploration, and audio logs in immersive sims; see [[Interactive Fiction]], [[Point-and-Click Adventure]], and [[Immersive Sim]].

Debate over whether walking simulators count as games has been heated. Critics argued that without challenge or failure there is nothing to play; defenders replied that choosing where to look and what to read is play, and that games need not be contests. The argument says more about the boundaries of the medium than about any single game; see [[Magic Circle]].

Because they need no combat systems or large worlds, walking simulators suited small independent studios, and several were made by teams of fewer than ten people. The genre has influenced larger games, which now include quiet stretches of exploration, and it overlaps with other low-pressure forms; see [[Cozy Game]] and [[Visual Novel]].
