**Assist mode** is a set of gameplay toggles — extra [[Invincibility Frames]], slower enemies, unlimited resources, optional [[Permadeath]] removal, camera and input adjustments — offered individually rather than bundled into a single "easy, normal, hard" choice. The premise is that difficulty is not one dial: a player with limited hand mobility and a player who wants only the story may need entirely different accommodations, and a slider serves neither well. Toggles let each player assemble the exact combination that removes their specific barrier and nothing else.

**Some studios attach a cost to using them.** A handful of games permanently flag a save that enables an assist toggle, disabling that save's [[Achievement Hunting|achievements]] for the rest of the playthrough — defended as protecting the meaning of a completion claim, criticized as re-imposing exactly the shame the toggle was built to remove. Other studios reject the distinction: an assist mode with an asterisk is not really optional, since the asterisk is what a player with a genuine need is asked to accept in exchange for using it.

The stronger argument runs the other way: a toggle nobody is shamed for using is the entire point. [[Accessibility]] work elsewhere in software — [[Reduced Motion]] settings, [[Color Contrast]] controls, [[Keyboard Navigation]] as an alternative to a mouse, [[Subtitling]] as an alternative to sound — is not treated as a lesser way to use the product, and the case for assist modes is that games should hold the same line. The argument is that framing the toggle as an accommodation rather than a concession raises uptake — that players use it once a game stops asking them to justify needing it. Individual studios have published usage figures for their own games, but no study measures uptake as a function of framing, so the claim stands on reasoning rather than evidence. The achievement penalty, meanwhile, has drawn sustained criticism as a holdover from a harder era of design.
