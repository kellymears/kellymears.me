A **game jam** is an event in which people make a complete game in a short, fixed time, usually around a theme announced at the start. Most last 48 or 72 hours, some a month. Jams are run online and in person, and they have become a training ground for game developers and a source of unusual ideas.

The first event called a game jam was the 2002 Indie Game Jam, where developers experimented with a shared engine. Ludum Dare, started in 2002, became the best known online jam, with a solo 48-hour competition and a team "jam" variant, and tens of thousands of entries over its history. The Global Game Jam, started in 2009, runs every January at hundreds of physical sites across the world on the same weekend.

The format has a few defining features:

- **A fixed deadline.** The time limit forces decisions. Features that would take weeks are impossible, so the game has to be small; see [[Parkinson's Law]].
- **A theme.** Announced at the start, so nobody arrives with a finished game, and interpreted loosely, which leads to surprising results.
- **Finishing.** A jam game must be playable, with a beginning and an end, even if rough. Getting something finished is the main lesson.
- **Community feedback.** Participants play and rate each other's games afterward.

The central lesson is scope. Nearly every first-time jammer plans too much, so experienced participants recommend picking one core mechanic, getting a playable version within hours, and spending the rest polishing and testing it; see [[Minimum Viable Product]] and [[Feature Creep]]. The last hours are always consumed by bugs and missing pieces that nobody planned for; see [[Ninety-Ninety Rule]].

Jams have produced well-known games. *Superhot* began as a 2013 jam prototype in which time moves only when the player does. *Celeste* started as a short game made in four days for a retro console. *Baba Is You*, a puzzle game where the rules are blocks the player pushes around, began at a jam in 2017; see [[Metroidbrainia]]. Many commercial games trace back to a jam idea that worked better than expected.

Constraints help. Tools like small "fantasy consoles," which imitate old hardware with tight limits on resolution, colors, and code size, are popular for jams because they cap ambition; see [[Pixel Art]]. Some jams add their own limits, such as one button or only one color, an approach shared with demo competitions; see [[Demoscene]].

Crunch is a concern: a weekend without sleep is part of jam culture, and organizers increasingly encourage breaks and healthy pacing. The software world has a similar format in the hackathon, though jam games are made for play rather than as product pitches. Teams form among strangers, which gives jams a social role too.
