**Sim racing** names the end of the racing genre that models the car rather than the fantasy of driving it: tire slip and grip curves, suspension travel and weight transfer, laser-scanned circuits accurate to the centimeter. Circuit scanning is also a rejection of [[Procedural Generation]] — a corner only teaches technique if it is the same corner every time, which is why studios keep re-scanning real asphalt instead. Every racing game sits somewhere on the spectrum this defines; *Assetto Corsa* sits near the simulation end, exposing raw setup data and tire wear, while the *Forza Horizon* line sits near the other, trading modeled grip for spectacle and forgiving [[Assist Mode]] settings a newcomer can lean on immediately.

**Force feedback is the genre's primary information channel**, not an add-on. A rumble motor reports that something happened; a direct-drive wheel reports how much grip is left before a tire lets go, the actual skill sim racing tests, which depends on [[Rolling Resistance]] and slip-angle modeling being accurate rather than plausible. Many sims report speed, gear and tire temperature only through the cockpit's [[Diegetic Interface]], the way a real dashboard works, instead of a screen overlay. That is why the hobby is hardware-dependent: a wheel, load-cell pedals and a rigid seat mount teach far more than a graphics setting does, and the genre's [[Game Modding]] scene spends much of its effort on force-feedback and physics data rather than cosmetics.

The spectrum is also a choice about who the [[Metagame]] serves. Setup tuning — camber, gear ratios, brake bias — rewards a driver willing to reproduce a lap with the [[Determinism]] a fair leaderboard requires, the same discipline a [[Speedrun]] rewards elsewhere. Arcade racers keep that layer hidden so the fantasy of driving stays immediate.
