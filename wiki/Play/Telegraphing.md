**Telegraphing** is signaling an attack or event before it happens so players can see it coming and react. An enemy raises its sword before swinging, a boss glows before a big attack, a red circle appears on the ground where something will land. The warning, or **tell**, turns what would be random damage into a test of observation and timing.

The idea comes straight from animation. Disney's animators called it **anticipation**: a character crouches before jumping, or pulls an arm back before throwing, so the audience understands the action; see [[Twelve Basic Principles of Animation]]. Games inherited it for a practical reason. Human reaction time is around a quarter of a second, and inputs take a few frames more to register; see [[Input Latency]]. An attack that lands faster than that can't be dodged by watching, only memorized. A tell longer than reaction time makes it fair.

Telegraphs come in several forms:

- **Animation wind-ups.** A visible pose before the attack, often exaggerated so it reads at a distance or in a crowded fight.
- **Audio cues.** A distinct sound, such as a roar or a charging hum, that works even when the attacker is off-screen; see [[Sound]].
- **Visual effects.** A flash, glow, or color change, often red or orange, to mark danger; see [[Signifier]].
- **Ground markers.** Shapes on the floor showing where an area attack will land, common in online role-playing games.

The length of a telegraph sets difficulty. Long wind-ups suit beginners, while short ones demand faster reactions. [[Soulslike]] games are known for delayed attacks, where an enemy holds the wind-up longer than expected so players who dodge on instinct get hit. Players learn each enemy's rhythm, and fights become a kind of dance; see [[Parry and Riposte]] and [[Invincibility Frames]].

Fighting games rely on telegraphing differently. Fast moves have almost no visible tell, so players react to habits and positioning instead, guessing what the opponent will do; see [[Fighting Game]] and [[Frame Data]].

Telegraphing applies beyond combat. A cracked wall hints at a secret, a creaking floor warns of a collapse, and a distinctive enemy appears in a safe spot before it shows up in a dangerous one; see [[Level Design]]. Narrative uses the same idea when it sets up a payoff in advance; see [[Chekhov's Gun]].

Poor telegraphing is a common reason players call a game unfair. When damage comes from somewhere they couldn't see, or the hit area is larger than the animation suggests, players blame the game rather than themselves; see [[Hitbox and Hurtbox]]. Good telegraphs make players feel that every failure was their own and could be avoided next time, which keeps them trying; see [[Difficulty Curve]].
