**Achievement hunting** is the pursuit of a game's second objective list — the achievements or trophies a storefront tracks alongside it — rather than the goals the designers put in front of the player first. The list is written by the developers, but inside constraints the platform sets: a fixed Gamerscore budget per title on Xbox, rules on PlayStation about how many trophies of each tier a game may award. The result sits one layer up from play and survives independent of whether the underlying goal was meant to matter, which is why a game can ship an achievement for an action its designers privately consider a joke.

**Completion percentage becomes a public artifact** the moment a platform profile displays it. That visibility is the mechanism, not a side effect: a percentage attached to an identity performs the way a private save never could. The split between in-game achievements, tracked by the game's own save, and account-level achievements, tracked by the platform regardless of which save produced them, matters here — only the second kind survives a lost save, which is why hunters treat platform-level completion as the only kind that counts, and why some studios disable it the moment an [[Assist Mode]] toggle is enabled.

Whether this extends play or distorts it is a live argument, and a clean instance of [[Goodhart's Law]]: once "unlock every achievement" becomes the target, players route around difficulty, skip content the list does not reward, and treat [[Datamining]] the list as a legitimate strategy — the list also routinely breaks [[Spoiler Etiquette]], since titles for late-game achievements are often visible before a player reaches them. The counter-case is that an authored [[Metagame]] gives long-tail replay value to games otherwise finished once, feeding the same reward structure as [[Variable-Ratio Reinforcement]] and turning completion into its own [[Compulsion Loop]], one a full-completion [[Speedrun]] category chases under a race clock instead.
