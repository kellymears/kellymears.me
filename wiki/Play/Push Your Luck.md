**Push your luck** is a game mechanic built around a repeated choice: risk what you've gathered this turn for a chance at more, or stop and keep it. Each success makes continuing more tempting and failing more painful. A single bad result usually wipes out everything gained since the last time the player stopped.

The simplest example is Pig, a folk dice game. On your turn you roll a die as often as you like, adding up the results, but a 1 erases the turn's total and ends it. Every roll is the same decision. Countless games share the structure. Blackjack asks whether to take another card. Can't Stop, a 1980 board game by Sid Sackson, lets players climb columns on a board until a roll leaves no legal move. Incan Gold sends explorers deeper into a temple until they turn back or a second copy of the same hazard sends everyone still inside fleeing empty-handed.

The math is a matter of expected value. Continuing makes sense while the expected gain from one more attempt exceeds the expected loss; see [[Expected Value]]. In Pig, with 20 points at stake, a roll has a one-in-six chance of losing them all and a five-in-six chance of adding 4 on average. The two balance at exactly 20, and stopping there maximizes the points scored per turn. Winning is a different goal, though. A player far behind should push harder, since a safe turn rarely closes the gap, which turns the mechanic into a natural comeback system; see [[Catch-Up Mechanic]].

The appeal is psychological as much as mathematical:

- **Losses loom larger.** Losing a pile of points hurts more than gaining the same pile pleases, which makes players stop early; see [[Loss Aversion]].
- **Streaks feel meaningful.** Players believe a 1 is "due" after a long run without one, or that a hot streak will continue, though the die has no memory; see [[Gambler's Fallacy]].
- **Investment pulls forward.** Having already risked a lot makes it hard to walk away; see [[Sunk Cost Fallacy]].
- **Rolling feels like skill.** Choosing when to roll gives a sense of control over pure chance; see [[Illusion of control]].

For designers, push your luck places the randomness before the decision instead of after it, so players react to outcomes instead of being judged by them; see [[Input and Output Randomness]]. Each choice is meaningful because both options are reasonable; see [[Interesting Decisions]].

The pattern runs well beyond tabletop games. Roguelikes ask whether to explore one more room before descending, risking a run's progress for loot; see [[Roguelike]]. Poker asks whether to call another bet; see [[Poker]]. And the uneven, unpredictable rewards of continuing are close kin to what makes slot machines compelling; see [[Variable-Ratio Reinforcement]].
