**A legacy game** is a board game that changes permanently from one session to the next. Players put stickers on the board, write names on cards, tear up components, and open sealed boxes and envelopes when the game tells them to. Each copy becomes unique to the group that played it, a record of their choices.

The format was invented by the designer Rob Daviau with Risk Legacy, published in 2011. It took the classic war game and made every outcome stick: a player who conquered a city could name it, rules changed after certain events, and the winner of each game signed the board. Hidden packets, labeled with conditions like "open when a player is eliminated," introduced new rules mid-campaign.

The breakthrough came with Pandemic Legacy: Season 1 in 2015, designed by Daviau with Matt Leacock, creator of the cooperative game Pandemic. Played over twelve to twenty-four sessions representing the months of a year, it combined the original's teamwork against spreading diseases with an unfolding story. It topped the hobby's largest online ranking site for years; see [[Cooperative Game]].

Permanence changes how decisions feel:

- **Choices carry weight.** A lost city or a scarred character stays lost, so players deliberate harder; see [[Permadeath]] and [[Reversible Decision]].
- **Loss stings more.** Destroying a component, even a planned one, feels like a real loss; see [[Loss Aversion]].
- **Curiosity pulls players back.** Unopened boxes sitting in the game promise unknown content; see [[Zeigarnik Effect]].
- **The board becomes a record.** Stickers, names, and scribbled notes turn the game into a history of the group's campaign.

Legacy designs often protect losers. Risk Legacy gives players who lost advantages in the next game, so a campaign doesn't spiral toward one runaway winner; see [[Catch-Up Mechanic]]. The story often branches depending on results, and campaigns reveal plot through new rules rather than through text; see [[Branching Narrative]] and [[Environmental Storytelling]]. Progress that carries between sessions resembles the unlocks of digital games; see [[Meta-Progression]].

The format has costs. A group has to meet regularly for weeks, and losing a player midway is hard to fix. Once finished, a copy usually can't be played again, which some see as wasteful. Some publishers sell refill packs; others design games, such as the large dungeon-crawling campaign Gloomhaven, that change over time without destroying anything. Talking about a campaign risks giving away surprises to others who haven't played; see [[Spoiler Etiquette]].

Legacy games resemble long-running tabletop role-playing campaigns, which have always accumulated history; see [[Tabletop Role-Playing Game]]. A game whose parts are steadily replaced raises a question familiar to philosophers; see [[Ship of Theseus]].
