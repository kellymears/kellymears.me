The **video game crash of 1983** was the collapse of the North American home video game market. Revenues fell by around 97 percent between 1983 and 1985, several console makers left the business, and retailers came to see video games as a passing fad. The market recovered only after Nintendo arrived with a new console and strict rules about who could publish for it.

The boom before it was enormous. The Atari 2600, released in 1977, made home games a mass market; see [[Racing the Beam]]. By the early 1980s a crowd of competing consoles, including the Intellivision, ColecoVision, and Odyssey², was on sale, along with games from dozens of publishers. Arcade hits drove demand; see [[Arcade Game]].

Several causes combined:

- **Third-party floods.** In 1979 programmers who had left Atari founded Activision, the first company to make games for someone else's console. Others followed, and firms with no game experience, including a subsidiary of the cereal maker Quaker Oats, rushed out cheap titles.
- **Indistinguishable quality.** Shoppers couldn't tell good cartridges from bad before buying, so bad ones dragged down prices and trust for all; see [[Market for lemons]].
- **Famous flops.** Atari's 1982 home version of *Pac-Man* disappointed players, and its *E.T.* game, built in about five weeks for the Christmas season, sold far below the millions produced.
- **Home computers.** Price cuts on machines like the Commodore 64 let parents buy a computer that also played games.
- **Overproduction.** Publishers and retailers ordered on the assumption the boom would continue, then dumped unsold stock at bargain prices; see [[Speculative Bubble]].

In September 1983 Atari buried truckloads of unsold cartridges and hardware in a landfill in Alamogordo, New Mexico. The burial became a legend, often doubted, until a 2014 excavation dug up copies of *E.T.* and other games, now treated as artifacts; see [[Game Preservation]].

The crash was mostly an American event. Japan's market grew, with Nintendo's Famicom released in 1983, and home computer games thrived in Europe; see [[Demoscene]].

Nintendo brought the Famicom to North America in 1985 as the Nintendo Entertainment System, deliberately avoiding the words "video game." It was marketed with a toy robot and sold as an entertainment system to wary retailers. A lockout chip prevented unlicensed cartridges from running, licensed publishers were limited in how many games they could release each year, and a seal of quality promised buyers the game was approved. It succeeded, and the walled garden model it established still governs consoles and app stores; see [[Platform economics]] and [[Digital rights management]].

*Super Mario Bros.* helped carry the console and set the template for the revival; see [[Platformer]]. The crash still serves as a warning about what happens when a market fills with products buyers can't evaluate.
