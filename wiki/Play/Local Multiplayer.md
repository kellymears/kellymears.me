**Local multiplayer** means several people playing the same game on one machine, in the same room. It's how video games started. The earliest commercial hits were two-player games, played side by side at an arcade cabinet or on a living room television; see [[Arcade Game]].

There are three main arrangements:

- **Shared screen.** Everyone appears in one view, as in four-player arcade dungeon crawlers or platform fighters. It keeps players close together, which suits cooperation and close-range competition; see [[Fighting Game]].
- **Split screen.** The display is divided so each player gets their own camera, as in racing games and shooters. Each view must render the scene separately, which costs performance and often lowers detail; see [[Frame Rate]].
- **Hot seat.** Players take turns on the same device, passing the controller or keyboard. Turn-based strategy games used it widely before online play was common.

Split screen created its own etiquette. Since every player's view was visible to everyone else, glancing at an opponent's quarter of the screen revealed their position. Some groups banned it, others treated it as part of the game; see [[Fog of War]] and [[Perfect Information]].

The appeal is social. Playing beside someone means shared reactions, trash talk, coordination by voice and gesture, and the presence of another person, which changes how people play; see [[Social facilitation]]. Cooperative games built for one room lean into this, demanding shouted coordination that would feel different over a headset; see [[Cooperative Game]]. Games about deception gain from watching faces; see [[Social Deduction Game]].

Local play declined through the 2000s and 2010s as online play took over. Big-budget games dropped split screen to free up rendering power for one detailed view, and multiplayer increasingly meant playing with strangers or friends in other homes. Rhythm games with plastic instrument controllers were an exception, filling living rooms with bands for a few years; see [[Rhythm Game]].

Small studios led a revival. Independent developers released couch competitive and cooperative games built specifically for one screen, and consoles with controllers that split in two made a second player easy to add; see [[Indie Game]]. Some services now stream a local multiplayer game over the internet, letting remote friends join as if they were on the couch, which is a different solution from the networking used for online competition; see [[Rollback Netcode]].

Local multiplayer also lowers barriers. A friend who has never played can pick up a second controller, and difficulty settings or helper roles can let experienced and new players share a game; see [[Assist Mode]]. Families and households where people take turns benefit too, though griefing a person on the same couch has more immediate consequences than doing it online; see [[Griefing]].
