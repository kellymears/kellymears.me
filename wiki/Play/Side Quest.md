A **side quest** is an optional task outside a game's main storyline. A villager asks the player to find a lost ring, deliver a letter, or clear wolves from a farm. Completing it brings a reward, perhaps experience, money, or an item, and sometimes a small story of its own, but the main plot moves forward without it.

The form comes from role-playing games, both tabletop and digital, where adventurers wander between towns and pick up work as they go; see [[Tabletop Role-Playing Game]] and [[Computer Role-Playing Game]]. Side quests give a world texture. They introduce characters the main story has no room for, reward exploration, and let players decide how much of a game to see.

Their quality varies enormously. At the low end is the **fetch quest**: go somewhere, collect a number of items or kill a number of creatures, return. Large online role-playing games became notorious for these, since they're cheap to produce in volume. At the high end, side quests can carry a game's best writing, sometimes with consequences that ripple into the main story. Some role-playing games of the 2010s were praised for giving optional quests as much care as the central plot, with moral choices and characters who react to the player's decisions; see [[Branching Narrative]] and [[Non-Player Character]].

Side quests raise a recurring tension. The main story often insists on urgency: the world is ending, a friend has been kidnapped, the enemy army marches. Meanwhile the hero helps a farmer find chickens. The mismatch between what the story says and what the game encourages is a classic case of a broader problem; see [[Ludonarrative Dissonance]]. A few games resolve it by making time real, with a deadline that keeps running or a clock that resets, forcing players to choose what to skip; see [[Time as Resource]].

Open-world games expanded side content to enormous scale, often marking dozens of activities on a map. Critics argue this turns exploration into a checklist, and players feel obliged to clear every icon; see [[Open World Design]] and [[Checklist]]. The pull of unfinished tasks is real, and an open quest log can nag like an unfinished chore; see [[Zeigarnik Effect]] and [[Completionism]].

Rewards complicate things further. If side quests grant needed experience, they become mandatory in practice, and skipping them makes the main story harder; see [[Experience Point]]. If they grant nothing, players may skip them entirely.

The best side quests often work through what the player finds instead of what they're told, a quiet location with a story told by its objects or a hidden encounter with no marker at all; see [[Environmental Storytelling]]. Escorting someone fragile through danger is a type of side quest players have learned to dread; see [[Escort Mission]].
