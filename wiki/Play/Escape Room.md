An **escape room** is a live game in which a small team is shut in a themed room, or a series of rooms, and must solve a chain of puzzles to get out, usually within sixty minutes. Puzzles unlock padlocks, hidden drawers, and secret doors, and each solution leads to the next.

The idea came from video games first. Browser games such as *Crimson Room* (2004), by Toshimitsu Takagi, trapped the player in a room to search and combine objects, a descendant of older adventure games; see [[Point-and-Click Adventure]]. In 2007 the Japanese company SCRAP ran a "Real Escape Game" in Kyoto, putting players physically in the room. Parapark opened in Budapest in 2011, and the format spread through Europe and North America, reaching thousands of venues worldwide within a decade.

Rooms differ in how puzzles connect:

- **Linear**: each puzzle unlocks the next, so the whole team works on one thing at a time.
- **Open**: several puzzles are available at once, so a team can split up.
- **Pyramid**: separate threads that converge on a final lock.

Good rooms use the physical space to tell a story; see [[Environmental Storytelling]]. Clues hide in props, and the best puzzles depend on seeing an ordinary object in a new way, such as reading a painting as a map or using a mirror to reveal writing; see [[Functional Fixedness]] and [[Insight]]. Rooms with interconnected puzzles often feel like walking through a game where knowledge is the only key; see [[Metroidbrainia]].

The format is a team exercise in communication. Groups solve more when members announce what they find and keep track of who has which piece; see [[Transactive Memory]] and [[Cooperative Game]]. The clock adds pressure that sharpens focus but makes people miss the obvious; see [[Flow]] and [[Time as Resource]]. A staff member watching through cameras offers hints when a team stalls, playing a role much like a [[Game Master]].

Design practice has settled on some conventions. Players are told up front what not to touch, such as light fittings or ceiling tiles, to stop them dismantling the room. **Red herrings**, clues that lead nowhere, are widely disliked because, with limited time, they feel unfair. Interfaces built into the fiction, like a keypad on a fake bomb, keep immersion; see [[Diegetic Interface]].

Safety became a central concern after a 2019 fire at an escape room in Koszalin, Poland, killed five teenage girls who could not get out. Poland closed a large number of venues for inspection, and the industry now commonly keeps doors unlocked or provides a panic release, so the lock is part of the fiction rather than real; see [[Magic Circle]].

Board game and box versions bring the format home, and some games online borrow it too.
