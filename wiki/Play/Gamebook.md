**A gamebook** is a book the reader plays. Instead of reading pages in order, the reader reaches a choice at the end of each section, such as "to open the door, turn to 87; to run, turn to 112," and follows the instruction. The story branches, and different readers reach different endings.

Branching books had been tried earlier, but the form took off with the Choose Your Own Adventure series, published by Bantam Books from 1979, beginning with The Cave of Time by Edward Packard. Written in the second person, with the reader as the hero, the books were short, fast, and had many endings, a large share of them deaths. The series is widely reported to have sold more than 250 million copies.

In 1982, the British series Fighting Fantasy, started by Steve Jackson and Ian Livingstone with The Warlock of Firetop Mountain, added game rules. Readers rolled dice to create a character with scores for skill, stamina, and luck, fought monsters by rolling against them, and tracked items on a sheet. It brought the mechanics of tabletop role-playing to a single reader; see [[Tabletop Role-Playing Game]] and [[Skill Check]]. Many similar series followed in the 1980s, including the long-running Lone Wolf books, then faded in the 1990s as home computer games did the same thing with less bookkeeping. Several series have since been reissued, both in print for adults who read them as children and as apps.

The books have a characteristic structure:

- **Numbered sections**, shuffled so readers can't easily peek at outcomes.
- **Branch and bottleneck.** Paths split and rejoin at key points, keeping the writing manageable, since every fully separate path would double the work; see [[Branching Narrative]].
- **Many endings**, usually including several abrupt deaths; see [[Multiple Endings]] and [[Permadeath]].
- **Gated paths**, in which a door opens only if the reader has an item or a number found earlier.

Readers developed their own tricks, and the books' authors knew it. The most common was keeping a finger in the previous page to go back after a bad choice, a manual version of reloading a save; see [[Save Scumming]] and [[Undo]].

Gamebooks overlap heavily with interactive fiction on computers, which arrived in the same years; see [[Interactive Fiction]]. Both work like linked documents a reader moves through by choice; see [[Hypertext]]. Later descendants include visual novels and narrative video games driven by choices; see [[Visual Novel]]. Netflix's interactive film Bandersnatch, from 2018, put the format on television.

The second-person voice makes the reader the protagonist and occasionally talks to them directly about the book; see [[Fourth Wall]].
