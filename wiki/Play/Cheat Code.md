A **cheat code** is a secret input that changes a game's rules in the player's favor: infinite lives, invincibility, every weapon, a skipped level. Typing a password, pressing buttons in a set order, or entering a phrase at a console unlocks behavior the designers built but didn't advertise.

Many cheats began as tools for the people making the game. Testing a late level is slow if the tester has to play through every earlier one and survive, so programmers added shortcuts and sometimes left them in; see [[Playtesting]]. The best-known sequence, up, up, down, down, left, right, left, right, B, A, is usually said to have been added to a 1980s Konami shooter because its programmer found the game too hard to test. It then appeared in dozens of later games; see [[Konami Code]].

In the 1980s and 1990s, cheats were folklore. They spread through playground rumor, game magazines, strategy guides, and paid telephone hint lines. Some were real, many were invented, and the difference was hard to check. PC shooters gave cheats a typed form, and certain strings became famous well beyond the games that used them.

Hardware went further. **Cheat devices** sat between a console and its cartridge and altered the values the game read. The Game Genie, released in 1990, let players type codes that patched specific memory addresses, and Nintendo sued its maker over it. The courts sided with the device maker, finding that altering a game for private play didn't infringe copyright. Later devices edited a console's working memory directly, and PC programs called trainers do the same; see [[Datamining]] and [[Game Modding]].

Cheats served real purposes. They let struggling players see the ending, gave experienced players a sandbox to fool around in, and helped speedrunners and researchers probe how games work; see [[Tool-Assisted Speedrun]]. Modern settings that slow time or grant invincibility do the same job openly, without the secrecy; see [[Assist Mode]].

Several changes pushed cheats aside:

- **Online play.** A cheat that affects other people's games isn't playful, it's unfair, and publishers fight it with monitoring software; see [[Anti-Cheat]].
- **Achievements.** Many games disable trophies once a cheat is used, so cheating now costs something; see [[Achievement Hunting]].
- **Monetization.** Unlocks that a cheat once gave away became items sold for real money, and critics have pointed out that free shortcuts compete with paid ones; see [[Free-to-Play]].

Whether cheating spoils a game depends on who is affected. In single-player games, bending the rules is arguably just another way to play, the same impulse as reloading a save after a mistake; see [[Save Scumming]]. Games run on agreed rules, and a cheat is a private renegotiation of them; see [[Magic Circle]].
