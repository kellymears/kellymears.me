A **tabletop role-playing game** (TTRPG) is a game in which players each control a character in a shared imaginary world, deciding what their characters say and do, while one participant, the **game master**, describes the world, voices everyone else in it, and decides how the rules apply. Dice or other randomizers settle uncertain outcomes. There's usually no board and no winner; play is ongoing, often across a campaign of many sessions.

**Dungeons & Dragons**, created by Gary Gygax and Dave Arneson and published in 1974, grew out of tabletop wargames, where players commanded armies of miniatures. D&D shifted the focus to individual characters who could attempt anything the players described. It became a cultural phenomenon, survived a moral panic in the 1980s, and has had a large revival since the mid-2010s, helped by actual-play shows in which groups stream their sessions.

What makes TTRPGs distinctive is the human referee. A computer game can only offer the actions its designers built. At a table, a player can try to bribe the guard, set the inn on fire, or befriend the dragon, and the game master improvises a response. That flexibility is something video games have chased for decades; see [[Immersive Sim]] and [[Emergent Gameplay]].

Core elements:

- **Character sheets.** Numbers describing what a character is good at, which grow with experience; see [[Skill Tree]].
- **Resolution mechanics.** Rolling dice against a target number, the origin of the [[Skill Check]].
- **Session zero.** A conversation before a campaign starts about tone, content limits, and expectations, which many groups now treat as an essential first step.
- **The social contract.** Unwritten rules about sharing the spotlight, respecting the group, and keeping the [[Magic Circle]] intact.

TTRPGs shaped video games directly. Early computer role-playing games such as *Ultima*, *Wizardry*, and later *Baldur's Gate* adapted D&D's rules to computers; see [[Computer Role-Playing Game]]. Hit points, experience points, character classes, and dungeons all come from the tabletop. Text adventures and MUDs grew from the same culture; see [[Interactive Fiction]] and [[Bartle Taxonomy]].

The hobby has diversified far beyond fantasy combat: story games that focus on narrative over rules, solo journaling games, horror games about investigation, games written for exactly two players, and very short games playable in a single evening.

Language models have renewed interest in automated game masters. They can improvise in a way older systems couldn't, but they struggle with consistency over long campaigns, fair rule adjudication, and the social judgment a good human game master brings; see [[Large Language Model]] and [[Procedural Narrative]].
