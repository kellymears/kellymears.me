**Tutorial design** is the craft of teaching players how a game works. The best tutorials hardly seem like tutorials: they teach through play, one idea at a time, and leave players feeling they figured it out themselves. The worst stop the game to show pages of text about controls the player hasn't needed yet.

The canonical example is the first level of *Super Mario Bros.* (1985). With no instructions, its opening screens teach that Mario moves right, that the mushroom-shaped enemy hurts, that jumping on it kills it, and that blocks hide items, by arranging the level so the player can hardly avoid learning; see [[World 1-1]]. The level's layout carries the lesson that text would otherwise carry.

Principles that recur:

- **One idea at a time.** Introduce a mechanic alone, in a safe place, before combining it with others; see [[Cognitive Load]] and [[Progressive Disclosure]].
- **Teach just in time.** Explain the grappling hook when the player gets it, not at the start; see [[Metroidvania]].
- **Show, then let them do.** A demonstration, then a low-stakes chance to try, then a real test, the order used in many sports and crafts; see [[Motor Learning]].
- **Make it readable.** Shape, color, and sound should signal what can be used or climbed or will hurt; see [[Affordance]] and [[Telegraphing]].
- **Let failure be cheap** while learning, then raise the stakes; see [[Desirable Difficulty]].

Nintendo designer Koichi Hayashida described building levels of *Super Mario 3D Land* on the four-part structure of Japanese storytelling called **kishōtenketsu**: introduce a mechanic, develop it, twist it with a surprise, then conclude, all within one short level. *Portal* (2007) applies a similar progression across test chambers, each adding one rule to those already learned.

Common failures:

1. **Front-loading** every control in a long opening sequence, which players forget before they need it.
2. **Unskippable tutorials** that bore returning and experienced players.
3. **Tutorials that teach the wrong lesson**, such as a safe training area where the danger later learned is absent.
4. **Explaining what the interface already shows** instead of making it clear.
5. **Teaching only the basics** of games whose depth lies elsewhere. Fighting games long left players to learn advanced timing from the community; recent ones include detailed trials; see [[Fighting Game]].

Tutorials are tested more than most of a game, since designers can't judge what is obvious; see [[Playtesting]]. Many studios measure where new players quit, since the first minutes decide whether many stay at all, the same concern product designers call onboarding; see [[Onboarding]].

The ideal is a game that teaches at the pace of the player's growing skill, keeping them in the narrow band between boredom and frustration; see [[Flow]] and [[Difficulty Curve]].
