**A virtual pet** is a simulated creature that a player feeds, cleans, plays with, and keeps healthy. It lives in real time: it gets hungry while the player is away, and neglect makes it sick, unhappy, or, in many versions, dead.

The form became a global craze with the Tamagotchi, a small egg-shaped device on a keychain released by the Japanese toy company Bandai in 1996. Its pixelated creature hatched, grew through stages depending on how well it was cared for, and beeped for attention at any hour, including during school and the middle of the night. Tens of millions sold within just a few years. Schools in several countries banned them because they interrupted classes, and children asked parents to babysit them.

Earlier software had experimented with simulated companions, such as Little Computer People in 1985, and computer pet dogs and cats followed in the mid-1990s. Later virtual pets moved online, as on the website Neopets, launched in 1999, and to handheld consoles, with Nintendogs in 2005. Creature-raising became part of larger games too; see [[Creature Collector]].

The design hinges on time:

- **Real-time needs.** The pet's state changes whether or not the player is present, so checking in becomes a routine; see [[Time as Resource]] and [[Habit Formation]].
- **Consequence.** A pet that can die makes care feel meaningful; see [[Permadeath]]. Later versions softened this with pauses, departures, and revivals.
- **Growth.** The creature changes depending on care, rewarding attention with a visible result.

People form real feelings for these creatures, a reaction sometimes called the Tamagotchi effect. Children grieved dead Tamagotchis, and adults talk to software that shows a few signs of need. Humans readily attribute minds and feelings to things that behave as if they have them; see [[ELIZA Effect]] and [[Theory of Mind]]. Some research and commentary has asked whether virtual pets help lonely people, much as some companion robots are designed to; see [[Loneliness]] and [[Attachment Theory]].

The same mechanics now drive engagement in apps that have nothing to do with pets. A creature that looks sad when the user skips a day turns guilt into a retention tool, and streaks that break if a day is missed work on the same principle; see [[Gamification]] and [[Dark Pattern]]. Games that run while the player is away have a similar pull; see [[Idle Game]].

Modern caretaking games tend toward gentleness, with no death and low pressure, as part of a broader trend toward relaxing play; see [[Cozy Game]]. Characters that follow and depend on the player in larger games draw on the same instincts; see [[Companion AI]].
