**Loot** is the equipment, currency, and items that games hand out as rewards, dropped by defeated enemies, found in chests, or earned from quests. In many games it's drawn at random, so defeating the same monster twice can yield different rewards, and the hope of something rare keeps players going.

Behind the randomness sits a **loot table**: a list of possible items, each with a weight that sets its chance of dropping. A common item might have a weight of a hundred, a rare one a weight of one. Tables can nest, with one roll choosing a rarity tier and another choosing an item within it, and they can depend on difficulty, level, or the player's luck stat. Players frequently extract the exact numbers from game files; see [[Datamining]].

Random item generation came from early dungeon games, where the contents of each level were rolled fresh; see [[Roguelike]]. Diablo (1996) made it a genre's foundation, with items built from a base type plus randomly chosen prefixes and suffixes, and names colored by rarity. That color code, from common white through magic blue to rare and unique golds and oranges, spread across the industry. Online role-playing games added raid loot shared among dozens of players, with systems to decide who got what; see [[Multi-User Dungeon]].

Borderlands (2009) brought the formula to shooters, advertising a near-endless variety of procedurally generated guns, and Destiny (2014) followed, defining the **looter shooter**; see [[Procedural Generation]] and [[First-Person Shooter]]. Battle royale games scatter loot across the map, making the opening minutes a scramble; see [[Battle Royale]].

The appeal draws on how unpredictable rewards shape behavior. Rewards given after an unpredictable number of attempts sustain effort far more than fixed ones, which is why loot hunting can feel compulsive; see [[Variable-Ratio Reinforcement]] and [[Compulsion Loop]]. Long droughts are frustrating, so many games guarantee a rare drop after enough failures; see [[Pity Timer]]. Chasing small statistical upgrades through repeated runs of the same dungeon is a classic form of grinding, and many players find it relaxing rather than tedious.

Loot also creates economic pressure. Each drop adds items to the game world, and over time powerful gear becomes common and older rewards lose value. Designers balance this with ways to destroy or consume items; see [[Faucets and Sinks]]. Each expansion tends to raise item power to give players new goals; see [[Power Creep]].

Selling random loot for real money produced the loot box, a purchase with an unknown item inside. Several countries have examined whether loot boxes amount to gambling, especially for children, and some have restricted them; see [[Gacha Monetization]] and [[Free-to-Play]].
