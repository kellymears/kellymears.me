**Playtesting** is watching people play an unfinished game to learn whether it does what its designers intend. It is the central method of game design, because a game is a set of rules whose experience emerges only in play, and no amount of thinking about the rules predicts it reliably; see [[MDA Framework]] and [[Emergent Gameplay]].

Designers are the worst possible testers of their own work. They know where the secret is, what the icon means, and why the puzzle has to be solved this way, and they can't unknow it; see [[Curse of Knowledge]]. The first stranger to sit down will get stuck in places the team stopped noticing months ago.

Common forms:

- **Internal tests** by the team, for whether a system works at all.
- **Friends and family**, cheap but polite, and too familiar with the designers to be confused in the useful ways.
- **Fresh testers**, recruited once and never reused, because a tester who has learned the game can no longer show what a newcomer does.
- **Blind tests** in board games, where a group learns from the rulebook alone with the designer absent, testing the rules as written rather than as explained.
- **Telemetry** from many players, recording where they die, quit, or wander; see [[Heatmap]].

Valve's development of *Half-Life* in the late 1990s became a well-known example: designers watched testers in silence, noted every hesitation, and reworked levels until people did the intended thing without help. Studios now run dedicated user research labs with cameras, recordings, and eye tracking, borrowing the methods of [[Usability Testing]].

A few rules recur across the craft:

1. **Don't explain or defend.** If testers need help, the game failed, and the moment of failure is the finding.
2. **Watch behavior over opinions.** What players do is more reliable than what they say, and asking them to talk aloud as they play reveals what they expected at each moment.
3. **Trust the problem, not the proposed fix.** Players are usually right that something is wrong and usually wrong about how to fix it.
4. **Test early and roughly.** A paper version or graybox level exposes problems before art makes them expensive to change; see [[Paper prototype]] and [[Level Design]].

Playtesting has limits. Testers know they are being watched and play more diligently than people at home; see [[Hawthorne Effect]]. A handful of testers suits finding problems but not judging their prevalence; see [[Sample size]]. Recruiting only enthusiasts misses the players a game most needs to keep; see [[Selection Bias]].

Testers also tune difficulty, revealing where a challenge spikes or becomes trivial, and they reliably find any strategy the designers overlooked that beats everything else; see [[Difficulty Curve]] and [[Dominant Strategy]].
