A game is called **pay-to-win** when spending real money buys an advantage in play, such as stronger weapons, better characters or faster progress, over players who don't pay. The term is an accusation. Players accept paying for a game, and often for cosmetic extras, but resent losing to a credit card.

The model grew with [[Free-to-Play]] games, which earn their revenue through **microtransactions**, small in-game purchases. In many such games most players spend nothing, and a small fraction of heavy spenders, nicknamed **whales**, provides most of the revenue; see [[Long tail]]. Selling power to those players is lucrative, but it risks driving away the non-paying majority who make up the community and supply the opponents.

Pay-to-win comes in degrees. The most direct form sells items that are simply better. Randomized purchases sell chances at power, as in loot boxes and gacha pulls; see [[Gacha Monetization]] and [[Variable-Ratio Reinforcement]]. Time savers let players skip a deliberately slow progression; defenders call them convenience, while critics note that the slowness was designed to be annoying enough to pay to escape; see [[Grinding]] and [[Dark Pattern]]. Collectible card games have long faced the charge in a gentler form, since buying more packs builds a stronger collection; see [[Collectible Card Game]]. Over time, releasing new purchasable items that outclass the old pushes paying players to keep paying; see [[Power Creep]].

The best-known backlash came in 2017 with Star Wars Battlefront II. Its loot crates contained cards that boosted abilities, and unlocking popular characters seemed to take dozens of hours without paying. A company reply on Reddit explaining that the goal was to give players "a sense of pride and accomplishment" became the most downvoted comment in the site's history. Purchases were switched off before launch, and progression was rebuilt in 2018 to be earned through play. That same year, Belgium's gaming regulator concluded that some loot boxes were illegal gambling.

Many publishers responded by moving to cosmetic-only sales, where money buys appearance rather than power, as in the [[Battle Pass]] model. Cosmetics still sell partly on status, since a rare outfit is a visible sign of spending; see [[Conspicuous consumption]].

For competitive play, purchased advantages undermine the idea that a ranking reflects skill; see [[Matchmaking Rating]] and [[Esports]]. Some games try to contain the damage by matching spenders against each other or by capping purchased power in ranked modes. Others accept the trade openly.

Whether a game crosses the line is disputed. A rough test players apply is whether someone who never pays can, with skill and reasonable time, compete on equal terms. When the answer is no, the game is selling victory rather than entertainment, and a [[Live Service Game]] that depends on goodwill can lose its audience fast.
