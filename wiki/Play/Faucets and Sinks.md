In a game economy, **faucets** are sources that create currency and items, such as monster drops, quest rewards, and daily login bonuses. **Sinks** remove them: repair costs, consumables, fees, taxes on trades, and items that break or get used up. The balance between the two decides whether money in the game holds its value.

In a single-player game, imbalance is mostly a pacing problem. In an online world where currency persists and players trade, it becomes inflation. Faucets run constantly as thousands of players play, and if sinks don't keep pace, money piles up and prices climb. New players arrive into an economy where everything costs a fortune and their rewards buy little.

**Mudflation**, a term from the text-based online worlds of the 1990s, describes the item version. Gear never leaves the world, so as players accumulate it, once-rare items become common and nearly worthless, and designers add stronger items to keep rewards exciting, which repeats the cycle; see [[Multi-User Dungeon]] and [[Power Creep]].

Notable episodes:

- **Diablo II.** Gold became so plentiful that players stopped using it and traded with a particular ring, the Stone of Jordan, as their de facto currency.
- **EVE Online.** Its player-run economy grew complex enough that the developer hired an economist in 2007 and published regular economic reports.
- **Diablo III.** Launched in 2012 with an auction house that traded items for real money. It made buying gear more effective than finding it, which undercut the core reward of play, and it closed in 2014; see [[Compulsion Loop]].

Common sinks:

- **Upkeep**: repair costs, fuel, and durability that consume value over time.
- **Transaction fees** on trades and auctions, which take a cut of every exchange.
- **Item destruction**: upgrading by consuming items, risking loss on failure, or losing gear on death; see [[Permadeath]].
- **Status goods**: costly cosmetics, housing, and mounts that people buy for display; see [[Veblen Good]].

**Real-money trading** complicates everything. When items can be sold for cash outside the game, professional gold farming follows, along with bots, account theft, and economic distortion. Games that sell currency themselves face a conflict between economic health and revenue; see [[Free-to-Play]] and [[Gacha Monetization]].

Ongoing games manage their economies continuously, adjusting drop rates and adding sinks with each update; see [[Live Service Game]]. Some economies don't use money at all and run on favors and shared gifts; see [[Gift Economy]]. Players' time is the input behind every faucet; see [[Time as Resource]].

Designers sometimes compare the role to a central bank's: money creation and removal are policy choices; see [[Modern Monetary Theory]].
