A **wargame** simulates military conflict, either for entertainment or as a professional tool for training officers and testing plans. Wargames sit at the root of several hobby traditions and remain part of how armies, governments, and companies explore decisions too dangerous to rehearse for real.

Chess descends from an Indian game whose pieces stood for the divisions of an army, but the modern form began in Prussia. Around 1812, Georg von Reisswitz built a sand-table game of terrain and troops, and in 1824 his son, an army officer, published **Kriegsspiel**, "war game," played on real military maps with blocks for units. An umpire resolved combat using dice and tables based on battlefield experience, and gave each side only the information its scouts could see; see [[Fog of War]]. The chief of the general staff reportedly declared it not a game but training for war. After Prussia's victories in 1866 and 1870, other armies copied it.

Professional wargaming spread through navies and staff colleges; the US Naval War College has run them since the 1880s. During the Cold War, analysts used games to explore nuclear crises, and wargames remain standard for testing plans against an opponent who fights back; see [[Red teaming]]. A 2002 US exercise became famous when the opposing commander's unconventional tactics sank much of the fleet early on, and the exercise was reset, which critics said defeated its purpose.

The hobby took a different path. H. G. Wells published **Little Wars** in 1913, rules for battles with toy soldiers in the garden. Charles S. Roberts' *Tactics* (1954) and his company Avalon Hill made commercial board wargames a genre, adopting hexagonal grids by the early 1960s, which keep distances more even than squares; see [[Tile Map]]. Cardboard counters on hex maps became the standard, sometimes with thousands of pieces.

Miniatures gaming led somewhere unexpected. *Chainmail* (1971), medieval rules by Gary Gygax and Jeff Perren, included a fantasy supplement, and three years later Gygax and Dave Arneson turned single-figure combat into Dungeons & Dragons; see [[Tabletop Role-Playing Game]]. Computer wargames grew into strategy genres; see [[Grand Strategy Game]].

Designing a wargame means deciding what to simulate. Every game abstracts, and its choices encode a theory of what matters in war: supply, morale, terrain, command delay. A game that ignores supply teaches that supply doesn't matter; see [[Map and Territory]] and [[Procedural Rhetoric]]. Randomness stands for friction, the unpredictability of real combat; see [[Input and Output Randomness]].

A wargame is a structured thought experiment with an opponent. Its value lies less in predicting outcomes than in exposing assumptions, surprises, and decisions players hadn't considered; see [[Thought Experiment]] and [[Monte Carlo Method]].
