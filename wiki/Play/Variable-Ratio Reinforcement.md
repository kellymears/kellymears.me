**Variable-ratio reinforcement** is a schedule in which a reward follows a response, but the number of responses required varies unpredictably around an average rather than being fixed. It is one of the four simple schedules of partial reinforcement — fixed and variable ratio, fixed and variable interval — though Charles Ferster and B.F. Skinner's *Schedules of Reinforcement* (1957) catalogued many more than four. Skinner's pigeon and rat experiments, and the human behavioral literature that followed, established a specific and well-replicated finding — variable-ratio schedules produce the highest steady rate of response of any schedule tested, and behavior maintained on one resists extinction more than behavior maintained on a fixed schedule, because the subject cannot tell whether the next response is the one that pays.

A slot machine is the textbook illustration, not a metaphor bolted on afterward — the mechanism was described in operant terms before it was applied to game design. Loot drops with a hidden percentage chance, randomized pulls in a [[Gacha Monetization]] system common to [[Free-to-Play]] releases, and rare-item tables in an [[Idle Game]] or loot-driven [[Roguelike]] are structurally the same schedule: [[Seeded Randomness]] or a live generator sets the interval, and the player cannot predict which attempt resolves it — the condition under which the schedule's effect is strongest.

What is established is the behavioral finding above, replicated across species and settings. What is contested is the leap from that finding to clinical **addiction** in the substance-dependence sense. Problem-gambling research treats variable-ratio reinforcement as a documented contributing mechanism, but "gaming disorder" is a narrower, more disputed diagnostic category — the ICD-11 recognizes it, while the DSM-5 lists internet gaming disorder only as a condition proposed for further study — and correlational links between loot-box spending and problem-gambling scores do not by themselves prove that a [[Compulsion Loop]] operates on the brain the way [[Physical Dependence]] does. The schedule explains the pull; it does not by itself prove the addiction.
