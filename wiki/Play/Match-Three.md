A **match-three** game is a puzzle in which the player swaps adjacent tiles on a grid to line up three or more of the same kind, which then disappear. New tiles fall in to fill the gaps, sometimes forming new matches on their own. Simple to learn and endlessly replayable, the genre includes some of the most played games ever made.

Its ancestors include *Tetris* (1984), which established clearing lines of falling blocks, and tile-matching games of the late 1980s and 1990s. The modern form arrived with *Bejeweled* (2001) by PopCap Games, a browser game of gem swapping that sold tens of millions of copies and helped create the market for casual games played in short sessions. *Candy Crush Saga* (2012) carried the formula to phones and social networks, with hundreds of millions of players.

Why it works:

- **Constant feedback.** Every match produces sound, animation, and points, and the falling tiles often trigger chain reactions the player didn't plan, which feel like rewards; see [[Game Feel]].
- **Low cost of failure.** A bad move rarely ends a game, so players can play while distracted.
- **Unpredictable payoffs.** New tiles arrive at random, so big cascades come on an irregular schedule, the pattern that keeps people playing slot machines; see [[Variable-Ratio Reinforcement]].
- **Visible near-completion.** Partly finished goals stay on screen, nagging at the player to finish them; see [[Zeigarnik Effect]].

The genre's design has evolved around levels with specific goals and limited moves. Instead of an endless board, *Candy Crush* sets puzzles: clear the jelly, bring ingredients to the bottom, all within a set number of moves. Special tiles created by matching four or five, and combinations of special tiles, add strategy. Hundreds of levels unfold on a map, so the game always shows the next goal; see [[Difficulty Curve]].

The same structure supports one of mobile gaming's most profitable business models. The game is free; players run out of lives after failing levels and wait or pay to refill them, and boosters can be bought to clear hard levels. Some levels are tuned to be failed narrowly, just short of the goal, which makes a purchase tempting. Critics see this as exploiting near-miss psychology and loss aversion; see [[Free-to-Play]] and [[Dark Pattern]].

Match-three mechanics combine well with other genres. *Puzzle Quest* (2007) made matching gems the combat system of a role-playing game, and many mobile games use matching to drive battles or decorating stories; see [[Computer Role-Playing Game]].

Programmers like the genre as an exercise: detecting matches, applying gravity, and resolving cascades on a grid is a compact problem, and generating boards with no accidental matches or no available moves adds a puzzle of its own; see [[Procedural Generation]].
