**Mahjong** is a tile game, usually for four players, that took its modern form in China in the nineteenth century. Its exact origins are unclear. It probably grew out of earlier Chinese card games, and the popular stories crediting it to Confucius are legend. A standard set has 136 tiles: three suits, usually called dots, bamboo and characters, numbered one to nine with four copies of each, plus honor tiles for the four winds and three dragons. Many sets add flower and season tiles for a total of 144.

Play resembles the card game rummy. Each player holds a hand of thirteen tiles, draws one and discards one each turn, and aims to complete a hand of four sets and a pair. A set is three identical tiles or a run of three in one suit. Players can claim another player's discard to complete a set, which reveals part of their hand to the table. The first to complete a hand wins, and the scoring rules, which vary enormously between regional styles, reward rarer and more difficult hands.

Skill lies in managing what can't be seen. The wall of tiles is shuffled and the other hands are concealed, so a player has to reason from discards about what opponents are collecting and which tiles are dangerous to throw; see [[Hidden information]] and [[Input and Output Randomness]]. Each turn poses a choice between racing for a cheap hand and holding out for a valuable one; see [[Push Your Luck]] and [[Expected Value]]. The blend of luck, inference and nerve invites comparison with [[Poker]].

The game reached the United States in the early 1920s. Joseph Park Babcock, an American working in China, published simplified rules in 1920 under the trademarked spelling Mah-Jongg, and sets sold in huge numbers during a brief national fad. An American variant took root after the National Mah Jongg League was founded in New York in 1937, and it remains a long-running social tradition, especially among Jewish American women. In China the game's association with gambling made it officially discouraged for decades after 1949, though it never stopped being played at home. Japan developed **riichi mahjong**, named for the declaration a player makes when one tile from winning, which has a large online and professional scene.

Mahjong is also an AI benchmark for games with hidden information and chance. In 2019 a Microsoft Research program called Suphx reached the top rank on a major Japanese online server; see [[Reinforcement Learning]].

**Mahjong solitaire** is a different game that borrows the tiles. Players remove matching pairs from a stacked layout, a design popularized by Shanghai for home computers in 1986. It became a staple of casual gaming alongside [[Solitaire]] and [[Match-Three]] games.
