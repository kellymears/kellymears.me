A **crafting system** lets players combine gathered materials into new items according to recipes: wood and stone into an axe, herbs into a potion, ore into armor. It turns the world into a source of raw materials and gives players a way to make their own progress instead of waiting for it to drop as [[Loot]].

Early online role-playing games treated crafting as a profession. Ultima Online, from 1997, let players train skills such as blacksmithing and tailoring, and the goods they produced supplied an in-game economy. In massively multiplayer games, crafting soaks up materials that players collect and gives them something to trade, so designers tune it as part of the flow of resources into and out of the world; see [[Faucets and Sinks]].

Minecraft made crafting central to a generation of games. Its grid asks players to arrange items in a shape resembling the result, two sticks under three planks for a pickaxe, and the game originally explained none of the recipes, so players learned them from each other and from wikis. Survival games built on the same loop: gather, craft a better tool, use it to reach harder materials, craft again. Each step unlocks the next, a pattern that can be absorbing and that shades into a [[Compulsion Loop]]; see [[Sandbox Game]].

Crafting systems take several forms. Some are fixed recipe lists that serve mainly as a gate on progress, close to a [[Skill Tree]] in function. Others reward experiment, as in cooking systems that let players combine ingredients freely and discover what works, which can produce [[Emergent Gameplay]]. A [[Factory Game]] automates crafting into production chains, and a [[Colony Sim]] hands it off to simulated workers. Crafting from whatever lies around also fits the improvisation of the [[Immersive Sim]].

Players tend to value what they make themselves, a bias known in other settings as the [[IKEA Effect]], which helps crafted gear feel earned. Weapon durability systems use crafting to keep players gathering, though breaking weapons is one of the most divisive design choices in recent open-world games.

Crafting also has well-known failure modes. Bolted onto games that don't need it, it becomes padding: menus of recipes, inventories clogged with ingredients and errands to collect ten of something; see [[Grinding]]. Limited inventory space turns resource gathering into a packing problem; see [[Knapsack Problem]]. Large open worlds sometimes use crafting materials to give empty space a reason to be explored, which works only if gathering is enjoyable in itself; see [[Open World Design]].

The best crafting systems tie making things to the rest of the game, so that a crafted item opens a new place to go, a new way to solve a problem or a new thing to build.
