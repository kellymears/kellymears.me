**Meta-progression** is advancement that outlives the attempt which earned it. A run ends in death, the run-scoped state is discarded, and something — a currency, an unlocked option, a permanent statistical increase — persists into the next one. It is the single mechanism that separates the roguelite from the [[Roguelike]] proper, whose older definition insists that death returns the player to genuine zero.

The design problem it solves is the tail of the failure curve. A run-based game asks the player to lose repeatedly, and pure [[Seeded Randomness]] guarantees that some losses will be attributable to the seed rather than the player. Persistent unlocks convert those losses into deposits: the attempt still failed, but it was not spent. *Hades* is the case usually cited because it also spends the currency on narrative, so the payment for a failed run is a scene rather than a number, and the game's most-repeated activity becomes its primary delivery mechanism for story rather than an interruption of it.

**The mechanism is also the genre's main failure mode.** Persistent power makes early runs measurably harder than late ones, which inverts the difficulty curve against the player's growing skill. Push it far enough and the honest description of a loss changes from "played badly" to "attempted too early" — the game has begun measuring grind and calling it skill, a clean instance of [[Goodhart's Law]] operating on the player's own sense of progress. The extractive end of this design space is continuous with [[Gacha Monetization]], where the persistent track is deliberately paced to be uncomfortable and the discomfort is for sale, and with the compulsion literature that [[Physical Dependence]] describes in a different register.

The line worth holding is whether persistence removes obstacles or removes decisions. An unlock that adds a viable strategy widens the space the player reasons about; a flat damage increase narrows it, since the same strategy simply works now. The first sustains the [[Game Feel]] of mastery over hundreds of runs, and the second replaces mastery with attendance — which is why the mechanic appears wherever a designer wants a hard game to become a daily one, including in [[Live Service Game]] design that is not run-based at all.
