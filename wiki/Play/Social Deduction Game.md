A **social deduction game** splits players into an informed minority and an uninformed majority. The minority knows who its members are and tries to stay hidden; the majority must work out who is lying from what people say, how they vote, and how they behave. Almost all the play is conversation: accusing, defending, and reading each other.

The genre's ancestor is **Mafia**, created in 1986 by Dimitry Davidoff, a psychology student at Moscow State University, reportedly to study how an informed minority can manipulate an uninformed majority. Players are secretly assigned as townspeople or mafia. At night, everyone closes their eyes and the mafia silently choose someone to eliminate; during the day, everyone argues and votes to remove a suspect. The town wins by removing every member of the mafia, and the mafia wins by reaching parity. Andrew Plotkin's 1997 retheming as **Werewolf** spread it widely, and commercial versions added special roles, such as a seer who can check one player's identity each night.

Later games reshaped the formula:

- **The Resistance** (2009) removed player elimination, so nobody sits out, and replaced killings with missions whose results reveal that someone sabotaged them.
- **One Night Ultimate Werewolf** (2014) compressed the game to a single round of about ten minutes, with roles that swap identities so even players can't be sure what they are.
- **Among Us** (2018), a video game with tasks, sabotage, and emergency meetings, became hugely popular during the 2020 lockdowns, played over voice chat and streamed widely.

The games run on information asymmetry; see [[Information Asymmetry]]. The minority has more information but fewer numbers, while the majority has the votes but must convert scattered clues and behavior into a decision. Players try to guess what others know and want, constantly reasoning about others' beliefs about their beliefs; see [[Theory of Mind]]. Deception that holds up under questioning is hard, and liars often give themselves away through inconsistency more than nervousness.

Group dynamics decide many games. Early accusations snowball as people follow a confident speaker; see [[Social Proof]] and [[Availability Cascade]]. Quiet players attract suspicion whatever their role. A cohesive group can talk itself into the wrong answer; see [[Groupthink]].

Designers balance a few tensions. Too little information leaves the majority guessing at random; too much makes the minority's job impossible. Player elimination can leave people bored; see [[Magic Circle]] for why the agreed-upon rules let friends lie to each other comfortably. Some players enjoy deception and others find it stressful, so who is at the table matters as much as the rules.

The genre has appeared in research on group decision-making and in tests of language models' ability to deceive and detect deception.
