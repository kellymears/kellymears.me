A **real-time strategy** game, or RTS, has players gather resources, construct buildings, produce units, and fight, all at the same time and without turns. Everyone acts continuously, so speed of thought and of hand matter alongside strategy. The genre grew out of turn-based war games, where each side moves in sequence; see [[Wargame]].

Dune II, released by Westwood Studios in 1992, set the template: harvest a resource, spend it on structures that unlock units, and destroy the enemy base. Earlier games had mixed real-time action with strategy, but Dune II's loop is the one later games copied. Blizzard's Warcraft in 1994, Westwood's Command & Conquer in 1995, and Age of Empires in 1997 made the genre a pillar of PC gaming. StarCraft, released by Blizzard in 1998, became a national pastime in South Korea, where professional leagues filled stadiums and televised matches drew large audiences; see [[Esports]].

The skills split into two layers:

- **Macro** is the economy: building workers, expanding to new resources, keeping production running, and scouting to see what the opponent is doing; see [[Fog of War]]. Falling behind economically usually decides the game.
- **Micro** is the moment-to-moment control of units in a fight: pulling a wounded unit back, focusing fire, dodging an attack, or splitting units to avoid area damage.

Professional players issue several hundred actions per minute, a figure tracked and displayed by the games themselves. Opening sequences are memorized as **build orders**, refined collectively by the community; see [[Metagame]]. Units are balanced so each type beats some and loses to others, preventing any single army from dominating; see [[Dominant Strategy]]. The combination of planning under time pressure and constant attention switching makes the genre notoriously demanding; see [[Cognitive Load]].

Networking shaped the design. Sending the position of every unit over 1990s connections was impossible, so many RTS games used a deterministic lockstep model: each machine runs an identical simulation and only players' commands are exchanged. Any difference in calculation, however tiny, makes the games drift apart; see [[Determinism]].

The genre's custom map editors had an outsized legacy. Players built new games inside StarCraft and Warcraft III, and two of those modes grew into genres of their own: tower defense and the five-against-five battle arena games that eventually overtook the RTS in popularity; see [[Game Modding]] and [[Tower Defense]].

Through the 2010s, large-budget RTS releases became rare. The genre is hard to learn, punishing to lose one-on-one, and awkward on controllers. Its ideas continued elsewhere: in slower grand-scale strategy games, in auto battlers that remove the need for fast hands, and in city and colony builders; see [[Grand Strategy Game]], [[Auto Battler]], and [[Colony Sim]].
