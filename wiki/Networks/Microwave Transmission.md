**Microwave transmission** sends information between two fixed points using radio waves with frequencies from roughly one to a hundred gigahertz. Dish or horn antennas focus the energy into a narrow beam aimed at a receiver, often tens of kilometers away; see [[Antenna]] and [[Radio Spectrum]].

The beam must have a clear line of sight. Earth's curvature limits the distance between towers, typically to around 50 kilometers depending on height, so long routes are built as chains of **relay stations**, each receiving the signal, amplifying it and sending it on. Engineers also keep a margin around the straight line clear of obstacles, because radio energy spreads into an ellipse-shaped zone between the antennas; see [[Radio Propagation]].

Microwave relays matured after the Second World War, building on radar research. In 1951 AT&T opened a transcontinental chain of more than a hundred towers that carried telephone calls and, soon after, live television coast to coast. For several decades microwave carried much of long-distance telephone traffic in the United States, with many calls stacked onto each link; see [[Telephone Network]] and [[Multiplexing]]. Satellites took some traffic, and from the 1980s optical fiber, with far greater capacity and immunity to weather, replaced most long-haul links; see [[Optical Fiber]] and [[Satellite Internet]].

Microwave never disappeared. It's cheaper and faster to deploy than digging trenches for fiber, especially over rivers, mountains or private land. Many cell towers connect to the core network over microwave links, known as backhaul, and rural internet providers use it to bridge gaps; see [[Cellular Network]] and [[Last Mile]].

It also had a revival in finance. Light travels through glass at about two thirds of its speed in air, and fiber routes rarely follow straight lines; see [[Speed of Light]]. From around 2010, trading firms built microwave networks between Chicago and New York data centers that cut a few milliseconds off the round trip compared with the best fiber, an advantage worth large sums to automated traders; see [[Latency and Jitter]]. Similar links run between London and Frankfurt. Some firms have even experimented with lasers and shortwave radio across oceans, trading reliability and bandwidth for still lower delay.

The trade-offs are capacity and weather. A microwave link carries far less data than a fiber, and rain absorbs and scatters higher frequencies. Links are designed with spare signal strength so that heavy storms degrade them rather than cut them off; see [[Signal-to-Noise Ratio]].

The same band of frequencies heats food, a different use of the same physics; see [[Microwave Heating]]. Abandoned relay towers, many still standing along old routes, are reminders of the network's earlier scale.
