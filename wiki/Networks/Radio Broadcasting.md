**Radio broadcasting** sends sound from one transmitter to everyone in range who cares to listen. Earlier radio was point to point, ships and armies tapping out messages in code; see [[Telegraph]]. Broadcasting inverted that: one sender, an unknown and unlimited audience, and listeners who never transmit back.

Voice and music needed a way to impress sound on a radio wave. **Amplitude modulation** varies the strength of a steady carrier wave in step with the sound. Experimenters were sending voice this way by the early 1900s, and a 1906 Christmas Eve transmission by Reginald Fessenden is often cited as the first broadcast, though the evidence is thin. Regular commercial broadcasting began around 1920; a Pittsburgh station's report of that year's US presidential election returns is the usual landmark. Within a decade radios sat in tens of millions of homes, and the cheap transistor radio of the 1950s made them portable; see [[Transistor]].

AM's weakness is noise. Lightning, motors and power lines all produce bursts that change a wave's strength, so they come through as crackle; see [[Lightning]] and [[Signal-to-Noise Ratio]]. Edwin Armstrong patented **frequency modulation** in 1933, which varies the carrier's frequency instead and ignores most amplitude noise. FM sounds far cleaner but uses a wider channel, so it was placed at much higher frequencies, which travel only to the horizon; see [[Radio Spectrum]] and [[Radio Propagation]]. AM signals, by contrast, bounce off the upper atmosphere at night and can be heard hundreds of miles away. Stereo FM later added a second channel above the audible range, a neat piece of [[Multiplexing]].

Broadcasting shaped politics and culture. Leaders could address a whole nation at once, as Franklin Roosevelt did in the fireside chats from 1933, and propagandists used the same reach; in 1994 a Rwandan station incited genocide on air. A 1938 radio drama about a Martian invasion was later said to have caused mass panic, an account historians now consider greatly exaggerated.

Because spectrum is limited, governments license stations and attach obligations to them. Unlicensed **pirate radio** challenged that, most famously from ships anchored off Britain in the 1960s that played the pop music state radio ignored. Licensed hobbyists, in contrast, transmit but may not broadcast; see [[Amateur Radio]].

Radio has outlasted predictions of its death from television and then the internet. A single transmitter reaches any number of cheap receivers without network congestion, which is why emergency alerts still rely on it and why it remains a lifeline when power and phone networks fail. Digital radio standards and online streaming now carry many stations, though streaming sends a separate copy to each listener rather than one signal to all; see [[Adaptive Bitrate Streaming]] and [[Multicast]].
