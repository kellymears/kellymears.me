**Minitel** was a French online service that put a small terminal, a screen and a fold-down keyboard, into millions of homes starting in the early 1980s. Through it, people looked up phone numbers, booked trains, checked bank balances, read news, and chatted with strangers, years before most of the world had heard of the internet.

It belonged to a family of systems called **videotex**, which delivered pages of text and simple block graphics over telephone lines to a screen. Britain's Prestel, launched in 1979, used the same idea and attracted few customers, largely because users had to buy equipment and pay subscriptions. France tried a different approach; see [[Telephone Network]] and [[Modem]].

The French state telephone administration had a problem to solve. Printing paper directories was expensive, and a large program to expand phone service was underway. Its answer was to give terminals away free and put the directory online. After trials beginning in 1980, Minitel rolled out nationally in the early 1980s, and by the 1990s several million terminals were in use.

The business model was as important as the hardware:

- **Billing through the phone bill.** Services charged by the minute, collected by the telephone operator, so nobody needed a credit card or an account with each provider.
- **Revenue sharing.** The operator passed a share of each minute's charge to the service provider, which drew thousands of independent businesses into building services.
- **Short codes.** Services were reached by dialing a number and typing a name, which became part of advertising and everyday speech.

That arrangement made Minitel a market with two sides, users and service providers, each attracted by the growth of the other; see [[Two-Sided Market]] and [[Network Effect]]. Among its most profitable services were anonymous chat lines, many of them adult, which made the system famous and somewhat notorious.

Minitel was centralized where the internet was not. Terminals were dumb, the network was run by one operator, and services had to be registered with it. Every connection passed through infrastructure the operator controlled; see [[End-to-End Principle]]. That made it reliable and easy to use, and also limited what could be built on it, since nobody could deploy a new kind of protocol.

Its success arguably slowed French adoption of the internet in the 1990s, because many households already had a working online service; see [[Path Dependence]]. Use declined as the web spread, and the service was switched off in 2012.

Minitel is often cited as evidence that the hard parts of going online were social and economic, not technical. Free devices, simple billing, and a reason to log in did what better technology elsewhere had not; see [[Bulletin Board System]] and [[World Wide Web]].
