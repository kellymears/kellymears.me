**Amateur radio**, or ham radio, is the licensed use of reserved radio frequencies for non-commercial communication, experiment, and public service. Around three million people worldwide hold licenses. Unlike almost any other spectrum user, they may build and modify their own transmitters, as long as they stay within their bands and power limits; see [[Radio Spectrum]].

The rules reflect the bargain. Operators pass an exam on radio theory, regulations, and safety, and receive a **call sign** that identifies every transmission. In exchange for free access to valuable spectrum, they may not earn money from it, broadcast to the public, or hide what they send: encryption is generally forbidden, so anyone can listen.

Amateurs earned their place by making a discovery. When the US Radio Act of 1912 began licensing transmitters, hobbyists were pushed to wavelengths shorter than 200 meters, then thought useless for distance. In 1921 and 1923 amateurs spanned the Atlantic on those wavelengths, showing that shortwave signals bounce between the ground and the charged upper atmosphere and can circle the globe. Commercial and military shortwave followed.

That propagation depends on the sun. Solar activity, which rises and falls over an eleven-year cycle, charges the upper atmosphere and opens long-distance paths, while solar storms can black them out entirely; see [[Aurora]].

What hams do:

- **Contacts.** Voice, Morse code, and digital conversations with stations around the world, often logged and confirmed by postcard; see [[Telegraph]]. US licenses stopped requiring Morse code in 2007, but it remains popular because it gets through when little else does.
- **Weak-signal digital modes.** FT8, released in 2017 by the Nobel-winning astrophysicist Joe Taylor and colleagues, decodes signals too faint to hear, using strong error correction; see [[Error-Correcting Code]]. It quickly became the most used mode on the air.
- **Space.** Amateurs have launched over a hundred small satellites since 1961, bounce signals off the moon, and arrange scheduled school contacts with astronauts on the International Space Station.
- **Building.** Many make their own equipment, especially antennas; see [[Antenna]].

Amateurs also pioneered data networking by radio. **Packet radio**, developed in the late 1970s and 1980s, adapted networking ideas to radio links, with stations relaying messages for each other; see [[Packet Switching]] and [[Mesh Network]].

The service's public justification is emergencies. When storms destroy power, phone, and cellular networks, a battery-powered radio and a wire antenna still work; see [[Cellular Network]]. After Hurricane Katrina in 2005 and Hurricane Maria in 2017, volunteer operators passed messages for hospitals, shelters, and families, and organized amateur emergency groups train with officials for exactly that.

The hobby's culture, of tinkering, exchanging technical knowledge freely, and contacting strangers far away, overlapped heavily with early computing; see [[Bulletin Board System]].
