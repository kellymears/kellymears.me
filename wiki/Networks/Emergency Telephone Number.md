An **emergency telephone number** is a short code that connects a caller to emergency services from any phone, free of charge. The best known are 999 in the United Kingdom, 911 across North America and 112 throughout the European Union and many other countries.

London introduced the first such number, 999, in 1937, after a deadly house fire in which neighbors couldn't get through to the exchange in time. Calls to 999 triggered a light and a loud buzzer at the switchboard so operators would answer them first; see [[Telephone Network]]. The United States adopted 911 in 1968, with the first call placed in Haleyville, Alabama. The European Union standardized 112 in 1991, and mobile phone standards made it work on almost every handset, so travelers can usually reach help without knowing local numbers.

Calls go to a public-safety answering point, a call center where dispatchers take details and send help. The staffing problem is classic queueing: call volume is unpredictable, spikes during disasters, and every second of waiting matters; see [[Queueing Theory]].

Finding the caller has been the hard part. On a landline, the number traced to a fixed address, and **enhanced 911** delivered both automatically to the dispatcher; see [[Phone Number]]. Mobile phones broke that. Early systems located callers only to the nearest cell tower, sometimes kilometers away; see [[Cellular Network]]. Newer methods have the handset itself send a precise position from satellite positioning and Wi-Fi, often by a silent text message during the call. The technique, developed in the UK in the 2010s, has since spread widely; see [[Global Positioning System]] and [[SMS]].

Internet calling added more problems. A phone number on an internet line can be used anywhere, so the registered address may be wrong, and early services sometimes couldn't reach emergency centers at all; see [[Voice over IP]]. As copper lines are retired, emergency calling is among the hardest services to migrate. Some phones can now reach emergency services by satellite where there's no cell coverage; see [[Satellite Internet]].

The system is also abused. Prank and accidental calls consume a large share of traffic, and false reports meant to send armed police to a victim's home have caused deaths; see [[Swatting]]. Spoofed caller information makes such calls harder to trace; see [[Caller ID Authentication]]. Floods of automated calls have occasionally been used to overwhelm call centers; see [[Denial-of-Service Attack]].

Design choices matter for people under stress. A short number that is easy to remember and dial in the dark helps, and some countries still have several different numbers for different services, which can slow callers down. Witnesses who assume someone else has called are a separate problem; see [[Bystander Effect]].
