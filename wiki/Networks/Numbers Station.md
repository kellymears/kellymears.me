A **numbers station** is a shortwave radio transmission that reads out groups of numbers, letters or Morse code, usually in a flat or synthetic voice, often introduced by a few bars of music or a repeated tune. Nobody announces who runs them. The broadcasts are widely understood to be one-way messages from intelligence services to agents in other countries.

The method fits the job well. Shortwave signals bounce off the upper atmosphere and can be heard thousands of kilometers away; see [[Radio Propagation]] and [[Amateur Radio]]. Anyone can listen with an inexpensive receiver, which is the point: an agent needs nothing more incriminating than an ordinary radio, and because listening emits nothing, there's no way to tell who is tuned in or where. Compare that with sending a message to an agent, which reveals that the agent exists and where they are; see [[Traffic Analysis]].

The numbers are thought to be enciphered with a [[One-Time Pad]], a sheet of random digits shared in advance and used once. Done properly, this cipher is mathematically unbreakable, unlike machine ciphers that were broken in wartime; see [[Enigma Machine]]. Stations also tend to broadcast on fixed schedules whether or not there's a real message, sending dummy traffic so that a burst of activity reveals nothing.

Governments almost never confirm any of this, but court cases have. In a 2001 US trial of a Cuban spy network, evidence showed that agents had received instructions through shortwave numbers broadcasts and decoded them with one-time pads. A few stations have been linked to particular agencies by direction-finding and leaks. One famous British-attributed station, which opened each transmission with a few bars of an English folk song, went silent in 2008.

Some stations defy explanation. A Russian station nicknamed **The Buzzer** has transmitted a short, monotonous buzz almost continuously since at least the 1980s, broken only rarely by a voice reading out words or names. Theories range from a military channel kept open to deter interference, to a test signal, to something stranger; see [[Conspiracy Theory]].

Hobbyists have catalogued numbers stations for decades, assigning them codes by language and format and logging their schedules. A 1997 four-disc collection of recordings, The Conet Project, brought their eerie sound to a wider audience and has been sampled by musicians and filmmakers ever since.

Many Cold War stations fell silent as the internet offered new ways to hide messages, but some continue. Their survival suggests the old logic still holds. A broadcast that anyone can hear but only the intended reader can decode, sent to a receiver that gives nothing away, is hard to improve on; see [[Radio Broadcasting]] and [[Information Theory]].
