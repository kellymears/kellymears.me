A **two-way radio** both transmits and receives, unlike a broadcast receiver. Most share a single frequency among everyone on a channel, and that shapes how they're used: only one person can transmit at a time, so each holds a button to talk and releases it to listen. This is **push-to-talk**, and a channel that carries one direction at a time is **half duplex**; a telephone, where both people can speak at once, is full duplex.

Radio telephony grew from wireless telegraphy in the early twentieth century. Police departments adopted radio in the late 1920s and 1930s, starting with one-way dispatch to patrol cars and moving to two-way systems. During World War II, Motorola built backpack radios for the US Army, nicknamed walkie-talkies, and a handheld set known as the handie-talkie; the walkie-talkie name came to cover handheld radios generally. Several inventors are credited with early portable designs.

Sharing a channel requires discipline. Two people transmitting at once garble each other, and neither may know it happened. Radio users developed procedure words to manage turns: "over" to hand off, "roger" to confirm receipt, call signs to say who is speaking and to whom. Pilots and controllers still work this way, and a transmit button stuck down can block a whole frequency. Listening before transmitting is the human version of what network protocols later automated. The packet radio network built in Hawaii around 1970 faced the same problem of shared airwaves.

Range is limited by power, terrain, and antenna height. **Repeaters** placed on hills or towers receive on one frequency and retransmit on another, extending coverage across a city. **Trunked** systems assign channels from a shared pool to groups as they talk, letting many agencies share fewer frequencies.

Two-way radio spread to the public. Citizens band radio, created in the United States in 1958, became a craze in the 1970s, especially among truck drivers. Licensed hobbyists experiment across many bands. Cheap license-free handheld radios came later, along with push-to-talk services over cellular networks.

Interoperability is a recurring problem in emergencies. Agencies that buy different systems on different frequencies can't talk to each other, and reviews of the September 11 attacks in 2001 documented communication failures between responders. Modern public safety networks adopt shared standards and digital encryption, which in turn has raised questions about public access to police communications.

Radio remains essential where phone networks fail or don't reach: at sea, in aviation, in wilderness rescue, and during disasters. Its immediacy is the appeal: press, speak, and everyone on the channel hears at once.
