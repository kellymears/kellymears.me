**Instant messaging** is sending short text messages that arrive at once, between people who are usually both online, in a conversation that unfolds in real time. Email is closer to letters; instant messaging is closer to talking.

Its roots are in shared computers. Users of time-sharing systems in the 1960s and 1970s could send a line of text to another logged-in user's terminal, and Unix gained programs for a live split-screen chat; see [[Time-Sharing]] and [[Unix]]. MIT's Zephyr system, from 1987, delivered messages across a campus network. Group conversation in channels arrived with the 1988 chat system that became a staple of early online communities; see [[Internet Relay Chat]].

Consumer messaging took off in the late 1990s. ICQ, launched in 1996 by an Israeli startup, introduced the **buddy list**, which showed which of a person's contacts were online. AOL bought it in 1998 and had already launched AOL Instant Messenger, followed by Yahoo and Microsoft with their own services. The idea of **presence**, knowing who is available and posting away messages, became as important as the messages themselves.

The services didn't connect to each other. When Microsoft's messenger tried to let its users talk to AOL's in 1999, AOL repeatedly changed its protocol to lock them out. Because a messaging service is worth more the more of one's friends use it, each company had reason to guard its user base; see [[Network Effect]]. Jabber, started in 1999 by Jeremie Miller, offered an open alternative in which anyone could run a server and servers exchanged messages with each other, much like email; see [[Federation]]. It was standardized by the IETF as **XMPP** in 2004, and Google Talk used it, though Google later withdrew its support for connecting with outside servers.

Phones moved messaging off the desktop. Text messages had a cost per message and a length limit; see [[SMS]]. BlackBerry Messenger, WhatsApp in 2009, and Apple's iMessage in 2011 sent messages over data connections instead, letting people avoid carrier charges and adding photos, groups, and read receipts. Delivering to a sleeping phone required operating-system notification services; see [[Push notification]].

Messaging apps became the main channel for both private conversation and organizing. WhatsApp adopted end-to-end encryption by default in 2016, using the protocol developed for Signal, so the provider can't read messages; see [[End-to-End Encryption]]. That made messaging central to debates over law enforcement access. Spam and scams followed users into chat; see [[Email Spam]].

Interoperability has returned as a legal question. The European Union's Digital Markets Act requires the largest messaging services to open themselves to connection with rivals. Newer open protocols, such as Matrix, pursue federated messaging, while the old open services persist in smaller communities; see [[Mailing List]].
