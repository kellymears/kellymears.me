A **pneumatic tube** system moves sealed cylinders, called carriers or capsules, through pipes using air pressure: compressed air pushes from behind, or a vacuum pulls from ahead. A message, document, or small object travels between stations far faster than a person could carry it.

The first practical urban systems served the telegraph. Telegraph lines ended at central offices, and messages still had to cross the city to and from them by foot; see [[Telegraph]]. In 1853, Josiah Latimer Clark installed a tube in London linking the stock exchange with the telegraph company's central station, a few hundred yards away. Tubes became the local distribution layer for long-distance telegraphy, a solution to what would now be called the last stretch of the network; see [[Last Mile]].

Major cities built public pneumatic post networks. Paris opened its system in 1866, and it grew to hundreds of kilometers of tubes under the streets; Parisians sent express letters on special forms, delivered across the city within hours, until the service closed in 1984. Berlin, Vienna, and Prague built similar networks, and Prague's ran into the 21st century, until flooding damaged it in 2002. New York and other American cities carried mail between post offices in tubes from 1897 until the 1950s.

The networks worked much like later data networks. Messages were sealed into standard containers, routed through hub stations where operators redirected them to the next segment, and queued during busy periods; see [[Packet Switching]]. Capacity was limited by how many carriers a line could hold at once and how fast they traveled, the same trade-off between throughput and delay that governs any channel; see [[Bandwidth]] and [[Latency and Jitter]].

Telephones, cars, and faxes made city pneumatic posts obsolete, since messages no longer needed a physical carrier; see [[Fax]] and [[Telephone Network]]. But tubes remain common where physical objects must move quickly within one building. Hospitals send blood samples to laboratories and medications to wards, often with software routing carriers across many stations and controlling speed to avoid damaging samples. Banks used them for drive-through tellers, and stores used them to move cash to a central office.

The tubes have a lasting place in imagination. Futurists repeatedly proposed scaling them up to carry people, and nineteenth-century atmospheric railways in Britain and Ireland briefly ran trains on air pressure before leaking seals defeated them. Science fiction set in the future often featured tube mail, and the internet itself was once mocked, by a United States senator in 2006, as "a series of tubes."

Like carrier pigeons and couriers carrying disks, pneumatic posts are a reminder that a network need not be electrical; see [[Pigeon Post]] and [[Sneakernet]].
