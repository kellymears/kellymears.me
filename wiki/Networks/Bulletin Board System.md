A **bulletin board system**, or BBS, was a computer that others could dial into with a modem to read and post messages, swap files, and play games. From the late 1970s to the mid-1990s, bulletin boards were how most people outside universities and companies went online.

The first, CBBS, was written by Ward Christensen and Randy Suess in Chicago in early 1978, during a blizzard that kept them indoors. The recipe stayed simple: a personal computer, a modem, a phone line, and software that answered calls; see [[Modem]]. The person who ran it was the **sysop**, short for system operator, usually a hobbyist working from a spare bedroom.

The constraints shaped everything:

- **One caller at a time.** Most boards had a single phone line, so users took turns, a busy signal meant trying again later, and time limits kept anyone from hogging the line.
- **Local calls.** Long-distance calls were billed by the minute, so callers stuck to boards in their own area code. Bulletin boards became local communities, and users often met in person; see [[Telephone Network]].
- **Text screens.** Interfaces were menus of text, decorated with color and block-character art that grew into its own art scene; see [[ASCII Art]].

What happened there:

- **Message boards** organized by topic, and private mail between users.
- **File libraries**: shareware, utilities, images, and, on some boards, pirated software, which connected bulletin boards to the cracking groups that birthed the [[Demoscene]].
- **Door games**, external programs the board launched, often turn-based and played one session a day, such as the fantasy game Legend of the Red Dragon or the space-trading game TradeWars.

Boards linked into wider networks without the internet. **FidoNet**, started by Tom Jennings in 1984, had each board phone the next in the middle of the night, when calls were cheap, and pass along a day's batch of mail and forum posts. Messages hopped from board to board across continents over days; see [[Packet Switching]]. At its peak in the mid-1990s it had tens of thousands of nodes, a volunteer-run precursor to federated networks; see [[Federation]].

The sysop owned the board and set its rules, deciding who got access and what could be said, a model of small-scale moderation that later platforms struggled to scale. Some boards were general hangouts; others gathered people around a hobby, an identity, or an underground scene, including phone hacking; see [[Internet Relay Chat]].

Commercial online services and then the web emptied the boards in the mid-1990s, as internet access arrived with the whole world attached; see [[Eternal September]]. A few hundred still run, now reached over the internet rather than by phone, kept alive for nostalgia and for door games no one else hosts.
