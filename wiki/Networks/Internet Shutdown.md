An **internet shutdown** is a deliberate cut to internet access, usually ordered by a government and carried out by the network operators it controls or regulates. Shutdowns range from blocking a few social platforms, to cutting mobile data in one region, to taking an entire country offline. They're the bluntest form of network control; see [[Internet Censorship]].

The best-known early case came in Egypt in January 2011. During mass protests, the government ordered the country's providers to disconnect, and within minutes the routes announcing how to reach Egyptian networks vanished from the global routing system, making the country unreachable for about five days; see [[Border Gateway Protocol]] and [[Internet Service Provider]]. The protests continued, and the shutdown arguably drove more people into the streets.

Since then, shutdowns have become routine in many places. Digital rights groups tracking them, notably the KeepItOn coalition, have counted hundreds per year, with India consistently recording the most, many limited to particular districts. Kashmir's restrictions beginning in August 2019 lasted for months, with fast mobile data blocked for well over a year. Iran cut most of its international connectivity for about a week during protests in November 2019. Several countries, including Iraq, Syria, and Algeria, have shut down networks during national school exams to prevent cheating.

Methods vary in precision. A government can order providers to withdraw routes, switch off mobile towers, or block specific services while leaving the rest working; see [[Cellular Network]]. **Throttling**, slowing connections until they're unusable without cutting them, is harder to detect and to prove; see [[Quality of Service]]. Countries with few international connections, all running through state-controlled gateways, can be disconnected most easily; see [[Internet Exchange Point]]. Researchers detect shutdowns by watching for routes disappearing, traffic to large services dropping, and failed measurements from volunteers' devices.

The costs reach far beyond the stated target. Payments, hospitals, schools, and businesses rely on the same networks as protesters, and estimates of economic losses run to billions of dollars a year worldwide. Shutdowns also hide what happens during them, which human rights groups argue enables abuses to go unrecorded.

People work around them in limited ways. Encrypted tunnels defeat targeted blocks but not a total cutoff; see [[Virtual Private Network]]. Phone-to-phone apps can relay messages over short-range radio; see [[Mesh Network]]. Satellite terminals smuggled into a country bypass local infrastructure entirely; see [[Satellite Internet]]. Where nothing else works, files travel on physical media; see [[Sneakernet]].

The ability to shut down a network reflects its architecture. An internet designed to route around failure can still be cut wherever access concentrates into a few chokepoints; see [[Last Mile]].
