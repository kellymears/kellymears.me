**Near-field communication** (NFC) is a radio technology for exchanging small amounts of data over a few centimeters, by bringing two devices nearly into contact. It's behind contactless card payments, phone wallets, transit cards, building access badges, and tapping a phone on a tag to open a link.

It grew out of **RFID** (radio-frequency identification), which tags objects with chips readable by radio: inventory, library books, passports, pet microchips, and toll transponders. RFID covers a range of frequencies and distances, from centimeters to many meters. NFC is a standardized subset at 13.56 megahertz, designed for deliberate, short-range taps, and allows phones to act as reader, tag, or both.

The physics is unusual. The "near field" is the region close to an antenna, within a small fraction of a wavelength, where the effect is mainly magnetic coupling rather than a radio wave traveling outward; see [[Electromagnetic Induction]] and [[Magnetism]]. A reader's coil creates an alternating magnetic field. A tag's coil, brought close, picks up enough energy from it to power its chip, so **passive tags need no battery** and can be thin as a sticker. The tag answers by varying how much energy it draws, which the reader detects. This is why range is so short: the field weakens very quickly with distance; see [[Inverse-Square Law]].

That short range is the core security idea. Requiring a deliberate tap is a strong signal of intent, and an attacker has to get very close to read or talk to a card. Payment systems build more on top:

- **Tokenization.** A phone wallet doesn't transmit the real card number but a device-specific token; see [[Public-Key Cryptography]].
- **One-time codes.** Each transaction includes a cryptogram generated by the card's chip, so recorded data can't be replayed.
- **Limits.** Contactless payments without a PIN are capped at small amounts in many countries.

Attacks still exist. **Relay attacks** use two devices to extend the range: one near the victim's card, another at a payment terminal, passing messages between them. Some car keyless-entry systems, which use similar short-range radio, have been stolen this way, and timing checks that measure how long replies take are a defense; see [[Man-in-the-Middle Attack]]. Simple RFID badges and older transit cards often had weak or no encryption and were cloned easily, their security resting on attackers not knowing how they worked.

NFC also simplifies pairing other wireless links: tapping two devices can exchange the details needed to set up a [[Bluetooth]] or [[Wi-Fi]] connection, since being physically close is a reasonable sign the user intends it; see [[Zero Trust Network]].

Phones' NFC chips connect to a secure element that holds payment keys apart from the main operating system; see [[Trusted Platform Module]] and [[Passkey]].
