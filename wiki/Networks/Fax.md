**A fax** sends an image of a page over a telephone line. The sending machine scans the page line by line, turns light and dark into signals, and the receiving machine prints a copy. The name is short for facsimile, an exact copy.

The idea is older than the telephone. The Scottish inventor Alexander Bain patented a design in 1843 that used synchronized pendulums to scan and reproduce an image over a telegraph wire; see [[Telegraph]]. An Italian inventor's pantelegraph carried a commercial service between Paris and Lyon in the 1860s. Newspapers sent photographs by wire from the 1920s. Office fax machines arrived in the 1960s, but they were slow, and machines from different makers couldn't talk to each other.

A 1980 international standard, known as Group 3, fixed that. A Group 3 machine scans about 200 dots per inch across, sends through a built-in [[Modem]], and compresses each line by coding the lengths of runs of white and black with short codes for common lengths; see [[Data Compression]]. A typical page took under a minute. Because any machine could reach any other over the ordinary [[Telephone Network]], the value of owning one grew with every new one sold; see [[Network Effect]]. By the late 1980s it was everywhere.

Fax had properties email still lacks in some eyes:

- **It's a document.** A signed page arrives as an image of the signature, which courts and offices were used to.
- **It's direct.** The call goes from one machine to another, with a confirmation report, and with no server holding a copy.
- **It needs no setup.** A phone number and a machine are enough.

Japan kept faxing longest among rich countries, partly because handwritten Japanese was easier to fax than to type on early computers; see [[Character Encoding]]. In 2020, Japanese health centers reported COVID-19 cases to the government partly by fax, which drew attention. Health care elsewhere is similar: in 2018 England's health secretary banned the National Health Service from buying new fax machines and set a deadline to phase them out.

Fax was also abused. Advertisers sent junk faxes that used up the recipient's paper and ink, and the United States banned unsolicited fax advertising in 1991; see [[Email Spam]].

Fax depends on precise audio tones, so it breaks over networks built for speech. Voice compression and jitter distort the signal, and calls fail; a separate protocol was standardized to carry fax data over packet networks instead; see [[Voice over IP]].

Many faxes now never touch paper, sent from software to an email inbox, keeping the format alive mainly for compatibility; see [[Lindy Effect]].
