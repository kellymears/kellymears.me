An **internet service provider**, or ISP, connects customers to the internet. It runs the physical link to a home or business, assigns it addresses, routes its traffic onward, and usually provides supporting services such as name lookup.

For most of the internet's early history there were no commercial providers. The research networks that formed its backbone restricted use to research and education. Commercial dial-up providers began appearing around 1989, and the restrictions were relaxed through the early 1990s until the government-funded backbone was retired in 1995, leaving the network to private carriers. Online services such as CompuServe and America Online, which had run their own closed networks, turned into ISPs as the web made the open internet what customers wanted; see [[ARPANET]] and [[World Wide Web]].

Access technology has changed while the role stayed the same:

- **Dial-up** used ordinary phone lines and a modem, tying up the household's line while connected; see [[Modem]].
- **DSL** and **cable** reused phone and television wiring for always-on broadband.
- **Fiber** runs light to the premises and offers far more capacity; see [[Optical Fiber]].
- **Wireless** options include mobile networks, fixed wireless towers, and satellites; see [[Satellite Internet]]. Low-orbit satellite constellations have made service possible in places no cable will ever reach.

The expensive part is the final connection to each building, which is why many areas have only one or two choices; see [[Last Mile]].

Behind the access network, ISPs connect to each other. Smaller providers buy transit from larger ones, and networks of similar size exchange traffic directly, often at shared facilities. Routes between them are announced with a protocol that depends heavily on trust; see [[Internet Exchange Point]] and [[Border Gateway Protocol]].

Running out of IPv4 addresses changed what customers receive. Many providers now place several customers behind one shared public address, a carrier-grade form of address translation that complicates hosting and peer-to-peer connections; see [[Network Address Translation]] and [[IPv6]].

The provider's position makes it a chokepoint. It can see where traffic goes, even when contents are encrypted, and it can slow, block, or favor particular services. Debates over whether it may treat traffic unequally are the subject of net neutrality rules; see [[Net Neutrality]]. Governments use ISPs to enforce censorship, and copyright holders ask them to warn or disconnect customers accused of infringement; see [[Internet Censorship]].

Where there is little competition, complaints about price, data caps, and service quality recur. Some towns have built municipal networks in response, and some states have passed laws restricting them, a pattern that critics attribute to industry influence over regulators; see [[Regulatory Capture]].
