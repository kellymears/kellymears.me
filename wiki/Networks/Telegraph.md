The **telegraph** was the first technology to send messages faster than a person could carry them over long distances, and the first communications network in the modern sense. Its arrival separated information from transport.

The first telegraphs were optical. In the 1790s the Chappe brothers built a chain of towers across France, each with movable wooden arms whose positions encoded letters and words. Operators read the next tower through a telescope and copied the signal, relaying messages hundreds of miles in hours. It worked only in daylight and clear weather, and served the state, not the public.

The **electric telegraph** used current in a wire to move an electromagnet at the far end; see [[Magnetism]]. William Cooke and Charles Wheatstone built a working system for British railways in 1837. In the United States, Samuel Morse and Alfred Vail opened a line between Washington and Baltimore in 1844 with the message "What hath God wrought."

**Morse code** encoded each letter as a pattern of short and long pulses. Vail reportedly assigned the shortest patterns to the most common letters by counting the type in a printer's cases, so E is a single dot. That is the principle of efficient variable-length coding formalized a century later; see [[Information Theory]]. Skilled operators decoded by ear, and could recognize each other by their rhythm, called a "fist."

Networks grew quickly. A transatlantic cable worked briefly in 1858 and reliably from 1866; see [[Submarine Cable]]. Messages crossed the network by **store and forward**: each office received a message, wrote it down, and sent it on along the next line, the same pattern that packet networks later automated; see [[Packet Switching]].

The effects were broad:

- **News and markets.** Wire services like Reuters sold news by telegraph, and prices in distant markets converged.
- **Time.** Railways and observatories used telegraph time signals to synchronize clocks, which led to standard time zones; see [[Time Zone]].
- **Brevity.** Companies charged by the word, so senders wrote in clipped "telegraphese," and businesses used codebooks that replaced common phrases with single words; see [[Data Compression]].
- **Encoding.** Printing telegraphs needed codes machines could handle, and the five-bit Baudot code led eventually to ASCII; see [[Character Encoding]].

Tom Standage's 1998 book *The Victorian Internet* argued that the telegraph went through the same arc as the internet: utopian predictions of world peace, then crime, scams, and information overload; see [[Network Effect]].

Radio extended telegraphy to ships around 1900, and the telephone replaced it for most uses; see [[Telephone Network]]. Western Union stopped sending telegrams in 2006. Morse code survives in amateur radio and aviation beacons.
