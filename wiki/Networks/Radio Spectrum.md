The **radio spectrum** is the range of electromagnetic frequencies used for wireless communication, from a few kilohertz up to around 300 gigahertz; see [[Electromagnetic Spectrum]]. Broadcasting, phones, Wi-Fi, satellites, radar, and aviation all share it, and because two transmitters on the same frequency in the same place interfere, it is one of the most tightly managed resources there is.

Physics sets what each part of the spectrum is good for:

- **Low frequencies** travel far, bend around hills, and pass through walls, but a narrow band carries little data. AM radio and submarine communication live here.
- **Middle frequencies**, roughly 600 megahertz to 6 gigahertz, balance range and capacity, and are the most contested: mobile phones, Wi-Fi, GPS, and television all sit in this range; see [[Cellular Network]] and [[Global Positioning System]].
- **High frequencies**, the millimeter waves, offer wide channels and huge capacity but reach short distances and are blocked by walls, leaves, and even rain.

Capacity follows from width: a wider slice of spectrum can carry more bits per second, the relationship information theory makes precise; see [[Information Theory]] and [[Bandwidth]].

Regulation began after the Titanic sank in 1912, when amateur chatter and unattended radio rooms were blamed for confusion over distress calls. The US Radio Act that year required licenses, and pushed amateurs to the "useless" short wavelengths, which turned out to cross oceans; see [[Amateur Radio]]. Today the International Telecommunication Union coordinates allocations between countries, and national regulators such as the FCC assign bands within them.

Who gets a band was long decided by hearings. In 1959 the economist Ronald Coase argued that licenses should simply be sold, an idea ridiculed at the time and adopted by the FCC in 1994. Auctions have since raised hundreds of billions of dollars; one 2021 sale of mid-band frequencies for 5G brought in over $80 billion.

The alternative is **unlicensed spectrum**, shared by anyone following power limits. The 2.4 gigahertz band was left open largely because microwave ovens leak energy there, making it unattractive to licensees; see [[Microwave Heating]]. That leftover band became home to Wi-Fi and Bluetooth, one of regulation's most productive accidents; see [[Wi-Fi]] and [[Bluetooth]]. Sharing works through politeness rules and techniques that tolerate interference; see [[Spread Spectrum]]. Treating airwaves as a commons, rather than property, has its own risks; see [[Tragedy of the Commons]].

Neighbors in the spectrum can collide. In 2022 the US rollout of new 5G frequencies was delayed near airports over fears of interference with aircraft altimeters in an adjacent band, and meteorologists have warned that transmissions near 24 gigahertz could blur satellite readings of water vapor.

Squeezing more from the same frequencies is a constant project: smaller cells reuse them more often, and smarter antennas aim energy where it is needed; see [[Antenna]].
