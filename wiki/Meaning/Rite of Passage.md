A **rite of passage** is a ritual that marks a person's move from one social status to another: child to adult, single to married, student to graduate, recruit to soldier, living to dead. The French ethnographer Arnold van Gennep compared such rituals across many cultures in *Les Rites de passage* (1909) and found the same three-part structure:

1. **Separation.** The person is removed from their old status, often physically, through a journey, seclusion, or a change of clothes or hair.
2. **Transition.** An in-between period in which the person belongs to neither the old status nor the new one. Van Gennep called it the **liminal** phase, from the Latin for threshold.
3. **Incorporation.** The person returns and is received into the new status, often with a feast, a new name, or new rights.

In the 1960s the anthropologist Victor Turner, working among the Ndembu of Zambia, developed the middle phase. Liminal people are "betwixt and between": stripped of rank, often treated as equals, subjected to ordeals, and given secret knowledge. Turner called the intense bond that forms among people going through it together **communitas**. Similar bonds form at summer camps, in boot camps, and among university freshmen.

Initiations are often deliberately hard. A classic 1959 experiment by Elliot Aronson and Judson Mills found that people who went through an embarrassing initiation to join a dull discussion group rated it more highly than people who had an easy entry; see [[Effort justification]]. Painful or expensive rituals also serve as proof of commitment, since only the committed would pay that price; see [[Costly signaling]]. Taken too far, the same dynamic produces hazing.

Rites of passage set apart a special space and time with their own rules, much as games do; see [[Magic Circle]]. They also give communities a way to face death and change together, which some psychologists argue is part of how cultures manage the awareness of mortality; see [[Terror Management Theory]].

Industrial societies have thinned many of these rituals. Life transitions are often gradual and private, and some argue that people invent their own markers, such as a first apartment, a tattoo, or a long journey. Some rituals that feel ancient are recent creations; see [[Invented Tradition]]. Joseph Campbell based the structure of the hero's journey, separation, initiation, and return, partly on van Gennep's scheme; see [[Hero's Journey]].

**Liminal space** has also become an internet aesthetic since around 2019: photos of empty hallways, closed malls, and deserted pools at night. These are transitional places seen when no one is passing through, and they produce an uneasy nostalgia; see [[Nostalgia]].
