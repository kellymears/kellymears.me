**Cultural capital** is the stock of knowledge, skills, tastes, and credentials that helps people move through a society and signals where they belong in it. Knowing which fork to use, how to talk to a professor, which books to have read, or how to describe a wine are all forms of it. Like money, it can be accumulated, passed down, and converted into other advantages.

The idea comes from the French sociologist Pierre Bourdieu, who developed it in the 1960s and 1970s, partly with the sociologist Jean-Claude Passeron. Bourdieu was trying to explain why children from educated families did better at school even when schools were free and examinations were formally fair. The answer was that schools rewarded a way of speaking, reading, and behaving that some children absorbed at home and others had to learn from scratch.

Bourdieu distinguished three forms:

- **Embodied** capital lives in a person: accent, manners, vocabulary, ease in certain settings. It takes years to acquire and can't be bought quickly.
- **Objectified** capital lives in things: books, paintings, instruments, and the knowledge needed to appreciate them.
- **Institutionalized** capital lives in credentials: degrees and qualifications that certify cultural competence.

The related term **habitus** describes the ingrained dispositions people absorb from their upbringing, the unspoken sense of how to act and what to like. Habitus makes cultural capital feel natural to those who have it, as if their tastes were simply good taste.

Bourdieu's 1979 book Distinction, based on large surveys in France, argued that taste is a social weapon. Preferences in music, food, and art map closely onto class, and expressing the right preferences marks one as belonging. Displays of expensive goods do similar work through money; see [[Veblen Good]]. Language is a central case: speaking the prestigious variety is itself an asset; see [[Linguistic Prescriptivism]] and [[Diglossia]].

Cultural capital interacts with other resources. Personal networks are a separate kind of capital that cultural capital helps build; see [[Social Capital]]. Advantage compounds, as small early differences in preparation turn into large gaps; see [[Matthew Effect]]. People moving between class settings often adjust their speech and manners as they go; see [[Code-Switching]]. Feeling out of place in elite institutions despite having earned a place there is a common experience among first-generation students; see [[Impostor Syndrome]].

Later research complicated the picture. In the 1990s, American sociologists found that high-status people increasingly signaled distinction through broad, omnivorous taste instead of exclusive highbrow preferences. Groups also define themselves by their cultural markers; see [[Social Identity Theory]] and [[Shibboleth]]. And some nations come to doubt the worth of their own culture compared with others; see [[Cultural Cringe]].
