An **implicature** is something a speaker communicates without literally saying it. If someone asks whether a coworker is a good programmer and the answer is "Well, they're always on time," the reply says nothing false about programming, yet the listener understands the coworker is not very good. The philosopher Paul Grice introduced the term in lectures given in 1967 and published in 1975 as "Logic and Conversation."

Grice proposed that conversation runs on a **cooperative principle**: people assume that speakers are trying to contribute usefully to the exchange. He broke this down into four maxims:

- **Quantity.** Say as much as is needed, and no more.
- **Quality.** Say only what you believe to be true and have evidence for.
- **Relation.** Be relevant.
- **Manner.** Be clear, brief, and orderly, and avoid ambiguity.

Implicatures arise when listeners reconcile what was said with the assumption that the speaker is cooperating. Grice's own example was a reference letter for a philosophy job that praised only the candidate's excellent English and regular attendance. The writer obviously knows more, so the missing praise signals that there is nothing good to say.

Some patterns are so regular that they feel like part of the words. "Some of the students passed" suggests that not all did, because a speaker who knew all passed would have said so; linguists call this a **scalar implicature**. Unlike logical entailment, an implicature can be canceled: "some passed, in fact all of them" is not a contradiction. That cancellability is how linguists distinguish implied meaning from literal meaning.

Speakers also break maxims openly to create meaning. Saying "lovely weather" in a downpour violates quality so obviously that the listener infers sarcasm. Understatement, hints, and polite indirect requests work the same way; see [[Speech Act]] for how "Can you pass the salt?" becomes a request. All of it depends on modeling what the speaker knows and intends; see [[Theory of Mind]].

Implicature explains several familiar problems:

- **Misunderstandings across cultures.** How much indirectness is normal, and how much detail is polite, vary between communities; see [[Code-Switching]].
- **Text without context.** Online, irony and hints often fail because readers lack the shared background that makes them clear; see [[Context Collapse]].
- **Evasive language.** Statements that are technically true can still mislead, which is why legal and official writing aims for explicitness; see [[Plain Language]] and [[Euphemism Treadmill]].

Language models are a recent test case. They handle many conventional implicatures well, because the patterns are common in text, but can miss implications that depend on the specific situation, and their tendency to agree can interact with users' hints; see [[Large Language Model]] and [[Sycophancy]]. Users also learn that being explicit gets better results than implying; see [[Prompt Engineering]].
