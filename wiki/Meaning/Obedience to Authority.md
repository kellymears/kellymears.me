**Obedience to authority** is following the instructions of someone perceived as having legitimate power, even against one's own judgment. The psychologist Stanley Milgram studied it in experiments at Yale University beginning in 1961, the year Adolf Eichmann, an organizer of the Holocaust, went on trial claiming he had only followed orders.

Participants were told they were taking part in a study of learning. Assigned the role of teacher, they were to give an electric shock to a learner, actually an actor, for each wrong answer, increasing the voltage in steps up to 450 volts on a machine labeled with warnings. No shocks were real. As voltage rose the learner protested, complained of a heart condition, screamed, and then fell silent. When participants hesitated, the experimenter replied with scripted prods, such as "the experiment requires that you continue."

In the best-known version, 26 of 40 participants, 65 percent, continued to the maximum. Many were visibly distressed yet continued. Before the study, psychiatrists Milgram surveyed had predicted almost no one would.

Milgram ran many variations, and they matter as much as the headline:

- **Proximity.** When the teacher had to press the learner's hand onto a shock plate, full obedience fell to 30 percent.
- **Presence of authority.** When the experimenter gave orders by telephone, obedience dropped sharply.
- **Legitimacy.** In a run-down office building away from Yale, obedience fell somewhat but remained substantial.
- **Peers.** When two other teachers refused, few participants continued, the same effect of an ally seen in studies of group pressure; see [[Conformity]].

Milgram explained the results through an **agentic state**, in which people see themselves as instruments of another's will and no longer responsible; see [[Bystander Effect]]. The gradual escalation also mattered: each step was only slightly worse than the last, so stopping meant admitting the previous steps were wrong; see [[Cognitive Dissonance]] and [[Sunk Cost Fallacy]]. The study warns against explaining atrocities by the character of the people involved instead of the situation; see [[Fundamental Attribution Error]].

Later scholarship has complicated the picture. The researcher Gina Perry, examining Milgram's archives, found that experimenters sometimes departed from the script and that many participants doubted the shocks were real. Notably, the most direct order, "you have no other choice, you must go on," tended to produce refusal rather than obedience. Some psychologists now argue that participants continued less from blind obedience than from identifying with the scientific project they believed worthwhile; see [[Social Identity Theory]].

A partial replication in 2009, stopping at 150 volts for ethical reasons, found rates similar to Milgram's at that point. The experiments, which caused participants real distress, helped prompt modern rules for research ethics.

The practical lesson concerns institutions. Organizations that let people raise concerns without penalty make it easier to refuse harmful instructions; see [[Psychological Safety]].
