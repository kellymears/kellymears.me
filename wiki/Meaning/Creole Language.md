A **creole language** is a full, natural language that developed from a mixture of other languages, typically when speakers of several languages were thrown together and needed to communicate. Creoles are among the clearest cases of new languages being born in recorded history.

The process usually starts with a **pidgin**: a simplified contact language with a small vocabulary and minimal grammar, used for trade or work between groups without a common tongue. Pidgins aren't anyone's native language. Most of their words often come from one dominant language, called the *lexifier*, while grammar is pared down. Pidgins arose along trade routes, in ports, and above all on colonial plantations, where enslaved people from many language backgrounds were forced together.

A pidgin becomes a **creole** when children grow up speaking it as their first language. Children don't just learn the pidgin; they expand it into a complete language, with consistent grammar for tense, mood, and aspect, and the expressive range of any other language. Haitian Creole, Jamaican Patois, Tok Pisin in Papua New Guinea, and Papiamento in the Caribbean are examples. Tok Pisin is now an official language and used in parliament and newspapers.

A striking modern case occurred in Nicaragua. When schools for deaf children opened in the late 1970s, students who had previously used only home signs began to communicate with each other. Younger children who entered the community then turned that rough system into a full sign language, Nicaraguan Sign Language, with grammar the older students lacked. Linguists see this as evidence of children's innate capacity to build language structure.

A **lingua franca** is different: an existing language used as a common medium between speakers of other languages, such as Latin in medieval Europe, Swahili in East Africa, or English today in science, aviation, and software. A lingua franca may simplify in use, but it doesn't become a new language.

Creoles have long been stigmatized as "broken" versions of their source languages, partly because of their colonial origins. Linguists reject this: creoles are as complex and rule-governed as any language. Speakers often move between a creole and the standard language depending on setting, a form of [[Code-Switching]]. Whether a variety is called a dialect, a creole, or a language is often a matter of politics and prestige, captured in the old line that a language is a dialect with an army and a navy; see [[Shibboleth]].

Creoles connect to several ideas about language. They show grammar emerging from use, much like conventions emerging in any community; see [[Collective Action]]. They raise questions about how language shapes thought; see [[Linguistic Relativity]]. They pose challenges for translation and software localization, since many have little written standard; see [[Video Game Localization]] and [[Subtitling]]. And they show languages changing fast under pressure; see [[Semantic Change]].
