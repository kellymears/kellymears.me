**Diglossia** is a situation in which a community uses two distinct varieties of the same language for different purposes. A **high** variety serves formal writing, religion, news, and speeches, and is learned mainly at school. A **low** variety is the language of home, conversation, and everyday life, learned as a mother tongue. Everyone knows which to use where, and using the wrong one sounds absurd or offensive.

The linguist Charles Ferguson described the pattern in a 1959 article, drawing on four main cases:

- **Arabic**, where Modern Standard Arabic, close to classical Arabic, is used for writing and formal speech across the Arab world, while regional dialects, sometimes mutually hard to understand, serve daily life.
- **Swiss German**, where Standard German is used for most writing and formal settings while Swiss German dialects dominate speech, including among the educated.
- **Greek**, where an archaizing formal variety competed for over a century with the popular spoken form, a dispute settled in 1976 when the spoken form became the official standard.
- **Haiti**, where French served formal purposes and Haitian Creole was the everyday language of almost everyone; see [[Creole Language]].

The sociolinguist Joshua Fishman extended the idea in 1967 to communities using two different languages in this divided way, such as Spanish and Guaraní in Paraguay.

Diglossia tends to be stable for long periods, sometimes for centuries, but it carries tension. Because the high variety must be learned formally, it can mark education and class, and exclude those without schooling; see [[Shibboleth]] and [[Plain Language]]. Speakers may feel their own everyday language is inferior or not a real language at all; see [[Cultural Cringe]]. Writing in the low variety can be a political act, and nationalist movements have often championed it as the authentic voice of the people; see [[Imagined Community]].

Some situations resemble diglossia in milder forms. Many English speakers shift between a formal register and casual speech, or between a standard form and a regional dialect, depending on audience; see [[Code-Switching]] and [[Politeness Theory]]. Vocabulary can carry a similar split, with borrowed formal words alongside plain native ones; see [[Loanword]].

Digital communication has complicated the picture. Arabic speakers increasingly write their dialects in text messages and social media, sometimes in Latin letters with numerals for missing sounds, giving the low variety a written life it rarely had before.

Shifts between varieties also show how language connects to identity and belonging; see [[Social Identity Theory]]. When a low variety loses speakers to a dominant language altogether, diglossia can be a stage on the way to its disappearance; see [[Language Death]].
