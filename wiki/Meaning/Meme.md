A **meme** is an idea, behavior, or style that spreads from person to person within a culture. The biologist Richard Dawkins coined the word in *The Selfish Gene* (1976), shortening the Greek *mimeme*, "something imitated," to rhyme with gene. His examples were tunes, catchphrases, fashions, and ways of making pots or building arches.

Dawkins's point was that genes might not be the only things that evolve by natural selection. Anything that is copied, varies in copying, and competes for limited space could evolve the same way. Ideas compete for human attention and memory, and those better at being copied spread, whether or not they benefit the people who carry them; see [[Attention Economy]].

An academic field, memetics, tried to develop this into a science. A journal ran from 1997 to 2005 before closing. Critics argued that memes lack a clear unit, and that culture isn't copied faithfully like DNA but rebuilt by each person, who interprets and changes it. The analogy remained more useful as a metaphor than as a theory.

The internet adopted the word for a narrower sense: an image, video, phrase, or format that users copy, remix, and pass around. Early examples include the dancing baby animation of 1996 and the captioned cat photos of the mid-2000s. The rickroll, a bait-and-switch link to a 1987 pop video, spread in 2007. Most internet memes are templates: a fixed structure with a slot for new content, such as a reaction image with captions or a stock photo whose roles stand for anything; see [[Snowclone]].

The scholar Limor Shifman defined internet memes as groups of items sharing characteristics, created with awareness of one another and spread online by many users. The emphasis is on variation: a meme is not one image but the family of its variants.

Some features of meme culture:

- **Speed and decay.** Memes rise and fade within days or weeks, with irony layered on irony until outsiders can't follow; see [[Context Collapse]].
- **Group signals.** Knowing a meme marks membership, and some are coded to say one thing to insiders and another to outsiders; see [[Shibboleth]] and [[Dog Whistle]].
- **Commercial use.** Brands imitate memes, usually late, which tends to kill them.
- **Ownership.** Memes are built from copyrighted images used without permission, relying on tolerance or fair use; see [[Public Domain]].

The lawyer Mike Godwin has said he devised his famous law about Nazi comparisons in 1990 partly as an experiment in deliberately spreading an idea; see [[Godwin's Law]].

Memes overlap with older forms of folk transmission: legends, jokes, and sayings that spread and mutate without authors; see [[Urban Legend]]. Words and phrases themselves spread, compete, and shift meaning by similar processes; see [[Semantic Change]].
