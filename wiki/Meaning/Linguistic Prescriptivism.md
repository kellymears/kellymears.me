**Linguistic prescriptivism** is the effort to tell people how they ought to speak and write: which forms are correct, which words are proper, which usages are mistakes. Its counterpart, **descriptivism**, is the approach of linguistics as a science, recording how people actually use a language without ranking their choices. The two approaches answer different questions, one about what people should do and one about what they do, but they collide often.

Many of the best-known rules of English have recent, specific origins. The objection to ending a sentence with a preposition is usually traced to the poet John Dryden, who criticized it in 1672, apparently by analogy with Latin. Robert Lowth's Short Introduction to English Grammar, published in 1762, popularized many judgments about correct usage. The ban on splitting infinitives, as in "to boldly go," arose in the nineteenth century, though English writers had split them for centuries. Linguists sometimes call such rules zombie rules: widely taught, rarely followed by good writers, and impossible to kill.

Some countries created formal authorities. The Académie française, founded in 1635, is charged with guarding the French language, and the Spanish Royal Academy followed in 1713. English never had one. Its authority came instead from dictionaries, grammars, and style guides. Noah Webster's dictionary of 1828 set distinct American spellings, such as color and center, partly as an act of national independence. When a major American dictionary in 1961 included common usages without condemning them, critics attacked it fiercely for abandoning standards.

Prescriptive rules do useful work. A shared standard helps people across regions understand each other in writing, and clear conventions reduce ambiguity in law, science, and instructions; see [[Plain Language]].

But the standard is usually the speech of a particular group, and judgments of correctness often track social status more than clarity. Speakers of other dialects may have to switch between varieties depending on the setting; see [[Code-Switching]] and [[Standard language]]. A usage can mark someone as an outsider instantly; see [[Shibboleth]]. Command of the prestigious form is a resource that education and class distribute unevenly; see [[Cultural Capital]].

Language changes regardless of rules. Words shift meaning over generations, and some former errors become standard; see [[Semantic Change]] and [[Folk Etymology]]. The singular "they," condemned by many grammarians, has been used since the fourteenth century and is now widely accepted. Polite replacements for sensitive words keep wearing out and being renewed; see [[Euphemism Treadmill]]. New forms that start as mistakes can turn into accepted variants; see [[Eggcorn]].

Languages designed from scratch show what pure prescription looks like, since every rule is decided in advance; see [[Constructed Language]].
