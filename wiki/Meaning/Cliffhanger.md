A **cliffhanger** ends a chapter, episode, or season at a moment of suspense: a character in peril, a secret about to be revealed, a choice not yet made. The resolution waits for the next installment, and the audience comes back to find out.

The device is older than the name. In the One Thousand and One Nights, Scheherazade postpones an execution by breaking off each night's story at an exciting point, so the king spares her to hear the rest. The frame turns the cliffhanger into a matter of life and death for the teller.

Serial publishing made it commercial. Nineteenth-century novels often appeared in weekly or monthly parts, and authors learned that a suspenseful ending sold the next issue. Thomas Hardy's A Pair of Blue Eyes, serialized in 1872 and 1873, left a character literally clinging to a cliff face at the end of one installment, and the scene is often cited as the source of the term. Silent film serials of the 1910s ended each episode with the heroine in mortal danger, and radio and television carried the habit on.

Television raised the stakes with the season finale. In 1980, an American prime-time drama ended its season with a central character shot by an unseen assailant, and the question of who did it occupied newspapers, betting shops, and conversation for months. The resolving episode drew one of the largest audiences in American television history.

Several mechanisms make cliffhangers work. Unresolved questions are hard to put down; see [[Curiosity]] and [[Zeigarnik Effect]]. Anticipation of a reward can be more motivating than the reward itself; see [[Reward Prediction Error]]. And audiences who have spent hours with characters develop attachments that make their fate feel personal; see [[Parasocial Relationship]].

Cliffhangers also carry risks. Resolving an impossible situation too easily feels like cheating, the sense of a convenient rescue from nowhere; see [[Deus ex Machina]]. Writers who end a season without knowing the resolution may have to rewrite earlier events to escape their own trap; see [[Retcon]]. And when a series is cancelled, its last cliffhanger is never resolved, which can sour the memory of the whole; see [[Peak-End Rule]].

Streaming changed the rhythm. When the next episode starts automatically within seconds, a cliffhanger no longer bridges a week of waiting; it bridges a countdown, and it can keep viewers watching late into the night; see [[Doomscrolling]]. Platforms competing for time have strong reasons to end every episode on a hook; see [[Attention Economy]]. Serialized games, podcasts, and web fiction use the same technique, and readers left hanging sometimes write their own resolutions; see [[Fan Fiction]].
