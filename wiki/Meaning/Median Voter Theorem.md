**The median voter theorem** says that if voters' preferences can be arranged along a single line, such as from left to right, and each voter prefers options closer to their own position, then majority rule selects the position of the median voter, the one with as many voters on each side; see [[Mean and Median]]. No other position can beat it in a head-to-head vote.

The idea has an economic ancestor. In 1929, the economist Harold Hotelling described two shops choosing where to locate along a street of evenly spread customers. Each does best by moving toward the middle, so they end up side by side in the center, the pattern often illustrated by two ice cream sellers on a beach. Duncan Black formalized the voting version in 1948, and Anthony Downs extended it to party competition in a 1957 book, An Economic Theory of Democracy. In the Downsian model, two parties seeking to win converge on the median voter's preferred policy.

The theorem matters partly because it avoids a famous problem. With unrestricted preferences, majorities can cycle, with A beating B, B beating C, and C beating A. Arranging preferences along one line rules out cycles; see [[Arrow's Impossibility Theorem]].

Its assumptions are strong:

- **One dimension.** Real politics involves economics, social issues, foreign policy, and more. With two or more dimensions, there is usually no stable winning position, and majorities can be led almost anywhere.
- **Two candidates.** Systems with more parties behave differently; see [[Duverger's Law]].
- **Everyone votes.** If unhappy voters at the extremes stay home, parties have reason to keep them happy.
- **Candidates can move freely.** Real politicians have records, convictions, and donors.
- **One election.** Party primaries come first and are decided by more committed voters, pulling candidates away from the center; see [[Gerrymandering]].

Observed politics fits the model only partly. Parties in two-party systems often sound similar on broadly popular issues, and candidates tend to move toward the center after winning a primary. But in many democracies, parties have grown more polarized in recent decades, which the basic model doesn't predict. Explanations include the factors above, along with media fragmentation and party activists; see [[Filter Bubble]] and [[Social Identity Theory]].

The model also implies that the range of politically viable positions follows the median voter, which helps explain why mainstream parties shift when public opinion does; see [[Overton Window]]. Some voting systems are said to reward broadly acceptable candidates near the middle; see [[Ranked-Choice Voting]].

As with other spatial reasoning, the distribution matters. If voters cluster in two camps rather than a single hump, the middle may be sparsely populated, and the median voter may hold views few people share; see [[Normal Distribution]].
