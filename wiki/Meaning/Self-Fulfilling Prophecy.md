A **self-fulfilling prophecy** is a prediction that causes its own fulfillment. The belief is false, or at least uncertain, when it is formed, but acting on it changes the situation until it becomes true. The sociologist Robert K. Merton named the idea in a 1948 essay, building on what became known as the **Thomas theorem**, from W. I. and Dorothy Swaine Thomas's 1928 observation that situations people define as real are real in their consequences.

Merton's main example was a bank run. A solvent bank holds only a fraction of its deposits in cash. If a rumor spreads that it is failing, depositors rush to withdraw, the cash runs out, and the bank fails, confirming the rumor. Nothing about the bank's finances was wrong until people believed it was. Deposit insurance, introduced in the United States in 1933, works by removing the reason to run, so the prophecy never starts.

Other examples share the same loop:

- **Markets.** Expecting a stock to fall leads people to sell, which makes it fall. George Soros called this feedback between beliefs and prices reflexivity.
- **Popularity.** A product or person believed to be winning attracts the attention, funding, and talent that make them win; see [[Matthew Effect]] and [[Network Effect]].
- **Panics and shortages.** Fear of a shortage leads to stockpiling, which empties the shelves; see [[Availability Cascade]] and [[Moral Panic]].
- **Bodies.** Expecting a treatment to help or hurt can change symptoms; see [[Placebo Effect]] and [[Nocebo Effect]].

The most famous claim in psychology is the **Pygmalion effect**. In a 1968 study, Robert Rosenthal and Lenore Jacobson told teachers that certain pupils, actually chosen at random, were about to bloom intellectually, and reported that those children later gained more on IQ tests. The finding was widely cited but later challenged; reanalyses found the effects were small and inconsistent, and it has become a case study in how striking results spread faster than corrections; see [[Replication Crisis]]. Expectations do seem to shape how teachers and managers treat people, but less dramatically than the original story suggested. Related research looks at how believing one can succeed affects effort; see [[Self-Efficacy]] and [[Growth Mindset]].

The opposite is the **self-defeating prophecy**, a prediction that prevents itself. Warnings of disaster prompt the work that averts it, and then the warnings look exaggerated; the large effort to fix date-handling bugs before the year 2000 is often cited.

The concept also explains how labels stick. Treating someone as a troublemaker can push them toward the role, and measured observation changes what is measured; see [[Hawthorne Effect]]. Once a prophecy has fulfilled itself, the outcome seems to confirm that the original belief was right all along; see [[Confirmation Bias]].
