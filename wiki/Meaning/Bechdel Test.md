The **Bechdel test** asks three questions of a film or other story. Does it have at least two women in it? Do they talk to each other? Do they talk about something besides a man? Some versions add that the women must be named. The bar sounds almost impossibly low, and the surprise is how many films have failed it.

It began as a joke. In 1985, a strip in Alison Bechdel's comic Dykes to Watch Out For showed a character explaining a personal rule of only watching movies that meet those three conditions; the last qualifying film the character could name was Alien. Bechdel credited the idea to a friend, Liz Wallace, which is why it's sometimes called the **Bechdel-Wallace test**, and has pointed back to Virginia Woolf's A Room of One's Own, from 1929, which observed that women in fiction were almost always shown only in relation to men. The test spread online in the 2000s and became a meme; see [[Meme]].

Its value is statistical, not critical. Any single film can pass or fail for reasons unrelated to its quality or its politics. A story with one lead character can fail without saying anything about women, and a film can pass on a ten-second exchange about lunch. Applied to hundreds or thousands of films, though, the failure rate becomes a measurement of something real, the habit of writing women as satellites to male protagonists. Volunteer-run databases have catalogued thousands of films against the test, a crowdsourced classification with the usual ambiguity about edge cases; see [[Data Labeling]].

Turning a vague concern about representation into three yes-or-no questions is an act of operationalization, with the same benefits and costs: it makes the concern countable and easy to argue about, but it measures only what it asks; see [[Operationalization]]. Once the test became well known, it could become a target, with writers adding a token exchange to pass; see [[Goodhart's Law]]. Its defenders generally answer that it was never meant to certify a film as feminist, only to make a pattern visible.

The format inspired variants. The Mako Mori test, proposed by fans online in 2013, asks whether a female character has a story arc that doesn't exist only to support a man's; see [[Fan Fiction]]. Computational studies now count dialogue by gender across thousands of screenplays, a way of extending the test's logic with language tools; see [[Named Entity Recognition]].

The pattern the test exposes has consequences beyond the screen. What audiences see repeatedly shapes what seems normal, and research on how group stereotypes affect performance suggests representation isn't merely cosmetic; see [[Stereotype Threat]] and [[Mere-Exposure Effect]]. Story templates that center a single hero tend to push everyone else to the margins; see [[Hero's Journey]].
