**Comparative advantage** is the principle that two parties gain by trading even when one of them is better at producing everything. What matters isn't who is best at a task but who gives up the least to do it. Each side should specialize in what it does at the lowest cost in forgone alternatives, and trade for the rest; see [[Opportunity Cost]].

The classic example comes from David Ricardo's Principles of Political Economy and Taxation, published in 1817. In Ricardo's numbers, Portugal could produce both wine and cloth with less labor than England. Portugal had an **absolute advantage** in both. Yet Portugal's edge was much larger in wine, so it did better making wine and buying cloth from England than making both itself, and England did better making cloth. Total output of both goods rose, and both countries could consume more.

A familiar everyday version involves a lawyer who types faster than any typist. Hiring a typist still makes sense, because every hour the lawyer spends typing is an hour not spent on legal work worth far more. Teams work the same way: the strongest engineer shouldn't take every task, since their time is the scarcest, though specializing too far leaves a team dependent on one person; see [[Bus Factor]].

The economist Paul Samuelson recalled being challenged by the mathematician Stanislaw Ulam to name one proposition in the social sciences that was both true and not obvious. Samuelson offered comparative advantage, noting that thousands of intelligent people had never grasped it or believed it once explained.

The principle has limits that critics stress. It shows that trade raises total output, not that everyone gains. Workers in industries that shrink can lose jobs, and research on the effect of Chinese import competition on American regions in the 2000s found the losses concentrated and lasting, not quickly absorbed elsewhere. Advantage also isn't fixed: industries get better with experience, so a country that specializes early may lock in a position others can't easily reach; see [[Learning Curve]] and [[Matthew Effect]].

Trade disputes often take the shape of a prisoner's dilemma, in which each country gains by imposing tariffs whatever the other does, yet both end up worse off than with open trade; see [[Prisoner's Dilemma]]. The same reasoning also counters the belief that a new source of labor, whether immigrants, machines or trading partners, must take a fixed number of jobs from existing workers; see [[Lump of Labor Fallacy]].

The logic now appears in discussions of automation, where the question is less whether machines can do a task than what people give up by not doing it; see [[Moravec's Paradox]] and [[Baumol's Cost Disease]].
