**Terror management theory** is a social-psychological research program, formulated in the mid-1980s by Jeff Greenberg, Sheldon Solomon and Tom Pyszczynski, holding that people manage the potential terror of knowing they will die by investing in cultural worldviews and in self-esteem. A worldview offers symbolic permanence — a nation, a faith, a body of work that outlasts the person — and self-esteem is the sense of meeting its standards. Both are described as anxiety buffers.

The program's value is that it turned a philosophical claim into testable predictions. **Mortality salience** is the standard manipulation: participants write briefly about their own death, then complete an unrelated task. The predicted result is intensified worldview defense — harsher judgment of transgressors, warmer evaluation of in-group members, stronger endorsement of cultural norms. It also predicts *when*: effects should appear only after delay and distraction, once death thoughts leave focal awareness. That timing prediction gives the program real [[Falsifiability]], which its broader [[Exhaustive Claim]] — that this explains religion, nationalism and status-seeking alike — does not.

The evidence is contested. Meta-analysis of hundreds of studies reports a moderate average effect, but with small samples, flexible outcome measures and clear signs of publication bias, which is [[Truncation Bias]] operating on a whole literature. A large pre-registered multi-laboratory replication of the classic judicial-severity finding did not reproduce it; the original authors disputed the protocol. The situation matches much of the social priming literature, including the [[Anchoring Effect]], and is unresolved rather than settled either way.

One distinction is worth holding. The theory draws on Ernest Becker's *The Denial of Death*, but the philosophy is not evidence for the experiments, and a failed replication does not refute the philosophy. Treating a literary argument and an effect size as one claim is what makes this literature hard to read.
