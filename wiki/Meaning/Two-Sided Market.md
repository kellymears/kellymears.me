A **two-sided market** is one where a business serves two distinct groups who need each other, and makes money by bringing them together. Credit cards connect shoppers and merchants, game consoles connect players and developers, ride-hailing apps connect riders and drivers, and app stores connect users and app makers. The economists Jean-Charles Rochet and Jean Tirole formalized the idea in the early 2000s, and Tirole's work on platforms was part of his 2014 Nobel prize.

What makes these markets unusual is the cross-side [[Network Effect]]: each group values the platform more as the other grows. More riders attract more drivers, which shortens waits and attracts more riders. That loop shapes the business in several ways.

- **The chicken-and-egg problem.** A new platform is worthless to both sides until each has arrived. Founders solve it by faking or buying one side first, seeding listings by hand, guaranteeing early drivers a wage, or launching one city or campus at a time; see [[Recommender System]] for the related cold start.
- **Subsidizing one side.** Prices need not follow costs. Platforms often charge the side that is harder to attract little or nothing, and recover the money from the other. Shoppers pay nothing to use a credit card while merchants pay a fee on every sale, and consoles are often sold at a loss and earn it back from game royalties.
- **Winner-take-most.** Because the larger platform is more useful to both sides, markets tend to tip toward one dominant player; see [[Matthew Effect]] and [[Path Dependence]]. Using several platforms at once, called multi-homing, slows this down, which is why drivers running two ride apps keep that market competitive.
- **Governance.** Platforms make rules for both sides, policing fraud, setting ranking algorithms, and running reviews, acting almost as private regulators; see [[Information Asymmetry]].

The trouble comes once a platform dominates. With both sides locked in, it can raise fees, reduce quality, and favor its own products, which Cory Doctorow calls [[Enshittification]]. Advertising-funded platforms are a special case where users are one side and advertisers the other, so users aren't the paying customer; see [[Attention Economy]].

Antitrust law struggles with these markets. Standard tests look for prices above cost, but a platform that charges one side nothing looks harmless even when it holds great power. Courts and regulators in Europe and the US have spent two decades adjusting, and fights over app store fees turn on it.

Interoperability is one proposed remedy. If users could take their contacts, history, and reputation to a rival, as phone number portability lets them keep a number, the network effect would belong less to the platform; see [[Federation]].

Matching markets that run without prices, like school choice and medical residencies, share some of these features; see [[Stable Matching]].
