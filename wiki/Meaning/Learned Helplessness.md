**Learned helplessness** is the passivity an organism develops after repeated exposure to an outcome it cannot control, a passivity that then persists even in a new situation where control has become possible. Martin Seligman and Steven Maier demonstrated it in dogs in 1967: animals given inescapable shocks in an initial phase later failed to escape shocks they easily could avoid, lying down and taking them, while animals whose earlier shocks had been escapable learned the new escape readily.

The mechanism the original theory proposed was a generalized belief — the animal (or person) learns that outcomes are independent of behavior, and then applies that lesson beyond the situation where it was actually true. In humans this shows up as a specific explanatory style: attributing bad outcomes to causes that are stable ("this will always be true"), global ("this affects everything"), and internal ("this is about me"), which predicts giving up faster on later, unrelated tasks. Seligman later revised his own model, noting that passivity after uncontrollable events is actually the default response, produced by a specific brain circuit, and that what needs explaining is the *absence* of helplessness in animals and people with a resilient explanatory style — a reframe he called the theory's inversion.

The concept is easy to over-apply to any case of low motivation; it specifically requires a documented history of uncontrollability, not just difficulty or failure. It has been influential — and contested — in explaining features of clinical depression, chronic institutional poverty, and abusive relationships, where the objection is usually not that the mechanism is wrong but that labeling a person "helpless" can obscure how real the external constraints still are, sliding into blaming the victim for a rational adaptation to a genuinely uncontrollable environment.
