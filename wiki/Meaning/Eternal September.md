**Eternal September** is the name Usenet users gave to the period beginning in September 1993, when a flood of newcomers arrived and never stopped. The term now describes any online community whose culture is overwhelmed when it grows faster than it can teach newcomers its norms.

Usenet, created in 1979 and 1980, was a worldwide network of discussion groups, called newsgroups, where messages spread from server to server. For years, access came mostly through universities and research labs. Every September, a new class of students got accounts and arrived in newsgroups without knowing the conventions: posting off-topic, asking questions answered many times before, quoting entire messages, and typing in all caps. Regulars spent a month or so pointing them to guidelines and FAQs, collectively called netiquette, and by October most had adapted.

In September 1993, America Online began offering Usenet access to its subscribers, and other commercial services followed. New users now arrived every day of the year, in numbers far larger than any university's freshman class. Dave Fischer described it in a January 1994 post as "the September that never ended," and the name stuck.

The pattern recurs because communities pass on their culture informally. Regulars model behavior, correct mistakes, and point people to resources. That works when newcomers are a small fraction of members; when they are the majority, the newcomers' habits become the norm. Visible rule-breaking that goes uncorrected signals that the rules don't apply; see [[Broken Windows Theory]] and [[Social Proof]]. Longtime members often give up and leave, or go quiet; see [[Spiral of Silence]].

Many communities have their own versions:

- Forums and chat channels swamped after being featured somewhere popular; see [[Internet Relay Chat]].
- Question-and-answer sites whose regulars burn out answering duplicate questions; see [[Cunningham's Law]].
- Social networks whose character changes as they grow, sometimes followed by a decline in quality; see [[Enshittification]] and [[Network Effect]].

Communities defend themselves in various ways: requiring new accounts to wait before posting, splitting into smaller groups, hiding behind invitation-only membership, or relying on jargon that marks insiders; see [[Shibboleth]] and [[Dunbar's Number]]. Each defense makes it harder for newcomers to join, and too much gatekeeping can make a community stagnate.

The term carries some snobbery. Newcomers have always been treated as a problem by people who were newcomers themselves not long before, and many communities that complained about AOL users went on to welcome millions more people to the internet. Still, the core observation holds: norms depend on the people who carry them, and a community that grows faster than it can pass them on becomes a different community; see [[Third Place]] and [[Context Collapse]].
