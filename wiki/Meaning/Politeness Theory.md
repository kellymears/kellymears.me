**Politeness theory** explains why people so rarely say exactly what they want. The linguists Penelope Brown and Stephen Levinson proposed it in an essay in 1978, expanded into the book *Politeness* (1987). It builds on the sociologist Erving Goffman's concept of **face**, the public self-image each person claims in an interaction and expects others to respect.

Brown and Levinson divided face in two:

- **Positive face**: the wish to be liked, approved of, and included.
- **Negative face**: the wish to be unimpeded, free to act without imposition.

Many ordinary speech acts threaten one or the other. A request imposes on the listener's freedom; criticism challenges their wish for approval; refusing an invitation risks rejecting the person; see [[Speech Act]]. Speakers manage these **face-threatening acts** with strategies, roughly in order of increasing caution:

1. **Bald on record**: "Close the window." Efficient, and acceptable in emergencies or between close equals.
2. **Positive politeness**: building rapport and common ground. "Hey, buddy, mind closing that?"
3. **Negative politeness**: deference, hedging, apology. "Sorry to bother you, but could you possibly close the window?"
4. **Off record**: a hint that leaves the listener room to ignore it. "It's cold in here." The listener has to infer the request; see [[Implicature]].

Choice of strategy depends on social distance, the power difference between speakers, and the size of the imposition.

Languages build these distinctions into grammar. Many European languages distinguish familiar and formal "you," the tu and vous of French. Japanese and Korean have elaborate systems of honorific verb forms. Choosing the wrong level is a social signal, and speakers shift styles with their audience; see [[Code-Switching]] and [[Internationalization]].

Critics argued that the model reflects Western individualism. The linguist Sachiko Ide argued that Japanese politeness is less a strategic choice than a matter of discernment, following social norms that dictate the right forms regardless of intent.

Politeness can carry real costs. Hedged warnings can fail to register: aviation safety training teaches junior crew members to state concerns plainly to captains, after accident investigations found important warnings buried in deferential phrasing. Teams need enough safety that blunt speech isn't punished; see [[Psychological Safety]]. Plain wording is a form of respect in official writing; see [[Plain Language]].

A popular 2007 online comment described **ask culture**, where people make requests freely and expect that no is an acceptable answer, versus **guess culture**, where people hint and avoid asking unless they are fairly sure the answer is yes. Each finds the other rude. Mixed audiences online make the right register hard to choose; see [[Context Collapse]].

Polite words wear out with use and are replaced, as softened terms take on the meaning they were meant to soften; see [[Euphemism Treadmill]].
