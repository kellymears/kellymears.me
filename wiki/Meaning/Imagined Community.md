An **imagined community** is a group whose members will never meet, know, or even hear of most of the others, yet feel bound to them. The political scientist Benedict Anderson introduced the term in *Imagined Communities* (1983) to explain nations. A citizen of a country of millions knows a few hundred people at most, yet will speak of "we" and, in a war, may die for the rest; see [[Dunbar's Number]].

Anderson stressed that imagined doesn't mean false. Any community larger than a village is imagined in this sense, held together by a shared picture rather than by face-to-face contact. The interesting question is how people came to imagine themselves as nations rather than as subjects of a dynasty or members of a religion.

His answer was **print capitalism**. After the printing press, publishers seeking larger markets printed in local languages rather than in Latin. Readers of the same vernacular became aware of one another as an audience, and the printed form of a language standardized it, separating it from neighbors and dialects. Newspapers mattered most. Anderson cited Hegel's remark that reading the morning paper had replaced morning prayer: each reader performs the ritual alone, knowing that thousands of others are doing the same at the same moment. A shared language also creates boundaries, marking who belongs by how they speak; see [[Shibboleth]] and [[Creole Language]].

Anderson argued that the first nationalisms appeared not in Europe but among the colonial-born elites of the Americas, whose newspapers and administrative boundaries defined their sense of a shared homeland. In the 1991 edition he added a chapter on the census, the map, and the museum, the tools colonial states used to classify populations and territory, which later nations inherited; see [[Legibility]] and [[Taxonomy]].

Imagined communities need shared stories of their past, and many of those stories are newer than they appear; see [[Invented Tradition]]. They also define themselves against outsiders. Smaller nations sometimes feel their own culture is inferior to that of larger ones; see [[Cultural Cringe]].

The concept travels beyond nations. Fandoms, online forums, followers of a podcast, and users of a programming language form communities through shared media and vocabulary, in which members feel kinship with strangers. Broadcast and social media can produce a one-sided version of the same feeling toward the people at the center; see [[Parasocial Relationship]]. Because loose ties connect large populations in only a few steps, such communities can spread fast; see [[Small-World Network]] and [[Strength of Weak Ties]].

The same machinery can also split communities. When media fragments, people can come to imagine different communities sharing one territory; see [[Filter Bubble]].
