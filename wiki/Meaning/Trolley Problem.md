The **trolley problem** is a thought experiment in ethics. A runaway trolley will kill five people on the track ahead. You can pull a lever to divert it onto a side track, where it will kill one person instead. Should you? Most people say yes. The philosopher Philippa Foot introduced a version in 1967, and Judith Jarvis Thomson named it and built the variants that made it famous.

The interest lies in the variants. In Thomson's **footbridge** case, the trolley can be stopped only by pushing a large man off a bridge into its path, killing him and saving the five. The arithmetic is identical, one life for five, yet most people say pushing is wrong.

The major ethical theories pull different ways:

- **Utilitarianism**, and consequentialism more broadly, judges actions by their outcomes. Five lives outweigh one, so both pulling and pushing are right.
- **Deontology**, associated with Immanuel Kant, holds that some acts are wrong whatever their outcome. Using a person merely as a means, as the footbridge does, violates a duty in a way that diverting a threat doesn't.
- **The doctrine of double effect**, from Thomas Aquinas, distinguishes harm intended as a means from harm foreseen as a side effect. The person on the side track isn't needed to stop the trolley; the man on the bridge is.

Psychologists took the problem into the lab. Joshua Greene's brain imaging studies in 2001 found that the footbridge case engaged emotional regions more than the lever case did, and proposed that fast intuitions resist personal harm while slower reasoning tallies outcomes; see [[Dual Process Theory]]. Answers also move with wording and order, and with whether lives are described as saved or lost; see [[Framing Effect]]. People judge harmful actions more harshly than equally harmful failures to act, an omission bias closely related to status quo bias; see [[Loss Aversion]].

The Moral Machine experiment at MIT collected about 40 million decisions from people in 233 countries and territories about whom an out-of-control self-driving car should spare. Preferences for sparing more lives and younger people were broad, but their strength varied by culture. Engineers note that real vehicles rarely face clean forced choices, and the larger questions concern how much risk to accept and who bears it; see [[Risk Compensation]].

The problem returns in debates over machine values. Deciding how an AI system should weigh harms forces the same choice between outcomes and rules; see [[AI Alignment]] and [[Reward Hacking]].

Critics argue the scenarios are so artificial that intuitions about them reveal little about real decisions. Defenders reply that thought experiments isolate one variable at a time, as controlled experiments do; see [[Randomized Controlled Trial]].
