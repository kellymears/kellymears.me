**Civil disobedience** is the deliberate, public breaking of a law that the lawbreaker considers unjust, done without violence and with a willingness to accept the legal consequences. Its purpose is to persuade rather than to coerce: by suffering the penalty openly, protesters try to show the majority what the law does and to appeal to its sense of justice. That separates civil disobedience from ordinary crime, which hides, and from revolution, which rejects the legal order as a whole; see [[Social Contract]].

The term comes from an 1849 essay by Henry David Thoreau, later titled Civil Disobedience, written after Thoreau spent a night in jail for refusing to pay a poll tax in protest against slavery and the war with Mexico. The essay argued that individuals should not let governments override their conscience. Mohandas Gandhi developed nonviolent resistance into a mass political method, called satyagraha, first in South Africa and then in India. The 1930 Salt March, a walk of about 240 miles to the sea to make salt in defiance of the British monopoly on it, drew tens of thousands into breaking the law.

The American civil rights movement made it central to its strategy. The Montgomery bus boycott of 1955 to 1956, lunch counter sit-ins that began in Greensboro in 1960, and the freedom rides all invited arrest. In the 1963 Letter from Birmingham Jail, Martin Luther King Jr. defended breaking unjust laws "openly, lovingly, and with a willingness to accept the penalty,". Later movements, from draft resistance to climate protests, adopted the same repertoire.

Philosophers debate when it is justified. John Rawls defined it as a public, nonviolent, conscientious act contrary to law, aimed at changing a policy, and argued it is justified in a nearly just society when serious injustice persists and normal channels have failed. It sits between respect for law and a conviction that some duties outrank it; see [[Deontology]].

The strategy depends on numbers and visibility. Protest faces the [[Free Rider Problem]] and other obstacles to [[Collective Action]], and many people privately oppose a law but stay quiet because they think they are alone; see [[Pluralistic Ignorance]] and [[Spiral of Silence]]. Public defiance can break that silence and spread rapidly; see [[Information Cascade]]. Harsh crackdowns often backfire by drawing attention to the cause; see [[Streisand Effect]]. Political scientists Erica Chenoweth and Maria Stephan found that, among major campaigns from 1900 to 2006, nonviolent ones succeeded about twice as often as violent ones.

Tenants withholding rent, workers striking and consumers boycotting use related forms of pressure; see [[Rent Strike]]. Not all conscientious lawbreaking is civil. The tradition's insistence on openness, nonviolence and accepting punishment is what distinguishes it from a minority imposing its will; see [[Tyranny of the Majority]].
