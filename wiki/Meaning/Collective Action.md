summary: Coordinated action toward a shared goal, and the free-rider problem that makes it hard.

**Collective action** is coordinated effort toward an outcome no participant could achieve alone. Its central difficulty, formalised by Mancur Olson, is that the benefit is usually *non-excludable*: if a campaign wins, everyone in the affected group gains, whether or not they participated. Each individual therefore has a rational incentive to let others bear the cost — the free-rider problem — and the result is that groups with a shared interest routinely fail to act on it.

The mechanisms that overcome it are well catalogued. **Selective incentives** give participants something non-participants do not get. **Small groups** work better than large ones because contribution is visible. **Conditional commitment** — I will act if enough others do — removes the risk of being the only one, which is why pledge-based organising works and why a stated threshold is a real mechanism rather than a slogan. **Social norms and solidarity** convert a cost into an identity, which is what durable organisations are for.

Elinor Ostrom's work on commons governance added the most hopeful finding: real communities manage shared resources sustainably far more often than the theory predicted, using locally-devised rules, graduated sanctions, and their own dispute resolution. The pessimistic model describes strangers; it describes neighbours much less well.
