The **spiral of silence** is a theory of public opinion in which people who sense their view is losing social ground grow less willing to voice it, and that growing silence makes the view look even less popular than it actually is, prompting further silence in a self-reinforcing loop. German political scientist Elisabeth Noelle-Neumann proposed it in 1974, arguing that humans carry something like a "quasi-statistical sense" — a constant, mostly unconscious monitoring of which opinions are gaining or losing ground — driven by a fear of social isolation that she treated as a basic, near-universal motivation.

The mechanism runs on perceived climate rather than actual headcount: a person doesn't need to know the true distribution of opinion, only their impression of which way it's trending, and media coverage is a primary input to that impression, since a view that dominates coverage looks dominant regardless of how evenly the underlying population is actually split. Holders of the ascendant-seeming view speak more confidently and more often, which further raises its apparent prevalence, while holders of the receding-seeming view increasingly self-censor at dinner tables, in comment sections, and in surveys — which is exactly the mechanism a genuinely held minority position can look, from outside, like it barely exists.

A "hard core" resists the spiral regardless of perceived isolation — people willing to voice an unpopular view even at real social cost — and Noelle-Neumann treated their persistence as often what keeps a receding opinion from vanishing outright. The theory is worth distinguishing from simple conformity or [[Groupthink]], which concern actually changing one's belief under group pressure; the spiral of silence specifically concerns *silence*, holding a belief privately while declining to state it, which is why anonymous or pseudonymous venues so often reveal opinion distributions that public, attributed venues had suggested were nearly nonexistent.
