A **Hobson's choice** is a free choice in which only one thing is offered. The options are to take it or to leave with nothing. It looks like a decision but leaves no real alternative.

The phrase comes from Thomas Hobson, a carrier who ran goods and passengers between Cambridge and London and rented horses from a stable in Cambridge in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries. To keep the best horses from being overworked, Hobson required customers to take the horse in the stall nearest the door, or none at all. The arrangement became famous among Cambridge students, the young John Milton wrote comic epitaphs on Hobson's death in 1631, and the phrase was in print by the 1660s.

A Hobson's choice differs from a dilemma. A dilemma offers two or more options, all unpleasant. A Hobson's choice offers one option and the illusion of choice. The line attributed to the early automaker Henry Ford, that customers could have a car in any color so long as it was black, is the classic modern example, reflecting a period when the Model T came in one color only.

Hobson's choices are common in modern life:

- **Terms of service.** Software and websites present long agreements that cannot be negotiated; the only alternative is not to use the product. Consent given this way is formal more than real; see [[Information Asymmetry]].
- **Market power.** When one provider dominates, as with a single internet service in a neighborhood, customers can accept its terms or go without. Platforms with many users can worsen their terms over time because leaving is costly; see [[Enshittification]] and [[Network Effect]].
- **Lock-in.** Products designed to be hard to repair or move away from narrow future choices to one; see [[Right to Repair]] and [[Planned Obsolescence]].

Interface designers can create Hobson's choices deliberately, hiding a refusal option or making it far harder than acceptance; see [[Dark Pattern]]. Defaults have strong effects even when alternatives exist, since many people never change them; see [[Choice Architecture]].

The psychology cuts several ways. People who sense that their options have been removed often resist, sometimes choosing against their interests to assert control; see [[Psychological Reactance]]. Repeated situations with no real alternative can teach people that their choices don't matter; see [[Learned Helplessness]]. Yet too many options can be paralyzing, and a single good offer can come as a relief; see [[Decision Fatigue]].

Framing also matters. The same offer, presented as an opportunity instead of an ultimatum, can feel quite different; see [[Framing Effect]]. A Hobson's choice is worth naming because it exposes the gap between having a choice and having options; see [[Sense of Agency]].
