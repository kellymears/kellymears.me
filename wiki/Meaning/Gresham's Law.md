**Gresham's law** is the observation that "bad money drives out good." When two kinds of money are legally worth the same but one is intrinsically more valuable, people keep the valuable one and spend the other. The worse money ends up doing nearly all the circulating.

The mechanism is easiest to see with coins. Suppose silver coins and copper coins with the same face value must both be accepted in payment. Anyone holding a silver coin would rather spend the copper one and keep the silver, which might be melted down or sold abroad for more. Soon only copper changes hands. The same happened with coins that had been clipped, shaved at the edges to steal a little metal; nobody spent a full-weight coin if a clipped one would do.

The principle is named after Thomas Gresham, a financier to the Tudor court in sixteenth-century England, though the name was applied only in 1858 by the Scottish economist Henry Dunning Macleod. The idea is far older. Nicolaus Copernicus wrote about it in a 1526 treatise on coinage, the medieval scholar Nicole Oresme described it in the fourteenth century, and Aristophanes compared Athens's preference for bad politicians to its preference for bad coins in a comedy from 405 BCE.

A modern example came in the United States after 1965, when dimes and quarters stopped being made from silver. Older silver coins were worth more as metal than as money, and they quickly vanished from circulation into drawers and melting pots.

The law depends on fixed exchange. If people can refuse bad money or value it at a discount, the effect reverses: during hyperinflations, people abandon the collapsing local currency and trade in a stable foreign one, a pattern sometimes called Thiers' law. Debates over printing money and monetary policy turn on related questions about what makes a currency trusted; see [[Modern Monetary Theory]] and [[Bitcoin]].

The idea is often stretched beyond money. Where buyers can't tell good products from bad, sellers of good ones are driven out, a market for lemons; the economist who described that problem cited Gresham's law directly; see [[Information Asymmetry]]. Cheap, attention-grabbing content can crowd out careful work when both are paid the same way; see [[Attention Economy]] and [[Enshittification]]. False claims spread faster than corrections; see [[Brandolini's Law]]. A metric that rewards the wrong behavior can drive good practice out of an organization; see [[Goodhart's Law]].

Shared resources degrade similarly when individuals benefit from spending down quality while costs fall on everyone; see [[Tragedy of the Commons]] and [[Externality]]. Currencies also gain value simply from wide acceptance; see [[Network Effect]].
