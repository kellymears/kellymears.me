A **speculative bubble** is a period when the price of an asset, such as stocks, houses, or tulip bulbs, rises far above any value its future income could justify, because buyers expect to resell at a higher price rather than hold it for what it earns. Bubbles end when new buyers run out, and the fall is usually faster than the rise.

The phrase **greater fool theory** names the logic. Paying too much is rational if a greater fool will pay even more later. Every buyer can believe this at once, and every buyer is right until the moment no one else arrives.

Famous episodes:

- **Tulip mania** in the Dutch Republic, 1636–37, when contracts for rare bulbs reportedly sold for the price of houses. Historians think later moralists exaggerated the scale.
- **The South Sea Bubble** of 1720 in Britain, when shares in a trading company with little actual trade rose about tenfold. Isaac Newton lost a fortune in it and supposedly said he could calculate the motions of the heavenly bodies, but not the madness of people.
- **The dot-com bubble**, 1995–2000, when companies with no profits went public at enormous valuations on the promise of the web; see [[Network Effect]].
- **The US housing bubble** of the mid-2000s, whose collapse in 2007–08 set off a global financial crisis.

The economist Hyman Minsky described a typical arc: a real innovation draws investment, rising prices draw speculators, lending loosens, prices detach from fundamentals, insiders start selling, and panic follows. The innovation is often real. The internet did change everything, just not on the timeline the 1999 prices assumed.

Psychology supplies the fuel. People buy because others are buying and getting rich; see [[Information Cascade]] and [[Social Proof]]. Rising prices are taken as evidence of value, which makes the asset behave a little like a [[Veblen Good]]. Stories about a new era displace arithmetic; see [[Availability Cascade]]. Once prices turn, holders refuse to sell at a loss and wait for a recovery; see [[Loss Aversion]].

Spotting a bubble beforehand is notoriously hard. Prices can stay irrational for years, and short sellers who are right too early go broke. After the crash it looks obvious; see [[Hindsight Bias]]. The few who called it are remembered as prophets, while equally confident predictions that never came true are forgotten; see [[Survivorship Bias]]. Risk models calibrated on the boom years tend to treat a crash as nearly impossible; see [[Black Swan]].

Bubbles also spread costs beyond the people who bought in. Debt taken on during the boom forces sales during the bust, which drags down prices for everyone, and banks that lent against inflated collateral can fail; see [[Externality]] and [[Moral hazard]].
