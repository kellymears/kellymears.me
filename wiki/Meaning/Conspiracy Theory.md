A **conspiracy theory** explains an event or situation as the result of a secret plot by powerful people, usually against the public interest, typically where a simpler explanation is supported by the evidence. The term is used for explanations that are unwarranted, not for every claim of conspiracy, since real conspiracies happen.

Real ones are documented: the Watergate break-in and cover-up; the US Public Health Service's Tuskegee study, which withheld treatment from Black men with syphilis for decades; tobacco companies concealing what they knew of smoking's harms. These were exposed by evidence, whistleblowers, investigative reporting, and documents, through the ordinary methods that unwarranted theories reject as compromised. The distinction lies not in whether powerful people ever plot but in how a theory relates to evidence.

Features common to unwarranted theories:

- **Unfalsifiable structure.** Absence of evidence becomes proof of a cover-up, and contrary evidence becomes disinformation, so nothing can count against the theory; see [[Falsifiability]] and [[Burden of Proof]].
- **Implausible scale.** Large secrets require many people to keep silent indefinitely, which grows less likely as the number involved rises; see [[Plausible Mechanism]].
- **Intentionality everywhere.** Accidents, incompetence, and complex systems are read as design; see [[Hanlon's Razor]].
- **Pattern-finding.** Coincidences and anomalies are treated as signals; see [[Pareidolia]].
- **Linked beliefs.** Belief in one theory predicts belief in others, sometimes contradictory ones. A 2012 study found that people who thought Princess Diana faked her death were more likely to also believe she was murdered.

Psychologists group the motives into three needs. **Epistemic**: wanting certainty and a clear explanation, especially for big events, which feel as though they need big causes. **Existential**: feeling safe and in control when one feels powerless. **Social**: belonging to a group with special knowledge, and maintaining a positive image of one's group by blaming others; see [[Social Identity Theory]].

Belief is sustained by the usual mechanisms of reasoning. Evidence is evaluated by whether it fits; repeated claims feel truer; and communities filter what members see; see [[Motivated Reasoning]], [[Illusory Truth Effect]], and [[Filter Bubble]]. Attempts at suppression can confirm the narrative; see [[Streisand Effect]].

Conspiracy theories have real consequences: lower vaccination rates, harassment of people cast as plotters, and violence. Historically, they have often targeted minorities, as in centuries of antisemitic plots, and have fueled panics about hidden threats; see [[Moral Panic]].

Responses with some evidence include prebunking, which warns people about manipulation techniques before they encounter them; addressing underlying distrust, which often has real causes; and avoiding ridicule, which pushes believers toward their community. Simpler explanations deserve preference until evidence demands more, and the question to ask of any theory is what would show it wrong; see [[Occam's Razor]].
