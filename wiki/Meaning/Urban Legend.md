An **urban legend** is a story about recent events told as true, usually as something that happened to a friend of a friend. It spreads by word of mouth, print, and now the internet, changing details as it goes while keeping its core. Folklorists call them contemporary legends, since "urban" refers to modern life rather than cities. The folklorist Jan Harold Brunvand popularized the subject with *The Vanishing Hitchhiker* in 1981.

Familiar examples:

- A hitchhiker picked up at night vanishes from the back seat, and turns out to have died years before at that spot.
- A traveler wakes in a bathtub of ice with a kidney removed by organ thieves.
- Alligators flushed as pets thrive in city sewers.
- Strangers hand out poisoned or razor-filled candy at Halloween.

The sociologist Joel Best investigated the last one in the 1980s and found no confirmed case of a stranger killing a child with Halloween candy. The most famous case, from 1974, was a father who poisoned his own son for insurance money. Yet warnings recur every autumn; see [[Moral Panic]].

Legends spread because they have the features that make stories memorable and worth repeating:

- **Plausibility.** They are set in ordinary places, close to real events.
- **A warning.** Many carry a lesson, such as don't trust strangers or new technology, which gives a reason to pass them on.
- **Emotion.** Disgust and fear make stories more likely to be shared; see [[Negativity Bias]].
- **A credible source.** The friend of a friend, a term coined by the writer Rodney Dale in 1978, is close enough to trust and far enough to be impossible to check.

Repetition by many people makes a story seem confirmed, and each teller adds conviction; see [[Information Cascade]] and [[Illusory Truth Effect]]. Media coverage can then amplify it into a perceived trend; see [[Availability Cascade]]. Rumors move through social networks much like updates through a peer-to-peer network, reaching nearly everyone in a few rounds; see [[Gossip Protocol]].

The internet changed their speed but not their nature. Chain emails warned of computer viruses that didn't exist. Forums produced deliberate fictions, called creepypasta, that escaped into belief: the Slender Man, invented for an online contest in 2009, was later treated as real by some readers. Fact-checking sites such as Snopes, founded in 1994, began by cataloging urban legends.

Legends also reveal what a society fears: crime, strangers, contamination, technology, and the loss of control. They overlap with explanations of hidden plots, though most legends claim no grand design; see [[Conspiracy Theory]].

People tend to believe others are more gullible than themselves, which makes debunking feel less needed than it is; see [[Third-Person Effect]]. Explanations of where words come from often spread the same way; see [[Folk Etymology]].
