The **panopticon** is a design for a prison proposed by the English philosopher Jeremy Bentham in letters written in 1787 and published in 1791. Cells are arranged in a ring around a central tower. Each cell is lit from behind, so a guard in the tower can see every inmate, while blinds keep the inmates from seeing the guard. No one can tell whether they are being watched at a given moment, so they must assume they always are.

Bentham's brother Samuel had devised the arrangement for supervising workers in Russia, and Jeremy promoted it for prisons, factories, schools, hospitals, and poorhouses. He called it "a new mode of obtaining power of mind over mind." The British government never built it, though later prisons borrowed the plan, among them Stateville in Illinois (1925) and the Presidio Modelo in Cuba (1926–1928).

The philosopher Michel Foucault made the panopticon famous in *Discipline and Punish* (1975). He argued that it captured how modern power works: not through public punishment of the body, but through constant visibility that leads people to discipline themselves. Schools, armies, factories, and hospitals record, examine, and rank, and people learn to behave as if observed; see [[Legibility]]. Being watched changes behavior even in mild settings; see [[Hawthorne Effect]].

The concept became a reference point for digital surveillance, though the fit is imperfect:

- **Scale.** Online, the watched are not a few hundred inmates but billions of people, tracked across websites and devices; see [[Third-Party Cookie]] and [[Browser Fingerprinting]].
- **Invisibility.** The panopticon depended on inmates knowing the tower was there. Much data collection is invisible, so it doesn't produce the self-discipline Bentham intended, until people learn of it; see [[Personal Data]].
- **Many watchers.** On social media, people watch each other, and the anticipation of an audience shapes what they post; see [[Context Collapse]].

The **chilling effect** is the self-censorship that follows from expected surveillance. A 2016 study by Jon Penney found that traffic to Wikipedia articles on terrorism-related topics dropped sharply after the 2013 disclosures of mass government surveillance. People who believe they are watched avoid expressing unpopular views, a dynamic close to the spiral of silence; see [[Spiral of Silence]]. Governments that filter the network rely on the same effect; see [[Internet Censorship]].

Workplace monitoring raises the panopticon's questions directly: keystroke logging, screen captures, and activity scores push workers to optimize what is measured rather than what matters; see [[Goodhart's Law]]. Watching also undermines the trust needed for people to admit mistakes; see [[Psychological Safety]]. Defenses range from law and encryption to technical methods that let data be analyzed without exposing individuals; see [[Differential Privacy]].
