summary: The theory of how new ideas and technologies spread through a society, slowly at first, then rapidly, then leveling off, carried by distinct groups of adopters whose reasons for joining differ at each stage.
---
**Diffusion of innovations** describes how new ideas, practices, and products spread through a population. Adoption rarely happens all at once. A few people try something new, then more, then a rush, and finally a slow trickle of holdouts. Plotted over time, the share of adopters traces an **S-curve**.

The theory grew from rural sociology. In 1943, the sociologists Bryce Ryan and Neal Gross studied how Iowa farmers took up hybrid seed corn, which produced clearly better yields yet took years to spread. Farmers rarely adopted on the evidence alone; most waited until neighbors had tried it. The communication scholar Everett Rogers synthesized hundreds of such studies in Diffusion of Innovations, first published in 1962 and revised through five editions.

Rogers divided adopters into categories by when they join:

- **Innovators**, about 2.5 percent, venturesome and tolerant of failure.
- **Early adopters**, about 13.5 percent, respected and well connected, whose choices others watch.
- **Early majority** and **late majority**, about a third each, who adopt once something is proven or common.
- **Laggards**, about 16 percent, who adopt last, if ever.

The percentages come from slicing a bell curve of adoption times at standard deviations, so they describe a model more than any particular market; see [[Normal Distribution]].

Rogers identified traits that speed adoption: a clear advantage over what exists, compatibility with current habits, simplicity, the chance to try before committing, and visible results that others can see. An innovation that fails these tests can stall however good it is on paper.

Social networks carry the spread. People adopt because others they know have; see [[Social Proof]] and [[Information Cascade]]. New information often arrives through acquaintances instead of close friends, who tend to know the same things; see [[Strength of Weak Ties]] and [[Small-World Network]]. Ideas that copy themselves from mind to mind follow similar dynamics; see [[Meme]].

For technology products, adoption feeds itself when each new user makes the product more valuable to the rest; see [[Network Effect]]. Early leads compound, and once a standard wins, switching away gets expensive; see [[Path Dependence]] and [[Matthew Effect]]. In 1991, the consultant Geoffrey Moore argued that many technology products die in a **chasm** between enthusiastic early adopters and a pragmatic majority that wants a complete, proven solution. Products often launch in a minimal form to find their first users before broadening; see [[Minimum Viable Product]].

Diffusion of ideas runs on the same curve. Positions that seem radical can move into the mainstream as more people adopt them; see [[Overton Window]]. Efficiency gains from a new technology can increase total use once it spreads; see [[Jevons Paradox]].
