**Conformity** is changing one's behavior or stated beliefs to match those of a group. Some conformity is sensible, since other people often know things we don't. Some of it is pressure to fit in. Social psychologists separate the two as **informational influence**, where the group is taken as evidence, and **normative influence**, where the group is a source of approval or rejection.

The most famous demonstration came from Solomon Asch in the early 1950s. A participant sat with a group of seven or so others, all secretly working for the experimenter, and judged which of three lines matched a reference line in length. The task was easy; alone, people almost never erred. On certain trials, though, the confederates all gave the same obviously wrong answer before the participant spoke. Across these trials about a third of responses went along with the group, and about three quarters of participants conformed at least once. About a quarter never did.

Asch's variations showed what drives it:

- **Unanimity matters most.** A single other person giving the correct answer, or even a different wrong one, cut conformity sharply. One dissenter makes dissent possible.
- **Group size** mattered up to about three or four confederates, then leveled off.
- **Privacy reduced it.** When participants wrote their answers instead of saying them aloud, conformity dropped, showing much of it was public compliance rather than changed perception.

Interviews revealed varied reasons. Some participants doubted their own eyes, some assumed the group knew something, and some knew they were right but didn't want to stand out.

Muzafer Sherif's earlier work in the 1930s showed the informational kind. In a dark room, a still point of light seems to move, and people asked how far it moved converged on a shared estimate when judging in groups, then kept that estimate when later tested alone; see [[Social Proof]].

A 1996 meta-analysis of studies using Asch's design found conformity higher in more collectivist cultures and lower in the United States in later decades than in Asch's time, suggesting the rate reflects culture as well as human nature.

Conformity underlies many group failures. Members of a team who privately doubt a plan may each assume the others agree; see [[Pluralistic Ignorance]] and [[Groupthink]]. People who sense their view is unpopular stop expressing it, making it seem rarer; see [[Spiral of Silence]]. Sequences of people copying earlier choices can lock in errors; see [[Information Cascade]]. And the accuracy of collective judgment depends on people forming views independently before hearing others'; see [[Wisdom of the Crowd]].

Remedies follow from Asch's findings: collect opinions privately before discussion, have junior members speak first, assign someone to argue the other side, and protect people who disagree; see [[Red teaming]] and [[Psychological Safety]].
