**The Rashomon effect** describes how people who witness the same event can give sincere, detailed, and contradictory accounts of it. The term comes from Akira Kurosawa's 1950 film Rashomon, in which a samurai's death in a forest is recounted by a bandit, the samurai's wife, the dead samurai speaking through a medium, and a woodcutter. Each version is plausible, each flatters or protects its teller, and the film never says which is true.

Kurosawa drew on two short stories by Ryūnosuke Akutagawa: "Rashōmon," which supplied the setting of a ruined city gate, and "In a Grove," published in 1922, which supplied the conflicting testimonies. The film won the top prize at the Venice Film Festival in 1951 and introduced many Western audiences to Japanese cinema. Its structure has been borrowed by countless films, novels, and television episodes.

The term moved into law, journalism, anthropology, and history. The anthropologist Karl Heider used it in 1988 to describe ethnographers who studied the same community and reached very different conclusions. It describes several distinct problems:

- **Memory.** Recollection is reconstruction, shaped by expectations and later information; see [[False Memory]].
- **Perspective.** Witnesses literally see different things from different positions, and attend to different details.
- **Interest.** People remember and tell stories in ways that protect their self-image or their side; see [[Social Identity Theory]].
- **Attribution.** Each witness judges others' actions by character and their own by circumstance; see [[Fundamental Attribution Error]].
- **Certainty.** Each person tends to feel that their own view is simply what happened; see [[Naive Realism]].

Storytellers use the effect deliberately. A narrator whose account can't be trusted is a related device; see [[Unreliable Narrator]]. Mystery fiction often gives readers competing testimonies to sift; see [[Fair-Play Mystery]]. Editing can make the same footage carry different meanings; see [[Kuleshov Effect]].

The concept also matters in practical settings. Incident reviews gather accounts from everyone involved, expecting them to differ, and treat the differences as information rather than lies; see [[Blameless Postmortem]]. Courts weigh conflicting eyewitness testimony, which research has shown to be far less reliable than juries tend to assume. Historians compare sources written from opposing sides.

A common misreading is that the effect proves there's no truth at all. Kurosawa's film is about the difficulty of knowing, and the self-interest that distorts testimony, not a claim that every version is equally valid. Physical evidence, independent records, and careful cross-checking can still narrow the possibilities; see [[Ground Truth]] and [[Map and Territory]].

Online, the same event can spread as several incompatible stories at once, each reaching an audience inclined to believe it; see [[Context Collapse]] and [[Filter Bubble]].
