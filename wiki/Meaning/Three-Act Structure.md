**Three-act structure** is the model of a story as three parts: a setup that establishes characters and a situation, a confrontation in which a central conflict escalates, and a resolution in which it is settled. It's the dominant framework taught for film and television writing and an implicit template for much popular fiction.

The idea is old. In the Poetics, Aristotle observed that a whole plot has a beginning, a middle and an end, and that its events should follow from each other by necessity or probability rather than arriving at random. Gustav Freytag, in 1863, analyzed five-act tragedy as a pyramid: exposition, **rising action**, climax, falling action and catastrophe, later generalized to **denouement**, the untying of the plot's knots.

Film adopted the three-act version. Syd Field's 1979 book Screenplay described a setup ending around a quarter of the way in with a plot point that spins the story in a new direction, a long second act of complications, and a second plot point about three-quarters through that launches the resolution. Later manuals subdivided further. Blake Snyder's Save the Cat, in 2005, specified fifteen beats with suggested page numbers, and many studio scripts follow such templates closely.

The structure encodes audience expectations. A story that opens on a stable world and then disrupts it gives the audience a question to carry. A story can also open in the middle of things and fill in the setup later; see [[In Medias Res]]. Escalation in the middle sustains interest, and a resolution should come from the characters' own choices, not from outside intervention, the failure named by [[Deus ex Machina]]. Details planted in the first act should pay off in the third; see [[Chekhov's Gun]]. Some objects exist only to set the plot moving; see [[MacGuffin]]. Mysteries add misdirection and an obligation to play fair with the reader; see [[Red Herring]] and [[Fair-Play Mystery]].

Television complicates the picture. Commercial broadcast episodes were long divided into acts ending at advertising breaks, each closing on a small hook, and serialized shows end seasons on larger ones; see [[Cliffhanger]].

Critics argue that the model is so flexible it describes almost anything, and that formula-driven scripts feel interchangeable. Alternatives exist. The [[Hero's Journey]] maps a different pattern of departure, ordeal and return. East Asian storytelling uses a four-part form, often described through [[Kishōtenketsu]], whose third part introduces a twist or contrast rather than a conflict. Literary fiction often abandons plot arcs entirely. Interactive stories raise the problem that players may not follow the planned sequence at all; see [[Branching Narrative]]. Narrators who can't be trusted can make the audience reread the whole structure at the end; see [[Unreliable Narrator]].
