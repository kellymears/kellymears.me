**Language death** is the end of a language as a means of everyday communication, when its last fluent speakers die or no children learn it. Of the roughly 7,000 languages spoken today, a large share, often estimated at around 40 percent, are endangered, and many have only a few elderly speakers. Meanwhile, about half the world's people speak one of a couple of dozen major languages.

Languages rarely die because their speakers die. They usually die through **language shift**, as a community moves to a more powerful language over a few generations. Parents who speak a minority language raise children in the dominant one, for school, work, or status, and the grandchildren may understand little of their grandparents' speech. Larger languages have a pull, since every additional speaker makes them more useful; see [[Network Effect]] and [[Matthew Effect]].

Shift was often forced. Schools in many countries punished children for speaking their home language, including Indigenous residential schools in North America and Australia. In 19th-century Wales, a wooden board called the Welsh Not was hung on children caught speaking Welsh.

What is lost includes more than vocabulary. Languages carry distinctive grammar, such as systems of evidence marking that require speakers to say how they know something, along with oral literature and knowledge of local plants, places, and history. Whether different languages shape thought differently is debated, but each records a different solution to the problems of communication; see [[Linguistic Relativity]] and [[Grammatical Gender]].

Revitalization has had real successes:

- **Hebrew** was revived as an everyday spoken language in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, after centuries used mainly in religion and writing, and now has millions of native speakers.
- **Māori** language nests, begun in New Zealand in 1982, immerse preschool children in the language with fluent elders. Hawaiian adopted the model soon after.
- **Manx**, whose last native speaker died in 1974, has new speakers raised through a Manx-medium primary school.
- **Welsh** gained official status, broadcasting, and Welsh-medium schools, and is widely spoken.

The common lesson is that a language survives only if children learn it at home or in immersion. Official recognition and signs help but don't substitute.

Linguists document endangered languages with recordings, dictionaries, and grammars, which can later support revival; see [[Digital Preservation]]. Getting scripts into digital text standards lets communities use their languages online; see [[Character Encoding]].

Language death also happens through mixing. Contact languages can arise as communities lose their original tongues, and new languages are born even as old ones fade; see [[Creole Language]]. Communities often keep a heritage language for identity even when daily life runs in another, switching between them; see [[Code-Switching]] and [[Imagined Community]].
