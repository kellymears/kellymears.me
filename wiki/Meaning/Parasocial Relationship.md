A **parasocial relationship** is the sense of intimacy, familiarity, and mutual regard a person develops toward a media figure — a celebrity, streamer, podcast host, or fictional character — who has no corresponding awareness of them. The term comes from sociologists Donald Horton and R. Richard Wohl in 1956, describing radio and television performers who address the audience directly, as if in conversation, generating what feels like a real relationship on only one side.

The mechanism runs through the same cognitive machinery as an ordinary friendship: repeated exposure builds familiarity, direct address (a host speaking to "you") triggers the same response as being spoken to in person, and disclosure of a figure's private life produces the reciprocity cue that normally signals trust is being earned. Livestreaming and social media intensify this by adding the appearance of two-way contact — a comment, a reply, a like — even though the relationship's asymmetry hasn't actually changed; the creator still doesn't know the viewer exists among thousands of others doing the same thing.

Parasocial bonds aren't inherently pathological — they scale a normal social instinct to an abnormal audience size, and most people hold them alongside, not instead of, real relationships. The concept is worth distinguishing from fandom generally: fandom can be entirely social, built among fellow fans, while the parasocial bond specifically runs toward the one figure who isn't participating in it. The risk cases are the ones that crowd out reciprocal relationships, or where a figure's business model depends on manufacturing a sense of one-on-one intimacy at scale — a dynamic platforms increasingly monetize directly, from subscription tiers to algorithmically surfaced "you and me" language.
