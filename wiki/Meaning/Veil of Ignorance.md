The **veil of ignorance** is a thought experiment for judging whether the rules of a society are fair. Imagine choosing those rules without knowing who you'll be once they take effect: rich or poor, healthy or ill, talented or not, in the majority or a minority, holding which religion or values. Rules chosen from behind that veil can't be tilted toward yourself, because you don't know which self to favor; see [[Thought Experiment]].

The philosopher John Rawls made the idea famous in A Theory of Justice, published in 1971. Rawls called the imagined choosing situation the **original position** and the resulting view **justice as fairness**. The economist John Harsanyi had used a similar device in the 1950s, and the two reached opposite conclusions from it.

Harsanyi reasoned that a person who didn't know their position would treat every position as equally likely and pick the society with the highest average welfare. That's ordinary expected-value reasoning, and it lands on a form of utilitarianism; see [[Expected Value]] and [[Trolley Problem]]. Rawls argued that with stakes this high, a whole life's prospects chosen once and for good, a rational chooser would look instead at the worst outcome and make it as good as possible, the maximin rule familiar from game theory; see [[Solved Game]].

From that, Rawls derived two principles. First, everyone gets equal basic liberties, such as freedom of speech and conscience, which can't be traded away for economic gains. Second, social and economic inequalities are acceptable only if positions are open to all under fair equality of opportunity and the inequalities work to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged, the **difference principle**. Inequality isn't forbidden; it has to pay its way for the people at the bottom. Debates about income floors often borrow this framing; see [[Universal Basic Income]].

Critics pressed from several sides. Robert Nozick argued in 1974 that justice depends on how holdings came about, not on the pattern they form, so no choice behind a veil can license redistribution. Others said maximin is far too cautious, since people routinely accept risk; see [[Loss Aversion]]. Experiments in which groups chose income distributions behind a simulated veil have reported that most chose to maximize the average subject to a guaranteed floor, a compromise between the two camps.

The device's reach extends beyond Rawls's own conclusions. It protects against majorities writing rules that serve themselves; see [[Tyranny of the Majority]]. It also shows up in procedural designs that deliberately keep deciders from knowing their stake, such as choosing decision-makers by lot; see [[Sortition]]. And it underlies a familiar test for any policy, which is whether you'd accept it if you didn't know whether it would happen to you.
