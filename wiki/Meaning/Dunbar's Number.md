**Dunbar's number** is the suggestion that humans can maintain stable social relationships with only about 150 people: people you know as individuals and whose relationships to each other you understand. British anthropologist Robin Dunbar proposed it in the early 1990s, working from a pattern in primates.

Dunbar's reasoning, called the **social brain hypothesis**, was that primates evolved large brains mainly to handle complex social lives, not to solve physical problems. Across primate species, he found that the size of the neocortex relative to the rest of the brain predicts typical group size. Extrapolating to the human neocortex gave a group of around 150. He then looked for that number in human life and reported finding it in hunter-gatherer clans, Neolithic villages, Roman army units, Hutterite communities that split once they passed about 150 members, and the number of people to whom people sent holiday cards.

Dunbar later described a series of layers, each roughly three times the size of the last:

- **About 5** intimate friends and family, the people you'd turn to in a crisis.
- **About 15** close friends.
- **About 50** good friends.
- **About 150** meaningful relationships.

The theory is that relationships cost time and attention, and the budget is limited. Primates bond through grooming, and Dunbar argued that language, gossip, laughter, and shared rituals let humans bond with more people at once.

The idea has been influential well beyond anthropology. Companies have capped team or site sizes near 150, and the notion that teams work better small appears in management thinking and in [[Brooks's Law]], where communication paths grow faster than headcount. Online platforms boast of thousands of friends or followers, but research on social networks finds that the number of people actually interacted with regularly stays far smaller, which is part of why [[Parasocial Relationship]] bonds with public figures feel real but aren't reciprocal.

Critics question the evidence. A 2021 statistical reanalysis using updated primate data found that the method could produce estimates anywhere from a handful of people to several hundred, with the confidence interval far too wide to support any single number. Others argue that group size depends heavily on culture, technology, and the definition of "relationship," and that the neocortex is not the only relevant brain structure. Even supporters treat 150 as an order of magnitude, not a constant.

What survives is a modest but useful point: attention for relationships is finite, more connections usually mean shallower ones, and coordination gets harder as groups grow, which is why groups so often split, form hierarchies, or rely on [[Legibility]] and formal rules once they pass a certain size. [[Theory of Mind]], the capacity to model what others think, is the cognitive work all those relationships demand.
