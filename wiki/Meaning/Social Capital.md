**Social capital** is the value that comes from relationships: the networks people belong to, the trust between them, and the norms of helping each other that let groups cooperate. Like money or skills, it can be built up and drawn on. A job found through a friend, a neighbor who watches the children, and a town where people trust each other enough to leave doors unlocked are all examples.

Several thinkers shaped the idea. Pierre Bourdieu, in the 1980s, treated social capital as one resource among others, alongside economic and cultural capital, and stressed how it helps privileged groups keep their advantages; see [[Matthew Effect]]. James Coleman described it as the relationships that make certain actions possible, such as the trust that let diamond merchants lend each other valuable stones without contracts.

Robert Putnam made the idea famous. In *Making Democracy Work* (1993), he argued that regions of Italy with dense civic associations, such as choirs and sports clubs, had more effective governments. In *Bowling Alone* (2000), he showed that Americans had joined fewer clubs, attended fewer meetings, and entertained friends less often since the 1960s; more people bowled, but fewer bowled in leagues. He blamed television, suburban sprawl, longer commutes, and generational change.

Putnam distinguished two types:

- **Bonding capital** ties together people who are alike, such as family, close friends, and ethnic or religious communities. It provides support but can exclude outsiders.
- **Bridging capital** connects people across differences, through acquaintances and mixed groups. It spreads information and opportunities; see [[Strength of Weak Ties]] and [[Small-World Network]].

Social capital helps groups solve problems that require cooperation, because trust lowers the cost of working together and repeated contact makes reputations matter; see [[Collective Action]] and [[Free Rider Problem]]. Exchanges of favors and gifts keep such networks alive; see [[Gift Economy]]. Informal gathering spots where people meet regularly help build it; see [[Third Place]].

The concept has drawn criticism. It is hard to measure, and studies often use proxies like club membership that may miss newer forms of connection. Strong networks can also be harmful: tight-knit groups can shut out others, and organized crime runs on trust and loyalty. Critics also worry that the idea shifts responsibility for public problems onto communities.

Whether the internet has built or eroded social capital remains debated. Online groups let people find others with shared interests across distances, but many connections are thin, and audiences mix in ways that make trust harder; see [[Context Collapse]] and [[Parasocial Relationship]]. The number of relationships a person can sustain stays limited regardless of platform; see [[Dunbar's Number]].
