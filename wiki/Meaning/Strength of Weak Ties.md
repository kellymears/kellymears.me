The **strength of weak ties** is the idea that casual acquaintances are often more useful than close friends for finding new information and opportunities. The sociologist Mark Granovetter introduced it in a 1973 paper of that title, one of the most cited in social science.

Granovetter distinguished **strong ties**, close friends and family seen often, from **weak ties**, acquaintances, former colleagues, and friends of friends. Interviewing people who had recently changed jobs in a Boston suburb, he found that most who heard about their new job through a personal contact had heard from someone they saw only occasionally or rarely, not from close friends.

The explanation lies in network structure. Close friends tend to know each other and share the same information, so a tight circle mostly circulates what everyone already knows. People also tend to befriend people like themselves, a pattern called **homophily**, which makes close circles even more alike. Weak ties are different: an acquaintance moves in other circles, so they act as a bridge, carrying news, ideas, and opportunities between groups that would otherwise be disconnected. Remove the weak ties, and a society fragments into isolated clusters.

The finding has held up well:

- **Jobs.** A 2022 study of millions of LinkedIn users, which randomly varied which connections the platform suggested, found that moderately weak ties were the most useful for job mobility.
- **Ideas and innovation.** People who bridge separate groups tend to have more novel ideas, because they encounter combinations others don't; the sociologist Ronald Burt called these gaps "structural holes."
- **Diffusion.** Simple information spreads fast across weak ties, but behaviors that need reinforcement from several people, such as adopting a risky new practice, spread better through clusters of strong ties; see [[Social Proof]].

Weak ties connect to several other ideas. They're what make a large network a [[Small-World Network]], where any two people are a few steps apart. They help explain why exposure to only like-minded contacts narrows what people see; see [[Filter Bubble]]. They complement the limits on close relationships; see [[Dunbar's Number]]. And platforms that collapse all ties into one audience change how they work; see [[Context Collapse]].

Weak ties also matter for well-being. Research by Gillian Sandstrom found that brief interactions with acquaintances, a barista or a neighbor, measurably improve mood, and remote work removed many of these casual contacts.

Technical networks show the same structure. A few long-range links in a mostly local network sharply shorten the paths between distant nodes, which is why peer-to-peer and gossip systems add random distant connections; see [[Gossip Protocol]] and [[Peer-to-Peer]].
