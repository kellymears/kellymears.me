An **availability cascade** is a self-reinforcing loop in which repetition of a belief in public discourse increases how plausible and important it seems, and that increased perceived plausibility drives still more repetition, independent of any new supporting evidence arriving along the way. Legal scholars Timur Kuran and Cass Sunstein named the pattern in a 1999 paper, building on Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman's earlier availability heuristic — the finding that people judge how common or risky something is by how easily examples of it come to mind, rather than by its actual base rate.

The mechanism has a social feedback loop layered on top of the individual cognitive bias: a vivid initial event (a single case, an early news story) gets amplified by media coverage that itself supplies fresh availability, activists and entrepreneurs with a stake in the issue push it further because visibility now serves their goals, and observers who privately doubt the growing alarm often stay quiet because the volume of repetition looks like [[Social Proof]] for a consensus that may not actually exist. Once the cascade is running, contrary evidence has trouble competing, because it's boring by comparison — a rare, striking case beats a mountain of unremarkable statistics for sheer memorability.

The concept doesn't require the underlying claim to be false — some cascades bring real, previously under-attended risks to appropriate public attention, and the same mechanism looks identical whether it's converging on truth or away from it, which is exactly why it's hard to spot from inside one. What makes it diagnosable after the fact is a mismatch between the intensity of public alarm and the base-rate data once someone bothers to measure it, the same gap that [[Moral Panic]] research tracks in its own cases. It's the population-scale version of the loop that runs the [[Streisand Effect]] at a single-story scale.
