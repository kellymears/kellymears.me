**Code-switching** is moving between two or more languages, dialects, or styles of speaking within a conversation or across settings. Linguists use the term in a narrow sense, and it has also taken on a broader social meaning.

In linguistics, code-switching is what bilingual speakers do when they alternate languages, sometimes mid-sentence: a Spanish-English speaker saying "I was going to call you, pero se me olvidó." For a long time it was dismissed as a sign of not knowing either language well. Research from the 1970s on, notably by Shana Poplack, showed the opposite: it follows grammatical rules, tends to occur at points where the two languages' structures line up, and is most common among the most fluent bilinguals. It does real communicative work: quoting someone, marking a topic as personal, signaling shared identity, or reaching for the word that fits best.

The social sense is broader. **Style shifting** describes how everyone adjusts speech by audience: you speak differently to a child, a friend, a judge, and a job interviewer. This is ordinary, and part of what linguists call communicative competence.

For members of marginalized groups, code-switching often means something more costly: adjusting speech, appearance, and behavior to fit the norms of a dominant group in order to be treated fairly. Black Americans who move between African American English and a "standard" variety at work are the most-studied example. Research in workplaces finds that people who code-switch are often judged more professional, but that maintaining it is tiring, and that the need to do it signals that their natural way of speaking is unfairly stigmatized. A dialect can mark someone as an outsider instantly, much as a [[Shibboleth]] does.

There is a close parallel in autistic experience. [[Masking]] means suppressing natural behavior, such as stimming or a direct communication style, and imitating neurotypical social scripts. Like code-switching, it can open doors, and like code-switching it has costs, which for autistic people have been linked to [[Autistic Burnout]]. Both rest on the idea that one way of speaking or behaving is the default and others are deviations, an idea questioned by [[Neurodiversity]] advocates and by sociolinguists who treat all dialects as rule-governed systems.

Code-switching depends on knowing your audience. When audiences merge, as on social media where friends, family, and employers read the same post, there is no single right register, the problem known as [[Context Collapse]].

The phenomenon also matters for technology. Speech recognition and language models often perform worse on dialects and mixed-language speech, which can push users toward a standard variety to be understood. [[Plain Language]] guidance and inclusive [[Microcopy]] try to avoid making any one register a requirement.
