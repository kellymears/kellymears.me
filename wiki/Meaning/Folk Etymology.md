**Folk etymology** is the process by which speakers reanalyse an unfamiliar word into familiar-looking parts and reshape it to match. *Crayfish* is Old French *crevice*, pulled toward *fish*. *Muskrat* is an Algonquian word pulled toward *musk* and *rat*. *Hangnail* is Old English *angnægl*, where *ang* meant painful and nothing hangs; *shamefaced* was *shamefast*, fixed in shame. The visible parts are the result of the mistake, not the source of the word.

Related mechanisms move word boundaries. **Back-formation** invents a shorter word by stripping what looks like a suffix: *edit* from *editor*, *burgle* from *burglar*. **Rebracketing** shifts the article — *a napron* became *an apron*, *an ewt* became *a newt*. *Pea* comes from *pease*, a mass noun mistaken for a plural. Spelling was interfered with too: *island* is native *igland*, respelled on the false model of Latin *insula*. The pressure throughout is toward legibility, the same instinct [[Plain Language]] serves deliberately.

The appetite for legible origins also produces stories that are themselves inventions. *Posh* is not "port out, starboard home", *golf* is not an acronym, and *rule of thumb* has no documented link to a law about beating a wife; acronymic origins for words older than the twentieth century are almost always retrospective. The [[Metasyntactic Variable]] `foo` is a partial case worth separating out: the word appears in 1930s comic strips and so predates the army acronym FUBAR often credited with producing it, even though the pairing with `bar` probably was shaped by the acronym. These are textbook [[Plausible Mechanism]]: tidy accounts with no [[Provenance]], spreading because they beat the honest answer of "origin unknown".

The lesson generalises past words. Visible internal structure is evidence about what speakers expected, not about where a thing came from — an [[Anchoring Effect]] made of morphemes, and one that applies to [[Naming]] as much as to etymology. Dated attestation, not plausibility, is the test.
