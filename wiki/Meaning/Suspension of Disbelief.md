**Suspension of disbelief** is an audience's willingness to accept the premises of a story, however impossible, in order to enjoy it. The phrase comes from the poet Samuel Taylor Coleridge, who wrote in 1817 of "that willing suspension of disbelief for the moment, which constitutes poetic faith." He was explaining how readers could be moved by the supernatural poems he wrote, provided he gave them enough human truth.

Readers accept dragons, faster-than-light travel, and talking animals easily. What breaks a story is usually not the impossible premise but violations of its own rules: a character acting against established personality, a problem solved by a power never mentioned, or a world that works differently when convenient. Audiences grant a story its premises once and then expect it to keep them; see [[Deus ex Machina]].

J. R. R. Tolkien argued, in his essay "On Fairy-Stories," that the phrase misdescribes success. A good storyteller creates a secondary world that the reader enters, and inside it what is told is true according to its laws, so the reader believes rather than suspending disbelief. When something false to that world occurs, the spell breaks and the reader is back outside, looking at a failed attempt.

Things that commonly break it:

- **Inconsistency**, such as continuity errors or rules forgotten when inconvenient; later revisions that rewrite past events are especially jarring; see [[Retcon]].
- **Unearned resolutions**, where setup and payoff don't match; see [[Chekhov's Gun]].
- **Unfair mysteries**, which hide the clues the reader needed; see [[Fair-Play Mystery]].
- **Reminders of artifice**, such as an actor looking at the camera or a misplaced interface; see [[Fourth Wall]].
- **Near-realism.** Characters that are almost but not quite lifelike can be more disturbing than stylized ones; see [[Uncanny Valley]].

Media manage it differently. Theater asks audiences to accept painted sets and actors speaking verse. Animation sets its own rules of physics. Games add a complication: players act within the world, so contradictions between what the story says and what the rules make them do can break belief; see [[Ludonarrative Dissonance]] and [[Magic Circle]]. Professional wrestling built a whole culture around performers and audiences maintaining a fiction together; see [[Kayfabe]].

Disbelief can also be suspended deliberately for effect. Many works break their own fiction, openly acknowledging their artifice to make a point or a joke, and audiences who know the rules enjoy the break.

Editing in film relies on audiences filling in what isn't shown, building meaning from juxtaposition; see [[Kuleshov Effect]]. The distinction between the story world and the telling of it helps explain which elements must stay consistent; see [[Diegesis]].

The same willingness can be exploited outside fiction, when audiences accept implausible claims because the story is satisfying; see [[Confidence Trick]].
