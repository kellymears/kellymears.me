**The tragedy of the commons** is Garrett Hardin's 1968 parable: a pasture open to every herder is individually rational to overgraze, because each added animal profits its owner fully while the cost of degradation is a shared [[Externality]] spread across every other herder. Every herder follows the same logic, capacity is exceeded, and the pasture collapses — widely read as proof that shared resources are inherently unmanageable without privatisation or state control.

The correction, from Elinor Ostrom's decades of fieldwork, is substantial, not a footnote. Ostrom catalogued real common-pool resources — irrigation systems, fisheries, forests, grazing land — managed sustainably for generations by the communities using them, without enclosure or a central regulator. She distilled the recurring features into design principles: defined boundaries around the resource and who may use it, allocation rules fit to local conditions rather than imposed from outside — the opposite of the simplification at [[Legibility]] — monitoring by the users themselves, graduated sanctions rather than instant expulsion, and cheap dispute resolution. She induced these as regularities accompanying success rather than a formula, added variables as the evidence grew, and warned against treating them as a panacea. They make the tragedy a design problem, not a solved one.

The sharpest correction is definitional: Hardin was describing **open access** — a resource nobody owns and nobody can exclude anyone from — not a commons in the sense Ostrom studied, governed by a defined community with rules and the standing to enforce them. Calling every shared resource "a commons" and citing Hardin to argue it must fail conflates the two. Both halves of the record matter: unmanaged open access does collapse under the incentives Hardin described, managed commons routinely do not, and which one is in front of you is an empirical, [[Ground Truth]] question about the [[Collective Action]] and governance in place, not a property of the resource itself. [[Commons-Based Peer Production]] borrows the vocabulary but not the mechanism: Hardin's argument needs a rivalrous resource that use depletes, while a digital commons is non-rival and fails the other way, by under-provision, held together by norms and licensing rather than price.
