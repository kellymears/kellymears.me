The **euphemism treadmill** is the cycle in which a gentle term introduced to replace an offensive one gradually absorbs the stigma it was meant to avoid, until it too must be replaced. Psychologist Steven Pinker popularized the phrase in 1994. His point was that the negative associations belong to the underlying concept, and attach to whatever word currently names it.

A **euphemism** is a mild or indirect word used in place of one considered harsh, rude, or taboo: "passed away" for died, "restroom" for toilet, "let go" for fired. Euphemisms do useful work. They soften bad news, show respect, and avoid needless offense. The treadmill describes what happens when the thing being named carries social stigma.

Classic sequences include:

- **Toilets.** Water closet, toilet (originally a word for grooming), bathroom, restroom, and lavatory, each a polite term that in time became the plain one.
- **Disability.** Terms that were once neutral clinical labels, such as "idiot," "imbecile," and "moron," became insults; their replacements followed. "Handicapped" gave way to "disabled," and some prefer "disabled person" while others prefer "person with a disability."
- **Old age and poverty.** "Old" became "elderly," "senior citizen," and "older adult"; "poor" became "underprivileged," "disadvantaged," and "low-income."

The linguistic process underneath is **pejoration**, the drift of a word's meaning toward the negative. A **dysphemism** goes the other way on purpose, choosing a harsher word for effect, and reclaimed slurs show that communities can sometimes take a word back.

The treadmill is often cited to argue that changing words is pointless, but the evidence is more mixed. Some replacements stick for generations. The pattern does show that changing a label rarely changes attitudes by itself, and that new terms work best alongside changes in how people are actually treated. Words also do more than reflect attitudes: whether they shape thought, the question behind [[Linguistic Relativity]], is still debated, and the choice of term can carry a frame, as in the [[Framing Effect]].

Euphemism also has darker uses. Bureaucratic language hides responsibility and harm: "collateral damage" for civilian deaths, "enhanced interrogation" for torture, "rightsizing" for layoffs. George Orwell's 1946 essay "Politics and the English Language" attacked this deliberately vague style, and [[Plain Language]] movements push back against it in government and law. Corporate euphemism is a cousin of the [[Dark Pattern]] when it obscures what people are agreeing to.

The treadmill links to other features of how words drift. [[Semantic Satiation]] makes words briefly lose meaning through repetition, [[Folk Etymology]] reshapes unfamiliar words, and a [[Shibboleth]] shows how word choice marks group membership, as current terms often signal awareness of a community's preferences. Choosing words carefully in interfaces, the work of [[Microcopy]], meets the same tension between clarity and gentleness.
