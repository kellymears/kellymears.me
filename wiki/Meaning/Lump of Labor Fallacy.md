The **lump of labor fallacy** is the belief that an economy has a fixed amount of work to share. If the amount were fixed, any new worker or labor-saving machine would take a job from someone else, and the only fair response would be to divide the lump differently: shorter hours, earlier retirement, fewer newcomers. The economist David Schloss named the idea in 1891 as a theory of the lump of labor, and economists have called it a fallacy ever since; see [[Logical Fallacy]].

The problem is that the amount of work isn't fixed. When a machine makes a product cheaper, buyers spend less on it and have money left to spend on other things, which employs other people. Cheaper goods also tend to sell in larger quantities, sometimes enough to raise employment in the very industry that automated; see [[Jevons Paradox]]. Entirely new kinds of work appear that no one could have listed in advance. Around 1900, roughly two in five American workers were in farming; today it's under two percent, and most of the work that replaced it didn't exist then.

A well-known modern example is the automated teller machine. As ATMs spread in the United States from the 1970s, the number of bank tellers didn't collapse for decades. Each branch needed fewer tellers, so branches became cheaper to open, banks opened more of them, and tellers shifted toward sales and service. The same reasoning answers fears about immigrants, women entering paid work, or older people working longer, each of which adds both workers and the spending that employs workers; see [[Comparative Advantage]].

Calling it a fallacy doesn't mean nobody loses. The argument is about the economy in aggregate over time. Individual workers whose skills become obsolete may never find comparable pay, and regions built around one industry can decline for a generation. Transitions are slow and costly even when the end state is richer. Meanwhile, work where productivity barely grows, such as caregiving, still has to match rising wages elsewhere, so its costs climb; see [[Baumol's Cost Disease]].

Policies built on the lump view have mixed records. France's move to a 35-hour week around 2000 was justified partly as a way to share work; studies have disagreed about how many jobs it created, and many found little effect.

Each wave of automation revives the question of whether this time is different, now with machines that write and reason. Some argue the historical pattern will hold; others that a technology able to do most cognitive tasks breaks the assumption that people keep a comparative edge somewhere. Proposals for an income floor often draw on that worry; see [[Universal Basic Income]] and [[Moravec's Paradox]].
