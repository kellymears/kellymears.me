A **portmanteau** is a word formed by blending parts of two words and combining their meanings: "smog" from smoke and fog, "brunch" from breakfast and lunch, "motel" from motor and hotel. Linguists usually call these blends.

The term comes from Lewis Carroll. In Through the Looking-Glass, published in 1871, Humpty Dumpty explains the nonsense poem "Jabberwocky" to Alice, saying that "slithy" means "lithe and slimy," and adding that it is "like a portmanteau—there are two meanings packed up into one word." A portmanteau was a kind of suitcase that opened into two equal compartments. Carroll's own coinages "chortle," from chuckle and snort, and "galumph," probably from gallop and triumph, entered ordinary English.

Blends appear in waves of new technology and culture:

- **Early twentieth century**: smog, brunch, motel.
- **Mid-century**: sitcom, from situation comedy; transistor, from transfer and resistor.
- **Digital era**: emoticon, malware, podcast, netizen, webinar.
- **Politics and media**: Brexit, coined in 2012 and widely copied for other countries; celebrity couple names; and blended words for social trends.

Not every short word is a blend. "Blog" is a clipping of weblog, which shortens one word rather than fusing two. Acronyms are built from initials instead; see [[Backronym]]. And some apparent blends are folk reinterpretations of older words; see [[Folk Etymology]] and [[Eggcorn]].

Blends tend to stick when the two sources overlap in sound, so the join is hard to see, and when the new word names something that needed a name. Most fail: deliberate blends in marketing and journalism are coined constantly, and only a few survive into common use. Those that last often lose their transparency over time, so few people now hear "motor" in motel; see [[Semantic Change]].

The process resembles other ways language fuses existing material. Mixing two idioms produces a different kind of blend; see [[Malaphor]]. Fixed phrase templates spawn endless variants; see [[Snowclone]]. Languages also borrow and adapt words from one another; see [[Loanword]]. In product naming, a blend can be memorable or merely confusing; see [[Naming]].

Computers build words from parts too. Language models split text into subword pieces learned from frequent combinations, which is why a novel blend may be processed as its two halves; see [[Byte-Pair Encoding]].

Some languages blend more readily than others. German and Dutch more often join whole words into compounds, while English, short on that habit, clips and fuses. Constructed languages sometimes adopt rules for systematic blending; see [[Constructed Language]].

Successful portmanteaus spread the way other catchy ideas do: through repetition and the pleasure of a clever fit; see [[Meme]]. A word that captures an idea neatly can also shape how people think about it; see [[Linguistic Relativity]].
