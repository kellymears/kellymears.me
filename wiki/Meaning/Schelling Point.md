A **Schelling point** is a solution people tend to choose when they must coordinate without talking, because it seems obvious to everyone, and everyone expects everyone else to see it too. The economist Thomas Schelling introduced the idea in his 1960 book *The Strategy of Conflict*, part of the work on game theory for which he shared the 2005 Nobel Prize in economics.

Schelling's best-known illustration asked people where and when they would meet a stranger in New York City, with no way to communicate in advance. Many of the people he asked chose the information booth at Grand Central Terminal, at noon. Nothing makes that spot correct, except that it is prominent, central, and likely to occur to the other person. Asked to pick a number, people disproportionately pick round or salient ones; asked to divide money, they split it evenly.

This is a **coordination game**: everyone benefits if they make the same choice, and the problem is not conflict of interest but knowing what the others will do. Driving on one side of the road is the classic case. Either side works, but everyone must pick the same. Once a convention forms, no one has reason to deviate, so it persists; see [[Path Dependence]] and [[Network Effect]].

What makes a point salient depends on shared culture: precedent, symmetry, uniqueness, round numbers, and what is usually done. Schelling noted that in negotiations and wars, settlements often land on natural boundaries, such as rivers, existing borders, or round percentages, because any other line invites endless haggling over why that line and not another; see [[Anchoring Effect]].

Coordination depends on **common knowledge**: not just that everyone knows something, but that everyone knows that everyone knows it. A secret that everyone privately holds doesn't enable action, which is why public announcements, rallies, and protests matter even when they tell people nothing new; see [[Pluralistic Ignorance]] and [[Collective Action]]. Engineers face the same problem: coordinating over unreliable messages can never produce certainty; see [[Two Generals Problem]].

The **stag hunt**, from a parable by Jean-Jacques Rousseau, adds risk. Hunters can cooperate to catch a stag, which requires everyone, or each can catch a hare alone. Cooperating pays more but fails if anyone defects, so people's choices depend on trust. Unlike the prisoner's dilemma, cooperation here is stable once trusted; see [[Prisoner's Dilemma]].

Schelling points show up widely. Technical standards create them deliberately; see [[Character Encoding]]. Defaults become the choice most people make simply because they are the obvious option; see [[Choice Architecture]]. In markets, investors may try to guess what others will think is valuable rather than what is, a dynamic that feeds bubbles; see [[Speculative Bubble]]. And shifting what views are acceptable to voice changes what people expect others to accept; see [[Overton Window]].
