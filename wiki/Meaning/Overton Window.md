**Overton window** describes the range of political positions considered acceptable to hold or advocate at a given moment — not what is legal, but what a mainstream figure can say without becoming unelectable. Positions inside the window are debated; positions outside it are unthinkable, radical, or unsayable, and the window's edges move over time as some formerly unthinkable position becomes debatable and some formerly normal position becomes unsayable.

The distinction that matters is between **moving the window** and **persuading inside it**. Winning an argument with someone who already accepts that a policy is worth discussing does not move anything; it adds a convert within an already-acceptable range. Moving the window means changing what counts as a legitimate position to hold at all, and that shift is usually driven less by any single argument than by a slow accumulation of coverage, institutional endorsement, and generational turnover — the same mix of forces behind a [[Moral Panic]], or a change in what [[Naming]] a policy makes it sound reasonable.

A durable misreading treats window movement as always the product of a coordinated campaign: an organisation deliberately stakes out an extreme position so a moderate one looks reasonable by comparison. That tactic is real — it is the door-in-the-face ask, or what students of social movements call the radical flank effect, an extreme position shifting the reference point against which the moderate one is judged — and it is sometimes amplified by [[Astroturfing]] to manufacture the appearance of spontaneous support. But most drift has no author. It is [[Path Dependence]] operating on public opinion, and crediting it to a mastermind is a [[Plausible Mechanism]] — a satisfying story standing in for a genuinely diffuse process.

The mechanism rhymes with [[Anchoring Effect]]: a proposal's plausibility is judged against whatever positions are already visible nearby, so shifting what is visible reshapes what everything else looks like.
