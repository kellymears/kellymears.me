**Enshittification** is writer and activist Cory Doctorow's term, coined in 2022, for the pattern in which online platforms get steadily worse for the people who use them. The American Dialect Society named it word of the year for 2023. Doctorow describes a sequence of stages:

1. **Good to users.** A platform starts by offering real value to attract users.
2. **Abuse users to serve business customers.** Once users are locked in, the platform shifts value to advertisers, sellers, or publishers: more ads in the feed, worse search results, more promoted content.
3. **Abuse business customers to serve shareholders.** Once businesses depend on the platform, it squeezes them too, raising fees, cutting reach, and requiring paid promotion to be seen.
4. **Decline.** The platform becomes a pile of value extraction held together by lock-in, until something lets people leave.

The mechanism is lock-in. A [[Network Effect]] makes a platform valuable because everyone else is there, and leaving means losing your contacts, audience, purchase history, or reviews. These switching costs, a form of [[Path Dependence]], let a platform make its product worse without losing many users.

Doctorow's examples include social media feeds that replace posts from people you follow with ads and recommended content, marketplaces where the top search results are paid placements, and search engines whose results fill with ads and content written for [[Search Engine Optimization]] rather than readers. Related patterns include [[Planned Obsolescence]], subscriptions for features that were once bought outright, and the [[Dark Pattern]] that makes cancelling harder than joining.

The argument is about incentives, not individual villainy. Doctorow points to four forces that once disciplined platforms: competition, regulation, workers who could refuse, and interoperability that let users take their data elsewhere. He argues that market concentration, [[Regulatory Capture]], and laws restricting reverse engineering weakened all four. Extracting value from a captive audience is a form of [[Rent-Seeking]], and the [[Matthew Effect]] explains why markets tip toward a few dominant platforms in the first place.

His proposed remedies focus on exit:

- **Interoperability.** Letting other services connect to a platform, so users can leave without losing their network.
- **End-to-end delivery.** Requiring platforms to deliver what users asked for, such as posts from people they follow, without throttling them for payment.
- **Ownership.** [[Right to Repair]] and the freedom to modify what you own.

Users find their own exits: open feeds such as [[RSS]], federated social networks, and [[Local-First Software]] that keeps data under the user's control.

Critics note the term is vivid but loose, sometimes applied to any product change people dislike. And not all decline is extraction: free services may simply be finding a way to pay for themselves, the tension at the heart of [[Free-to-Play]].
