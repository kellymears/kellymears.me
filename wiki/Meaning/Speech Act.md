A **speech act** is something done by saying something. "I promise to come tomorrow" doesn't describe a promise; it makes one. "I now pronounce you married," said by the right person in the right setting, changes two people's legal status. The philosopher J. L. Austin developed the idea in lectures published as *How to Do Things with Words* (1962), challenging the assumption that the main job of language is to state facts that are true or false.

Austin began by contrasting **performatives**, which perform an action, with statements, which describe. Performatives can't be true or false, but they can fail. Austin called the requirements **felicity conditions**: the right person must speak in the right circumstances, following the right procedure, and mean it. A wedding conducted by an actor in a play doesn't marry anyone, and a promise made with no intention of keeping it is insincere; see [[Kayfabe]] and [[Magic Circle]] for settings where everyone agrees words don't count.

He then argued that nearly every utterance acts in several ways at once:

- **Locutionary act.** The words themselves and their literal meaning.
- **Illocutionary act.** What the speaker does in saying them: asking, warning, ordering, apologizing.
- **Perlocutionary act.** The effect on the listener, such as persuading, frightening, or amusing them.

John Searle extended the theory in 1969 and later grouped illocutionary acts into five kinds: assertives, which state; directives, which ask others to act; commissives, which commit the speaker; expressives, which convey feelings; and declarations, which change reality by saying so, such as firing someone or declaring war.

Many speech acts are **indirect**. "Can you pass the salt?" is literally a question about ability but works as a request, and listeners understand it immediately. Explaining how people work out meaning beyond the literal words is the business of [[Implicature]], and it depends on guessing what the speaker intends; see [[Theory of Mind]]. Politeness often drives indirectness, and what counts as polite varies between cultures and settings; see [[Code-Switching]] and [[Euphemism Treadmill]].

Speech act theory has practical uses. Law depends on performatives: contracts, wills, verdicts, and oaths are all acts accomplished in words, with strict felicity conditions. Saying the right phrase can mark membership in a group; see [[Shibboleth]]. Clear official writing tries to make the intended act unmistakable; see [[Plain Language]].

Computer science borrowed the theory for agents that communicate. Agent communication languages of the 1990s labeled messages with performatives such as "inform," "request," and "propose." Language models face a new version of the question, since their text can now act: a model calling a tool sends an email or runs a command rather than describing one; see [[Tool Use]] and [[Large Language Model]].
