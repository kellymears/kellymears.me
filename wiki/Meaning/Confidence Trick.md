A **confidence trick** is a fraud in which the victim hands over money voluntarily, because trust was established before anything was asked for. The name is literal: a swindler arrested in New York in 1849 approached strangers, asked whether they had confidence enough to lend him a watch until tomorrow, and the press called him the confidence man.

The long con has a stable structure, cataloged by the linguist David Maurer in 1940. A **roper** finds the mark and befriends them. An **insideman** plays the expert, working out of a setting built for the purpose. A **convincer**, a small early payout in real money, makes the scheme feel proven. The **send** dispatches the mark to fetch a larger sum, and the **blow-off** ends the relationship in a way that discourages complaint.

Three mechanisms do most of the work. **Assumed identity** costs nothing to claim and is rarely tested, the problem [[Code Signing]] exists to solve in the machine case. **Borrowed social proof** transfers credibility without transferring verification: an introduction from a trusted party is treated as though they checked, when they were only introduced too. And the mark's own incentive sustains belief — often they think they are in on something slightly crooked, which suppresses reporting, and by the send they have sunk enough that disbelief is expensive. A story arriving pre-endorsed deserves what any [[Plausible Mechanism]] gets: a demand for [[Provenance]].

High-status milieus are permeable for structural reasons. Credentials go unchecked because checking reads as an insult, membership is treated as transitive, and each gatekeeper assumes an earlier one verified. [[Prompt Injection]] has the same shape — instructions inheriting authority from the channel that carried them — and the remedy is the same: check against [[Ground Truth]] rather than against confidence.
