**Doxing**, also spelled doxxing, is publishing someone's private identifying information without consent, usually to invite harassment. The target might be a pseudonymous account whose real name gets revealed, or a person whose home address, phone number, employer, or relatives' details are posted for a hostile audience. The word comes from "docs," as in dropping documents, slang from hacker communities in the 1990s.

Most doxing doesn't involve breaking into anything. It's assembled from fragments that are each public or nearly so. Property and voter records, court filings and business registrations list names and addresses. Data brokers sell profiles combining them; see [[Data Broker]]. Old domain registrations can expose a website owner's address; see [[Domain Registrar]]. Photos can carry the coordinates where they were taken, and backgrounds reveal landmarks; see [[Metadata]]. A reused username links an anonymous account to a named one. Matching these pieces is the same work as record linkage in databases, done by hand and aimed at one person; see [[Entity Resolution]].

That aggregation is the core problem. Each fact might be harmless alone, and the harm comes from combining them and handing the result to a crowd. Privacy law has struggled with this, since it tends to protect specific categories of secret information rather than the act of compiling ordinary details; see [[Personal Data]]. Some details are extracted through pretexts, such as a fake support call; see [[Phishing]].

Doxing often accompanies a pile-on. Someone posts something that travels far beyond its intended audience, strangers decide it deserves punishment, and finding the person's employer becomes the next step; see [[Context Collapse]]. Crowds also get it wrong. After the 2013 Boston Marathon bombing, users of online forums publicly named a missing student who had nothing to do with the attack, and the family was harassed before the real suspects were identified. Identifying people from photos by consensus resembles a crowd estimate, but without the independence that makes crowds accurate; see [[Wisdom of the Crowd]] and [[Information Cascade]].

The most dangerous escalation is **swatting**, a false report of a hostage situation or shooting at the target's address so that armed police arrive. In 2017, a swatting call over an online gaming dispute led police in Wichita, Kansas, to shoot and kill a man who had no connection to the dispute; the caller was sentenced to twenty years in federal prison.

Responses vary. Some jurisdictions have made doxing a crime, raising hard questions about where it ends and journalism begins, since reporters also identify people who would prefer to stay anonymous. Individuals can request removal from broker sites, use registration privacy, strip location data from photos, and avoid reusing handles. Publicizing a doxing attempt can spread the information further; see [[Streisand Effect]].
