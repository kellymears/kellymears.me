**Agenda-setting** is the idea that the news media shape which issues the public considers important. By choosing what to cover, how often, and how prominently, outlets tell their audiences what to think about, even when they have little power to change what people think. The political scientist Bernard Cohen put it this way in 1963: the press may not often succeed in telling people what to think, but it is stunningly successful in telling its readers what to think about.

The first systematic test came during the 1968 American presidential election. Maxwell McCombs and Donald Shaw surveyed undecided voters in Chapel Hill, North Carolina, asking which issues mattered most, and compared their answers with the coverage in the newspapers, magazines, and television news those voters used. The rankings matched closely. Their 1972 paper launched a research tradition that has since run to hundreds of studies across many countries.

The likely mechanism is accessibility. Issues that appear often come to mind easily, and what comes to mind easily feels important; see [[Availability Heuristic]] and [[Mere-Exposure Effect]]. Experiments by Shanto Iyengar and Donald Kinder in the 1980s showed that viewers shown more coverage of an issue rated it more important and then judged the president more by performance on it, an effect called priming; see [[Priming]].

Researchers later described a second level. Coverage emphasizes not only which topics matter but which attributes of them matter, such as whether a candidate is portrayed as competent or corrupt. That overlaps with the study of how presentation changes judgment; see [[Framing Effect]].

Before anything reaches the agenda, someone decides what gets through. **Gatekeeping** research, which began with a 1950 study of a newspaper wire editor's choices among incoming stories, examined how editors filter the flood of possible news. News judgment tends to favor conflict, novelty, and threat, so bad news is overrepresented; see [[Negativity Bias]]. Sudden waves of coverage can make a minor danger feel like a crisis; see [[Moral Panic]].

Platforms changed who sets the agenda. Ranking algorithms and trending lists now do much of the gatekeeping, optimizing for engagement rather than editorial priority; see [[Recommender System]] and [[Attention Economy]]. Politicians and activists can push topics into coverage by provocation, and organized campaigns can fake grassroots interest to do the same; see [[Astroturfing]]. Audiences fragmented by personalized feeds may no longer share a common agenda at all; see [[Filter Bubble]].

Agenda-setting interacts with which views seem acceptable to voice. Coverage that treats an issue as settled can quiet those who disagree, while coverage that treats a fringe position as debatable can widen the range of mainstream opinion; see [[Spiral of Silence]] and [[Overton Window]].
