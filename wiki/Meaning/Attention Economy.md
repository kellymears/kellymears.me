The **attention economy** treats human attention as a scarce resource that businesses compete for, much as they compete for oil or land. The idea starts with the economist and psychologist Herbert Simon, who wrote in 1971 that "a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention." When content is nearly free to produce and copy, the limit on how much gets consumed is the hours and focus people have; see [[Selective Attention]].

The business model that grew around this is advertising. Many online services charge users nothing and sell their attention to advertisers instead, which ties revenue to time spent and to how much the service knows about each user. That arrangement shapes products in predictable ways:

- **Engagement metrics.** Time on site, daily active users, and sessions per day become the numbers teams optimize, and features that raise them win even when users would rather spend less time; see [[Goodhart's Law]].
- **Endless feeds.** Infinite scroll and autoplay remove natural stopping points; see [[Doomscrolling]].
- **Variable rewards.** Pull-to-refresh and notification badges work like a slot machine, paying out new content unpredictably; see [[Compulsion Loop]].
- **Personalization.** Recommendation algorithms learn what holds each person and serve more of it, which can narrow what people see; see [[Recommender System]] and [[Filter Bubble]].
- **Tracking.** Knowing more about each user makes their attention worth more, which funds tracking across sites; see [[Third-Party Cookie]].

Critics such as Tristan Harris, a former design ethicist at Google, argue that these products are built to exploit psychological weaknesses, and that the techniques shade into manipulation; see [[Dark Pattern]]. Shoshana Zuboff's term *surveillance capitalism* describes the wider economy built on collecting behavioral data to predict and steer what people do.

The costs go beyond lost hours. Constant interruptions leave fragments of attention behind on the previous task, which lowers performance on the next one; see [[Attention Residue]]. Content that spreads widely tends to be whatever provokes strong emotion, especially outrage, and that shapes public discussion.

Platforms in this economy usually serve two groups at once, users and advertisers, and their incentives drift toward whichever side pays; see [[Two-Sided Market]] and [[Enshittification]]. Creators who depend on these platforms chase the same metrics, cultivating a feeling of closeness with their audiences; see [[Parasocial Relationship]].

Responses range from personal to structural. Individuals use blockers, grayscale screens, and app timers. Some designers argue for technology that respects stopping points and says when there's nothing new. Regulators have targeted tracking and addictive design aimed at children. Subscription models, which tie revenue to satisfaction rather than time spent, are one partial alternative.
