A **retcon**, short for *retroactive continuity*, is a change to the established facts of a fictional world made after those facts were published. A later story reveals that something earlier happened differently, or never happened, or meant something other than it seemed. The term spread through comics fandom in the 1980s, but the practice is as old as serial fiction.

The classic example predates the word. Arthur Conan Doyle killed Sherlock Holmes at the Reichenbach Falls in 1893, tired of the character. Under public pressure he brought Holmes back in 1903, explaining that the detective had faked his death and spent three years abroad. Soap operas routinely return characters from the dead or reveal hidden siblings, and in 1986 *Dallas* dismissed an entire season as a dream.

Retcons come in several kinds:

- **Additive.** New backstory fills a gap without contradicting anything, such as revealing a character's earlier history. Most audiences accept these freely.
- **Reinterpreting.** Earlier events stay the same but their meaning changes, as when a mentor turns out to have been the villain all along; see [[Unreliable Narrator]].
- **Subtractive.** Events are declared never to have happened, often by dream, alternate timeline, or magic. These provoke the most resentment.
- **Reboots.** The whole continuity starts over, which comics publishers have done repeatedly to make decades of backstory approachable to new readers, the fictional version of breaking compatibility; see [[Backward Compatibility]].

Writers retcon for practical reasons. Long-running series pile up contradictions written by different people over decades, and a popular character's death or a dead-end storyline limits future stories. A retcon can rescue a detail that was planted and never paid off; see [[Chekhov's Gun]]. Characters simplified over years sometimes get their complexity back; see [[Flanderization]].

The concept depends on **canon**, the set of works an audience treats as officially true within a fiction. The word was borrowed from religious scripture, and in fandom from Sherlock Holmes enthusiasts who called Doyle's original stories "the canon." Franchises sometimes declare large bodies of earlier material non-canonical, as Disney did with decades of *Star Wars* novels in 2014. Fans in turn distinguish canon from *fanon*, ideas so common in fan fiction that people mistake them for the official story, much as a plausible tale can displace a word's real origin; see [[Folk Etymology]].

Retcons provoke strong reactions because audiences invest in continuity. A change that undoes a story's consequences can make every later stake feel hollow, since anything might be reversed. Professional wrestling treats its storylines with the same flexibility; see [[Kayfabe]].

Software has a parallel. Rewriting a project's commit history makes the record tell a cleaner story than what happened, which is fine on a private branch and confusing once others rely on it; see [[Rebase]].
