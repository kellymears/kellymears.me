**Ranked-choice voting** lets voters rank candidates in order of preference instead of choosing just one. In its most common single-winner form, also called instant-runoff voting, counting proceeds in rounds:

1. Count every ballot's first choice. If a candidate has a majority, that candidate wins.
2. Otherwise, eliminate the candidate with the fewest first choices.
3. Move each of that candidate's ballots to its next-ranked candidate still in the race.
4. Repeat until someone has a majority of the remaining ballots.

The approach has nineteenth-century roots. Thomas Hare in Britain and Carl Andrae in Denmark developed the multi-winner version, the single transferable vote, in the 1850s, and the American architect William Robert Ware is credited with proposing the single-winner form around 1870. Australia adopted it for its federal House of Representatives in 1918 and has used it since. Ireland uses it for presidential elections, and the multi-winner form for its parliament. In the United States, Maine adopted it for many elections starting in 2018, Alaska in 2022, and New York City for primaries in 2021.

Supporters point to several benefits:

- **No spoilers.** Voters can rank a long-shot candidate first without wasting their vote, weakening the pull toward two parties; see [[Duverger's Law]].
- **Majority support.** The winner needs a majority of ballots still in play, not just the largest share.
- **One election instead of two.** It replaces separate runoff elections, which often have low turnout.
- **Less negative campaigning**, possibly, since candidates court their rivals' supporters for second choices.

Critics raise others:

- **Odd outcomes.** Ranking a candidate higher can, in rare cases, cause that candidate to lose. The system can also eliminate a candidate who would have beaten every rival one-on-one. Burlington, Vermont, saw this in a 2009 mayoral race and repealed the system the following year.
- **Exhausted ballots.** Ballots that don't rank all candidates drop out of later rounds.
- **Complexity.** Counting is slower and harder to verify by hand, and some voters find ranking confusing.

These trade-offs aren't unique to ranked ballots. Every system with three or more candidates has some flaw of this kind; see [[Arrow's Impossibility Theorem]]. Alternatives include approval voting, where voters mark every candidate they find acceptable, adopted by Fargo, North Dakota, in 2018, and methods that pick the candidate who wins every head-to-head matchup when one exists.

How results change depends on context. Ranked ballots may reward broadly acceptable candidates near the center; see [[Median Voter Theorem]]. The multi-winner form produces roughly proportional results, which also blunts the effect of district lines; see [[Gerrymandering]]. Some reformers prefer to skip elections altogether for certain bodies and choose members at random; see [[Sortition]].
