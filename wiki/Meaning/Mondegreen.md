**Mondegreen** is a misheard phrase reinterpreted as something else that still makes sense — "'scuse me while I kiss this guy" for "kiss the sky", "the ants are my friends" for "the answer, my friend". Sylvia Wright coined the term in 1954, recalling a childhood mishearing of a line from a Scottish ballad — "and laid him on the green" — as "and Lady Mondegreen", inventing a fictional casualty of the murder the ballad describes. The name is itself an instance of the thing it names, and it sits in a small [[Taxonomy]] of reanalysis errors alongside the [[Eggcorn]], the [[Malaphor]], and the templated [[Snowclone]].

Music and unfamiliar accents both produce mondegreens because the acoustic stream carries no reliable marker of where one word ends and the next begins — pauses fall inside words about as often as between them. A listener infers boundaries from context rather than reading them off the sound, so whatever is already primed wins the segmentation when the signal is ambiguous, an [[Anchoring Effect]] built out of half-heard phonemes. Melody stretches vowels and buries consonants, and an accent shifts phoneme boundaries away from the listener's own; both starve the inference, and expectation fills the rest — the same reason [[Automatic Speech Recognition]] substitutes plausible words for correct ones under acoustic uncertainty.

The cross-language cousin is *soramimi*, a Japanese term for hearing foreign-language lyrics as differently meaningful phrases in the listener's own language, exploited deliberately in parody dubs. The closer relative is the [[Eggcorn]]: both preserve sense through reanalysis, but a mondegreen dissolves once the [[Ground Truth]] lyric is known, where an eggcorn survives as the speaker's lasting analysis — a distinction [[Folk Etymology]] blurs at the level of whole languages.
