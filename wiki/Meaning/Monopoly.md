A **monopoly** is a market with a single seller of a product that has no close substitutes. Without competitors, the seller can charge more, produce less and innovate more slowly than a competitive market would allow, since customers have nowhere else to go; see [[Supply and Demand]]. An **oligopoly** is a market dominated by a few sellers, who may compete fiercely or settle into tacit coordination. A **monopsony** is the mirror image, a single buyer, such as the only large employer in a town, which can push wages down.

A **natural monopoly** exists where the fixed costs of an industry are so high that one provider is cheapest overall: it makes little sense to run two sets of water pipes or power lines down the same street. Such industries are often publicly owned or regulated, though regulators can end up serving the companies they oversee; see [[Regulatory Capture]]. Patents and copyrights grant temporary monopolies deliberately, as a reward for invention and creative work, until the work enters the [[Public Domain]].

Digital markets tend toward concentration. When a product becomes more valuable as more people use it, the largest network keeps attracting users, and the market can tip to one winner; see [[Network Effect]] and [[Two-Sided Market]]. Switching costs then hold customers in place; see [[Path Dependence]] and [[Walled Garden]]. Once rivals are gone, a platform can shift value from users and suppliers to itself, a pattern described as [[Enshittification]]. Firms also spend heavily to protect their position through lobbying rather than better products; see [[Rent-Seeking]].

**Antitrust** law, called competition law outside the United States, aims to restrain monopoly power. The US Sherman Act of 1890 outlawed monopolization and conspiracies to restrain trade. In 1911 the Supreme Court ordered Standard Oil, which controlled most American oil refining, split into more than thirty companies. In 1984 the Bell System, the national telephone monopoly, was broken up under a settlement with the government. A case against Microsoft over its bundling of a web browser with Windows ended in a 2001 settlement. In 2024 a US federal court ruled that Google had illegally maintained a monopoly in online search. Antitrust thinking has swung between a narrow focus on consumer prices and broader concern with corporate power.

Monopolies can also slow repair and extend product lifecycles in the seller's favor; see [[Planned Obsolescence]] and [[Right to Repair]]. Success that compounds into dominance is a market instance of the [[Matthew Effect]].

The board game Monopoly began as a critique. Lizzie Magie patented The Landlord's Game in 1904 to illustrate how land ownership concentrates wealth, with a set of rules in which one player ends up owning everything. The game was later adapted and sold by Parker Brothers in 1935, and its winner-take-all lesson survives.
