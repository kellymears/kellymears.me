The **iron law of oligarchy** holds that every complex organization, no matter how democratic its founding ideals, eventually comes to be run by a small group of leaders. The German-Italian sociologist Robert Michels stated it in *Political Parties* (1911), summarizing it as "who says organization, says oligarchy."

Michels studied the German Social Democratic Party, at the time the largest socialist party in the world and committed in principle to democracy and equality. He found it run by a stable cadre of professional officials. His reasons:

- **Size.** Thousands of members can't deliberate together, so they must delegate; see [[Dunbar's Number]].
- **Expertise.** Running an organization takes skills and knowledge that full-time leaders acquire and members don't.
- **Control of information.** Leaders manage communication, agendas, and the organization's press.
- **Interest.** Leaders come to depend on their positions and work to keep them, and their interests drift from members'; see [[Principal-Agent Problem]].
- **Member apathy.** Most members don't attend meetings, and those who do defer to leaders; see [[Free Rider Problem]].

Advantages compound: whoever leads gains visibility and connections that make reelection easier; see [[Matthew Effect]].

In 1970 the feminist Jo Freeman gave a talk, published in 1972 as "The Tyranny of Structurelessness," on the same problem from the opposite direction. Women's liberation groups had rejected leaders and formal roles as hierarchical. Freeman argued that structurelessness doesn't prevent elites; it hides them. Groups without formal structure are run by informal networks of friends, unaccountable because no one admits they exist. Her remedy was explicit structure: elected roles, rotation, written rules, and wide distribution of information.

The pattern recurs in online communities and open-source projects. Many are run by a founder with final say, which is at least honest about where power lies; see [[Benevolent Dictator for Life]]. Others adopt foundations, elected steering committees, and written conduct rules, applying Freeman's remedy; see [[Code of Conduct]]. Internet standards bodies developed rough consensus as a way to decide without voting; see [[Request for Comments]]. Volunteer wikis that started flat developed administrators, policies, and a dedicated core; see [[Wiki]] and [[Commons-Based Peer Production]].

Critics note that the law describes a tendency, not a certainty. Cooperatives and unions that rotate offices, limit terms, and keep members informed can resist it for long periods, and the economist Elinor Ostrom documented communities that governed shared resources democratically for centuries; see [[Tragedy of the Commons]]. Governments, which face the same pull toward insiders, can be captured by the industries they regulate; see [[Regulatory Capture]].

The law is sobering for anyone organizing people toward a shared goal; see [[Collective Action]].
