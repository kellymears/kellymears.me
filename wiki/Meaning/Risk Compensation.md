summary: The tendency of people to take more risks when they feel safer, which can cancel part of the benefit of a safety measure, a real effect that's often exaggerated into an argument against safety measures entirely.
---
**Risk compensation** is the tendency of people to behave less carefully when they feel more protected. A driver with better brakes may follow more closely; a skier wearing a helmet may ski faster. Some of the safety gained from the measure is spent on riskier behavior, so the net benefit is smaller than engineering alone would predict.

The economist Sam Peltzman brought the idea to wide attention in 1975, arguing that US car safety regulations hadn't reduced traffic deaths as much as expected, because drivers drove more aggressively and more pedestrians were killed. The **Peltzman effect** is named for that paper. Psychologist Gerald Wilde went further with **risk homeostasis**, the claim that people have a target level of risk and adjust their behavior to keep it constant, so safety measures can't reduce accidents at all.

The evidence supports a real but usually partial effect:

- **Anti-lock brakes.** A Munich taxi study in the 1980s found cars with anti-lock brakes were driven more aggressively and had no fewer accidents than those without.
- **Bicycle helmets.** One study found drivers passed closer to a cyclist wearing a helmet, though later analyses disputed the finding. Evidence on cyclists themselves riding faster is mixed.
- **Sports protection.** Better football helmets were followed by more head-first tackling, until rules banned it.
- **Medicine.** Fears that HIV prevention drugs or the HPV vaccine would increase risky sexual behavior were largely not borne out in studies.

Most researchers reject strong risk homeostasis. Seat belts, airbags, and road design have substantially reduced deaths per mile driven. The lesson is that behavior responds to safety, not that safety is futile. Claims that a protection is useless because of compensation deserve the same skepticism as claims it will work perfectly; see [[Motte and Bailey]].

The idea generalizes beyond physical risk:

- **Automation.** Drivers using assistance systems pay less attention, and pilots relying on autopilot lose manual skills; see [[Automation Bias]].
- **Software safety nets.** Teams with extensive tests, easy rollbacks, or strong type checking may review changes less carefully. That can be a fine trade-off, as long as it's a conscious one; see [[Defensive Default]].
- **Finance.** Insurance and bailouts can encourage risk-taking, an effect economists call moral hazard; see [[Information Asymmetry]].
- **Normalized risk.** Getting away with risky behavior repeatedly makes it feel safe, a related drift; see [[Normalization of Deviance]].

Designers sometimes work with the effect rather than against it. Some traffic engineers remove signs and road markings in town centers, so drivers feel less certain and slow down. The general principle is that people respond to perceived risk, not measured risk, so safety design has to account for what people feel as well as what's true.
