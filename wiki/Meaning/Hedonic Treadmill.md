The **hedonic treadmill** describes the tendency for a person's baseline level of subjective well-being to return to roughly where it was before a major change in circumstances, whether the change was a windfall or a setback. The term was coined by psychologists Brickman and Campbell in 1971, building on their earlier finding, published with Coates and Janoff-Bulman, that lottery winners were not measurably happier than a comparison group a year on, and that accident victims with paralysis had also recovered a surprising share of their prior baseline.

The mechanism is comparison, not memory loss: a person judges their situation against a reference point, and the reference point itself moves to track recent experience. A raise that once would have felt luxurious becomes the new normal within months, at which point maintaining the same felt level of satisfaction requires a further raise — hence "treadmill," running to stay in place. The same adaptation cuts the other way for loss, which is part of why resilience research finds most people recover more of their baseline well-being after adversity than they'd predict in advance.

The concept is often overstated into "money can't buy happiness," which the original research doesn't fully support — later work (Kahneman and Deaton, and subsequent replications and refinements) finds income continues to correlate with day-to-day emotional well-being past the point earlier summaries claimed it plateaued, just at a shrinking rate per dollar. The treadmill is better read as a claim about the *shape* of adaptation than a claim that circumstances are irrelevant: some changes (chronic pain, noise, a long commute) resist adaptation far more than others (a bigger house), which is exactly the kind of asymmetry that makes the concept useful for deciding what to actually optimize for.
