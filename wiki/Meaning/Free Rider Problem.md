The **free rider problem** arises when people can enjoy a benefit without contributing to its cost. Since each person gets the benefit whether or not they pay, each has a reason not to, and if enough people reason that way the benefit is never produced or slowly decays. It is one of the central problems of [[Collective Action]].

It is tied to the economics of **public goods**, which have two properties:

- **Non-excludable.** You can't easily stop people who didn't pay from using it. A lighthouse's light reaches every ship.
- **Non-rival.** One person's use doesn't reduce what's left for others. Your use of national defense or a clean-air law doesn't leave less for anyone else.

Markets underprovide public goods, because no seller can charge for something people get anyway. Street lighting, basic research, flood defenses, and public health measures such as herd immunity all have this shape. Goods that are non-excludable but rival, such as fisheries and grazing land, lead instead to the [[Tragedy of the Commons]], where the problem is overuse rather than underfunding. Both are close relatives of the [[Prisoner's Dilemma]], scaled up to many players.

Classic solutions include:

- **Taxation.** Governments fund public goods by compelling everyone to pay, which removes the option to free ride.
- **Making it excludable.** Paywalls, memberships, patents, and copyrights turn public goods into private ones, at the cost of shutting some people out.
- **Selective incentives.** Economist Mancur Olson argued in 1965 that large groups need private rewards for contributors, such as a union's insurance benefits or a professional body's journal, alongside the shared benefit.
- **Social norms.** Small groups manage through reputation, reciprocity, and shame. Elinor Ostrom documented communities that sustained shared resources for centuries this way.

Open source software is a striking modern case. Libraries used by millions of companies are often maintained by a handful of unpaid volunteers. The code is non-rival and non-excludable, so most users never contribute back. That leads to a dangerous [[Bus Factor]], to exhausted maintainers, and to [[Supply Chain Security]] incidents when a critical package is neglected or handed to the wrong person. Sponsorship programs, foundations, and dual licensing are attempts at selective incentives, and [[Commons-Based Peer Production]] describes why projects like Wikipedia succeed anyway.

Free riding also shows up in teams, as social loafing: people exert less effort in groups where individual contributions aren't visible. It is a cousin of the [[Bystander Effect]], where responsibility spread across many people becomes no one's.

Not all free riding is harmful. Knowledge spillovers, where one person's idea benefits many, are a main source of economic growth. The problem is not that people benefit freely, but that some goods won't exist unless someone finds a way to pay for them.
