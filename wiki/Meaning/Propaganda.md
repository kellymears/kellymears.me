**Propaganda** is communication designed to shape the beliefs and actions of a mass audience in the interest of whoever produces it. It is distinguished from ordinary persuasion less by whether it is true than by its methods: emotional appeal over evidence, selective framing over context, and repetition over argument. The word comes from the Congregation for Propagating the Faith, established by the Catholic Church in 1622 to spread its teaching, and took on its negative meaning during the twentieth century.

The First World War made propaganda an industrial activity. Governments on all sides produced posters, films and press campaigns; the United States set up the Committee on Public Information in 1917 to sell the war to the public. One of its veterans, Edward Bernays, went on to write a book titled Propaganda in 1928 and to found modern public relations, arguing that shaping public opinion was a necessary part of democracy. The totalitarian states of the 1930s took it much further. Nazi Germany had a ministry of propaganda that controlled press, film and radio; see [[Radio Broadcasting]]. Soviet poster designers made it an art form; see [[Constructivism]]. The phrase **big lie** comes from this era, describing a falsehood so outrageous that people assume no one would dare invent it.

Many classic techniques rely on known features of cognition. Repeating a claim makes it feel true, whatever the evidence; see [[Illusory Truth Effect]]. The same facts land differently depending on how they're presented; see [[Framing Effect]]. Showing that "everyone" believes something persuades the undecided; see [[Social Proof]]. Deciding which issues get covered at all is often more powerful than any argument; see [[Agenda-Setting]]. Coded language lets a message reach supporters while staying deniable; see [[Dog Whistle]]. Over time, propaganda can shift which ideas seem acceptable to say aloud; see [[Overton Window]].

Researchers distinguish **disinformation**, falsehood spread deliberately, from **misinformation**, falsehood spread by people who believe it. Disinformation campaigns often seed false claims that ordinary users then pass on sincerely. Digital media have lowered the cost of both. Fake accounts simulate grassroots support; see [[Astroturfing]]. Synthetic video and audio make fabricated evidence cheap; see [[Deepfake]]. Recommendation algorithms reward content that provokes strong reactions, whatever its accuracy; see [[Recommender System]]. A 2016 RAND analysis of Russian propaganda described a "firehose of falsehood": high volume, many channels and no commitment to consistency.

People tend to believe propaganda works on others more than on themselves; see [[Third-Person Effect]]. Defenses include fact-checking, which arrives after the claim has spread, and prebunking, which warns audiences about manipulation techniques in advance. Fear-driven campaigns can also feed [[Moral Panic]] and conspiracy theories.
