**Moral panic** is Stanley Cohen's term for an episode in which a condition, person, or group is defined as a threat to a society's core values, then amplified by media coverage until the public reaction is out of proportion to any measurable harm.

The framework as it is now taught has fixed parts, not all of them Cohen's. A **folk devil** is the stereotyped villain — a stripped-down, recognizable figure onto whom fear condenses, closer to a [[Prototype Theory]] category than to any real individual. **Moral entrepreneurs** — Howard Becker's term, from *Outsiders* (1963), which Cohen adopted rather than coined — campaign to have the threat recognized and punished; some act in good faith, some stand to gain, and [[Regulatory Capture]] names what happens when the second group writes the law. A campaign staged to look like spontaneous outrage rather than an organized push shades into [[Astroturfing]]. Media coverage supplies the amplification loop — reporting produces alarm, alarm produces more reporting — and the [[Naming]] of the danger does much of the work of making a diffuse fear feel real.

The load-bearing test is **disproportionality**, which belongs to a later and separate framework — Erich Goode and Nachman Ben-Yehuda's *Moral Panics* (1994), where it is one of five criteria alongside concern, hostility, consensus, and volatility. A panic is diagnosed not because a concern is fabricated but because the reaction — arrests, legislation, moral condemnation — exceeds any defensible measure of actual harm. [[Jaywalking]] shows the same machinery manufacturing a threat outright rather than merely inflating one, and both can permanently move what a society will legislate against, see [[Overton Window]].

The critique cuts the other way with equal force: naming something a moral panic can dismiss a concern without engaging its evidence, treating alarm itself as proof the alarm is unwarranted. Telling a manufactured panic apart from a proportionate response to real harm — the sort [[Terror Management Theory]] explains at the level of individual fear — is usually the harder question.
