**Tyranny of the majority** is the danger that rule by the majority becomes oppression of everyone else. Democracy settles disagreements by counting, but a permanent majority could vote away the property, liberty, or safety of a minority, and would be acting democratically while doing it. Majority rule is a procedure, not a guarantee of justice.

The worry is as old as democracy. Writers in ancient Greece criticized Athenian assemblies for condemning people on passion. The American founders took it seriously: James Madison's tenth Federalist essay, published in 1787, warned that a majority united by a common interest could trample the rights of others, and argued that a large republic with many competing factions would make such a majority harder to form.

Alexis de Tocqueville gave the phrase its fame. In Democracy in America, published in 1835 and 1840, Tocqueville observed that American majorities exercised enormous power, not only through laws but through opinion, making dissent socially costly even where it was legal. John Stuart Mill extended the point in On Liberty in 1859, arguing that the tyranny of prevailing opinion could be more dangerous than political oppression, since it reaches into private life; see [[Conformity]].

Democratic societies developed several defenses:

- **Rights.** Constitutions place certain protections beyond the reach of ordinary votes.
- **Courts.** Independent judges can strike down laws that violate those protections, though critics note the tension of unelected officials overruling elected ones.
- **Supermajorities.** Some changes, such as constitutional amendments, require far more than half the vote.
- **Divided power.** Federal systems, separate legislative chambers, and checks between branches make it harder for one majority to control everything.
- **Electoral design.** Proportional representation gives minorities seats, while winner-take-all systems tend to shut them out; see [[Duverger's Law]] and [[Ranked-Choice Voting]].

Majorities are less simple than they appear. Aggregating preferences can produce outcomes no coherent majority holds; see [[Arrow's Impossibility Theorem]]. Politicians competing for the middle tend to serve the median voter, not the whole population; see [[Median Voter Theorem]]. District boundaries can manufacture majorities that don't exist in the vote count; see [[Gerrymandering]]. Choosing representatives by lot offers another route around majority dominance; see [[Sortition]].

The social version can be the stronger one. People who believe their views are unpopular fall silent, making the majority look larger than it is; see [[Spiral of Silence]] and [[Pluralistic Ignorance]]. Group loyalty sharpens divisions between majority and minority identities; see [[Social Identity Theory]]. Sudden waves of public alarm have targeted minorities repeatedly; see [[Moral Panic]].

The opposite problem also exists. An organized minority can dominate a disengaged majority; see [[Iron Law of Oligarchy]].
