A **conceptual metaphor** is a way of understanding one domain of experience in terms of another. In *Metaphors We Live By* (1980), the linguist George Lakoff and philosopher Mark Johnson argued that metaphor is not mainly a decoration of poetic language but a basic structure of everyday thought, visible in ordinary phrases that nobody notices as figurative.

Their best-known example is the metaphor ARGUMENT IS WAR. People say they **defend** a position, **attack** a weak point, **win** or **lose** an argument, and **shoot down** a claim. The point is not only that the words come from fighting, but that people actually treat arguments as contests with sides and winners. A culture that thought of argument as a dance might argue differently.

Other common examples:

- **TIME IS MONEY.** Time is spent, saved, wasted, budgeted, and borrowed.
- **MORE IS UP.** Prices rise and fall, and numbers go up.
- **UNDERSTANDING IS SEEING.** "I see what you mean," "a clear explanation," "shed light on it."
- **IDEAS ARE OBJECTS.** People get an idea across, grasp it, or lose it.

Each metaphor maps structure from a familiar **source** domain, often bodily or physical, onto an abstract **target**. Lakoff and Johnson argued that many basic metaphors are grounded in physical experience: piles rise as things are added, so more is up. A metaphor highlights some aspects of its target and hides others. Treating argument as war hides that both people might learn something; see [[Map and Territory]].

When a metaphor is used long enough, people stop noticing it, and it becomes a **dead metaphor**. "Grasping" an idea and the "leg" of a table are examples, and many ordinary words were once metaphors; see [[Semantic Change]] and [[Folk Etymology]].

Computing is built from metaphors. Files, folders, windows, and the trash can come from the office; see [[Desktop Metaphor]] and [[Skeuomorphism]]. "Memory," "viruses," "bugs," and "the cloud" each carry assumptions from their source domains, which help newcomers but can also mislead them; see [[Mental Model]].

The theory has political uses. Lakoff later argued that political debates are won by framing, so describing taxes as a "burden" or a "relief" quietly argues for one side; see [[Overton Window]]. Experiments support a modest version of the claim: in a 2011 study, readers told that crime was a "beast" favored enforcement more than those told it was a "virus"; see [[Priming]].

Critics argue that the theory relies on intuition about phrases and can't show that people actually think through these metaphors rather than just inheriting the words. Experimental results are mixed, and some find the effects small. The debate overlaps with older questions about whether language shapes thought; see [[Linguistic Relativity]] and [[Prototype Theory]].
