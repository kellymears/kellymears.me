A **deus ex machina** is a plot resolution that arrives from outside the story's established logic: a sudden rescue, an unforeseen power, or a convenient coincidence that solves a problem the characters could not. The Latin means "god from the machine," translating a Greek phrase about the theater.

In Athenian tragedy, actors playing gods were sometimes lowered onto the stage by a crane, the *mēchanē*, to settle affairs that mortals had tangled. Euripides used the device often. At the end of *Medea*, the title character escapes punishment for murdering her children in a chariot sent by the sun god Helios. In *Poetics*, Aristotle criticized such endings, arguing that a plot's resolution should follow from the plot itself, by necessity or probability, rather than from machinery.

Later examples:

- **Molière's *Tartuffe*** (1664–1669). The swindler has won, until an officer of the king arrives to announce that the monarch has seen through everything.
- **The Threepenny Opera** (1928). Bertolt Brecht has a royal messenger ride in to pardon the hero on the gallows, a deliberately absurd ending that mocks the convention; see [[Fourth Wall]].
- **The eagles in Tolkien.** Their timely rescues in *The Lord of the Rings* are a perennial argument among readers. Tolkien defended sudden turns toward good fortune as central to fairy stories and coined the word eucatastrophe for them.

The opposite, **diabolus ex machina**, is a sudden, unearned disaster, such as a random accident that kills a character to create tragedy.

What makes the device feel cheap is the broken contract with the audience. Readers of mysteries expect to have been given the clues; a solution from nowhere cheats them; see [[Fair-Play Mystery]]. The standard remedy is setup: plant the rescuer, the power, or the rule early, so that its later use feels earned; see [[Chekhov's Gun]]. A story can also resolve by revealing that an apparently minor object was the key all along; see [[MacGuffin]]. Ongoing series sometimes rewrite their own history to escape a corner, which audiences often treat as the same cheat; see [[Retcon]].

Not every surprise ending is a flaw. Comedies and farces often embrace improbable resolutions, and some stories use the device ironically, as Brecht did. The rule of thumb among writers is that coincidence can get characters into trouble but shouldn't get them out.

Games face a version of the problem. A scripted rescue after an unwinnable fight, or a story that saves the player regardless of their choices, can make decisions feel meaningless; see [[Branching Narrative]] and [[Ludonarrative Dissonance]]. Generated stories avoid it only if their systems can produce endings from their own rules; see [[Procedural Narrative]].

How much a story owes its audience is partly a question of who decides what it means; see [[Death of the Author]].
