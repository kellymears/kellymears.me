A **snowclone** is a phrasal template with one or more slots that a speaker fills to produce a new, instantly recognizable statement — "X is the new Y", "the mother of all X", "in space, no one can hear you X". The pattern is not any one sentence but the frame around it; each instance is disposable, and the frame is what persists, a viral cousin of the [[Malaphor]] and part of the same [[Taxonomy]] of set-phrase mutation as the [[Eggcorn]] and the [[Mondegreen]].

Glen Whitman coined the term on his own blog, Agoraphilia, on 14 January 2004, in a post headed "Phrases for Lazy Writers in Kit Form Are the New Clichés". He was answering a request: Geoffrey Pullum had solicited a name for the pattern the previous October on *Language Log* — the group blog Mark Liberman and Pullum co-founded in 2003 — and endorsed the coinage there the day after it appeared. What Pullum kept complaining about was lazy journalism built on "if Eskimos have N words for snow, then Y have M words for Z", a claim that repeats with the same unearned confidence as a [[Folk Etymology]]. The template's own name is a joke about the template it describes.

Templates spread faster than novel phrasings because most of the cognitive work is already done. A snowclone arrives pre-verified for grammaticality and pre-loaded with the connotation of its famous original, so the load on speaker and listener is limited to filling one slot, not parsing new syntax — close to how a [[Constrained Decoding]] grammar narrows generation to a small set of legal completions, manufacturing fluency by removing choices rather than making better ones. The effect compounds like a [[Network Effect]]: each use increases recognizability, which increases the odds of the next use, until popularity and quality become hard to tell apart.

It is deliberate where its cousins are accidents of production — nobody sets out to mishear a lyric into a [[Mondegreen]] — and unlike the [[Backronym]], which repurposes existing letters, the snowclone repurposes an existing frame.
