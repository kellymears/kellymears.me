A **collocation** is a pairing of words that native speakers reach for together far more often than chance or grammar alone would predict — *strong tea* rather than *powerful tea*, *make a decision* rather than *do a decision*. Every substitute is grammatical and roughly synonymous; only one is idiomatic, and no rule says which — an arbitrariness close to the graded, exemplar-built categories [[Prototype Theory]] describes at the level of a single word rather than a pair.

That arbitrariness is what makes collocation the hardest layer of fluency to acquire. Grammar can be taught as a finite set of rules and vocabulary as a list of meanings, but which near-synonym a given noun prefers has to be absorbed word by word from enough real sentences, and there is no shortcut — a learner can know every word in *strong tea* and *powerful tea* and still not know which one a native speaker reaches for. It is also why violations are easy to spot from outside: an [[Eggcorn]], a [[Malaphor]], or a [[Mondegreen]] is often diagnosable because it produces a pairing that never occurs in real usage, an impossible collocation standing in for a possible one. Habitual company also holds a word's sense in place: part of what goes missing in [[Semantic Satiation]], where a word repeated alone turns to sound, is the surrounding usage that ordinarily does half the work of fixing what it means.

Corpus linguistics turns the intuition into a measurement. Given a large collection of real text as [[Ground Truth]] for what usage actually is, an association measure — mutual information, t-score, log-likelihood — compares how often two words co-occur against what chance predicts from their individual frequencies, ranking pairs by how tightly bound they are. A [[Snowclone]] takes the same principle further, fixing an entire template, while [[Grammatical Gender]] shows the same kind of memorized, non-derivable pairing one level down, between a noun and its agreement class.
