The **fundamental attribution error** is the tendency to explain other people's behavior by appeal to their character or disposition while explaining our own, identical behavior by appeal to our circumstances. Someone who cuts you off in traffic is "a jerk"; when you cut someone off, it's because you're late for something that matters. Social psychologist Lee Ross named the error in 1977, building on earlier attribution research by Edward Jones and Victor Harris, who found that observers still rated an essay-writer's true opinion as matching a pro- or anti-Castro essay they'd been *assigned* to write, even when told explicitly the position was assigned at random.

The mechanism is a difference in vantage point: an observer sees the other person as figure against a background they mostly ignore, so the person becomes the salient explanation, while the actor experiences their own situation from the inside, where the constraints, pressures, and reasons are exactly what's salient. This asymmetry is why the same error, run on oneself, tends to invert — a pattern sometimes called the actor-observer asymmetry, where people over-attribute their own failures to situation and their own successes to character, while doing the reverse for others.

The error matters most where it has consequences: it drives harsher judgments of poverty, crime, and unemployment as failures of character rather than circumstance, and it's a routine source of conflict in close relationships, where a partner's lapse reads as "who they are" while your own identical lapse reads as "a bad day." It's worth distinguishing from the [[Halo Effect]], which contaminates trait judgments with an unrelated positive impression; the attribution error is specifically about *where* you locate the cause of a behavior, not about whether the overall impression is favorable.
