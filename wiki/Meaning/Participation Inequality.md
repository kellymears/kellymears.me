**Participation inequality** is the tendency for a small fraction of people in an online community to produce most of what's in it. The usability researcher Jakob Nielsen summarized it in 2006 as the **90-9-1 rule**: about 90 percent of users read without contributing, 9 percent contribute occasionally, and 1 percent account for most contributions. A related **1% rule** circulated among bloggers around the same time. The non-contributors are traditionally called **lurkers**, a term from the era of bulletin boards and mailing lists; see [[Bulletin Board System]].

The exact proportions vary by community and aren't a law. Some studies of online health forums and other groups found distributions close to 90-9-1; others found more even participation, especially in small communities or ones where contributing is easy. The general shape holds widely: contribution is concentrated in a heavy tail, like many other human activities; see [[Pareto Principle]] and [[Zipf's Law]]. On large collaborative projects, a small share of editors make most of the edits; see [[Wiki]] and [[Commons-Based Peer Production]].

The concentration tends to reinforce itself. Regular contributors gain reputation and attention, which motivates them to contribute more, while newcomers' first posts may go unanswered; see [[Matthew Effect]]. Communities can come to depend on a handful of people, and lose much of their output when those people burn out or leave; see [[Bus Factor]].

The most practical consequence is distortion. Reviews, comments, and forum threads reflect the people who chose to write them, who tend to hold stronger opinions than the silent majority. Product ratings often cluster at the extremes, with many five-star and one-star reviews and few in between, partly because people with moderate experiences don't bother to write. Reading a community's posts as a sample of its members is a classic selection error; see [[Selection Bias]]. Visible opinion can then shape what others feel comfortable saying; see [[Spiral of Silence]] and [[Social Proof]].

Lurkers aren't freeloaders in any harmful sense. Most consume what others produce without depleting it, and many learn, buy, or act on what they read; see [[Free Rider Problem]]. They're also the audience that gives contributors a reason to post.

Nielsen's suggestions for flattening the curve included making contribution easier, such as ratings instead of written reviews; letting people edit rather than create from scratch; making some participation a side effect of ordinary use; and rewarding contributors without letting the most active dominate. Badges and points can raise participation but also concentrate it among people chasing the rewards; see [[Gamification]].

Communities also change when newcomers arrive faster than existing members can teach norms, and the mix of contributors shifts; see [[Eternal September]].
