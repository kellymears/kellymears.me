The **hero's journey**, or monomyth, is the claim that hero myths across the world share a single underlying story. The mythologist Joseph Campbell set it out in *The Hero with a Thousand Faces* (1949), borrowing the word monomyth from James Joyce's *Finnegans Wake*. Campbell summarized it as: a hero ventures from the everyday world into a region of supernatural wonder, wins a decisive victory, and comes back with the power to bestow benefits on others.

The journey has three acts, which Campbell took partly from the anthropologist Arnold van Gennep's structure of initiation rituals; see [[Rite of Passage]]:

1. **Departure.** The call to adventure, often refused at first, then accepted with help from a mentor, ending with crossing a threshold into the unknown.
2. **Initiation.** A road of trials, allies and enemies, a descent into the deepest danger, and a reward.
3. **Return.** The journey home, often pursued, and the hero's arrival as master of both worlds.

Campbell divided these into seventeen stages and drew heavily on the psychologist Carl Jung's ideas about universal symbols.

Its influence on popular storytelling is large. George Lucas credited Campbell's book in shaping *Star Wars* (1977). In 1985 the Disney story analyst Christopher Vogler wrote a memo condensing Campbell into twelve stages for screenwriters, later expanded into the book *The Writer's Journey* (1992). It became a standard Hollywood template.

Criticisms:

- **Selection.** Campbell chose myths that fit and trimmed the parts that didn't. Folklorists who catalog tales systematically find many more patterns; Vladimir Propp's *Morphology of the Folktale* (1928) had already identified 31 recurring plot functions in Russian fairy tales, a more modest and testable claim.
- **Narrowness.** The model centers a male hero who leaves and conquers. Maureen Murdock's *The Heroine's Journey* (1990) proposed an alternative.
- **Template over discovery.** Once writers began following the pattern on purpose, its presence in new stories proved nothing about myth. It also encourages formula, as every story converges on the same beats; see [[Snowclone]].

Pattern-hunting of this sort resembles how people form categories around a best example; see [[Prototype Theory]]. Story structures from outside the tradition, such as the four-part kishōtenketsu of Chinese and Japanese poetry and narrative, build around a twist rather than conflict.

Games adopted the structure eagerly, but players' choices can strain a fixed arc; see [[Branching Narrative]] and [[Ludonarrative Dissonance]]. Games that tell stories through places rather than cutscenes often leave the journey for the player to assemble; see [[Environmental Storytelling]]. A well-built journey plants its tools early, so the climax isn't rescued from outside; see [[Chekhov's Gun]] and [[Deus ex Machina]].

Tarot readers sometimes read the major arcana as a similar journey, from the Fool to the World; see [[Tarot]].
