The **Streisand Effect** is what happens when an attempt to suppress or remove a piece of information publicizes it instead, precisely because the suppression is itself newsworthy. The name comes from a 2003 case: Barbra Streisand sued a photographer over an aerial coastline-erosion survey that happened to include her Malibu house, and the lawsuit — not the photo — is what made a barely-viewed image famous, with hundreds of thousands of downloads following the filing.

The mechanism is a variant of reactance plus a media logic: censorship is itself a story, often a better one than the original content, and it signals to onlookers that the material must be worth seeing. A cease-and-desist letter, a takedown notice, or a superinjunction all carry the same risk — the legal or reputational move becomes the headline, and the original post gets archived, mirrored, and screenshotted by people who had no reason to seek it out before. Courts and PR firms now treat this as a live variable: sometimes ignoring a minor leak is the lower-attention path, and pursuing it is the mistake.

It is not a universal law — plenty of suppression works exactly as intended, quietly, because no one was watching for a story. The effect requires an audience primed to notice and share the *act* of suppression, which is why it shows up most reliably online, where the mechanics of removal (a 404, a notice-and-takedown, a court order) are themselves screenshottable content. It's worth distinguishing from the [[Availability Cascade]], where attention snowballs from ordinary salience rather than from a suppression attempt; the Streisand Effect specifically needs someone trying to make information go away.
