The **bystander effect** is the finding that an individual is less likely to help someone in distress the more other witnesses are present, the opposite of what intuition suggests a bigger crowd would guarantee. Psychologists Bibb Latané and John Darley developed the theory in 1968, prompted by the widely (and, later research showed, somewhat inaccurately) reported 1964 murder of Kitty Genovese in New York, which press coverage framed as dozens of neighbors watching and doing nothing.

Latané and Darley identified two separate mechanisms working together. Diffusion of responsibility means that as the number of onlookers grows, each individual's personal sense of obligation to act shrinks, since the responsibility feels shared rather than owned — with enough witnesses, everyone can privately assume someone else will call for help, or already has. Pluralistic ignorance supplies the second half: in an ambiguous situation, people look to others' reactions to judge whether it's actually an emergency, and everyone else, independently doing the same calm scan, projects a false sense that nothing's wrong, which is the same [[Social Proof]] mechanism running in reverse to suppress action rather than prompt it.

The effect has been replicated across dozens of experimental setups, from staged emergencies to online forums where a request for help gets slower responses in a crowded channel than a nearly empty one. The practical countermeasure follows directly from the mechanism: singling out one bystander by description ("you, in the blue jacket, call an ambulance") breaks the diffusion by assigning specific, unshareable responsibility, which is why first-aid training teaches exactly that phrasing instead of a general appeal to the crowd.
