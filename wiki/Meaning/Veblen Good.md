A **Veblen good** is a product for which demand rises as the price rises, the opposite of the usual rule. The high price is part of what buyers want, because it signals wealth and status. Luxury handbags, watches, cars, and some wines work this way: lowering the price can make them less desirable.

The idea is named for the economist and sociologist Thorstein Veblen, whose book *The Theory of the Leisure Class* (1899) coined the phrase **conspicuous consumption**: spending on visible goods and services mainly to display wealth. Veblen observed that the newly rich of the American Gilded Age competed through lavish displays, wasteful by design, because waste proved one could afford it. He also described conspicuous leisure, demonstrating that one didn't need to work.

The underlying logic is signaling. A signal is credible only if it's costly to fake. Anyone can claim to be rich, but only the rich can actually spend a fortune on a watch. Expense is the point; a cheaper watch that keeps better time doesn't send the same message. Biologists find the same logic in nature, where traits such as a peacock's tail may persist because they are costly to produce; see [[Information Asymmetry]].

Veblen goods are related to **positional goods**, whose value depends on how they compare to what others have. A big house matters partly because it's bigger than the neighbors'; an elite university degree because few people have one. Positional competition can become self-defeating: if everyone spends more to get ahead, nobody gets ahead, and everyone has spent more. This is a collective action problem, like an arms race; see [[Collective Action]] and [[Prisoner's Dilemma]].

Consequences:

- **Brand strategy.** Luxury firms limit supply, avoid discounts, and sometimes destroy unsold stock to protect prices and exclusivity.
- **Imitation and escalation.** When signals become affordable, through counterfeits or mass-market versions, the wealthy move to new ones. Fashion cycles partly reflect this chase.
- **Inconspicuous consumption.** Some elites now signal through things that are hard to display cheaply, such as education, health, time, and taste rather than logos.
- **Digital status.** Online, scarce or verified badges, rare in-game items, and limited editions work as positional goods; see [[Gacha Monetization]].

The concept links to broader questions of well-being. If satisfaction depends on relative position, rising incomes for everyone may not make people happier; see [[Hedonic Treadmill]]. Status displays spread through imitation of those with prestige; see [[Social Proof]] and [[Halo Effect]]. And a high price can make a product seem better than it is, a kind of [[Placebo Effect]] observed in wine tasting experiments.
