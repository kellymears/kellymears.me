**Deontology** is the family of ethical theories holding that the rightness of an action depends on whether it conforms to a duty or rule, not only on its consequences. The name comes from the Greek word for duty. A deontologist can say that lying, breaking a promise or punishing an innocent person is wrong even in a case where it would produce better results. That puts the view in sharp contrast with consequentialist theories, such as utilitarianism, which judge actions solely by their outcomes; see [[Trolley Problem]].

The most influential deontologist is Immanuel Kant, whose Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals appeared in 1785. Kant argued that moral rules must be binding on any rational being, whatever their desires, and called the supreme principle the **categorical imperative**. One formulation says to act only on a principle that you could will to become a universal law. A lying promise fails the test, since if everyone made lying promises, promises would stop meaning anything. The same "what if everyone did that?" question underlies everyday reasoning about the [[Free Rider Problem]] and the [[Tragedy of the Commons]]. Another formulation says to treat humanity, in yourself and others, always as an end and never merely as a means. Using people as tools, by deceiving or coercing them, disrespects their capacity to choose for themselves.

In a 1797 essay, Kant argued that it would be wrong to lie even to a murderer asking where a friend was hiding. Most later deontologists find this too rigid. In 1930 W. D. Ross proposed several prima facie duties, such as fidelity, gratitude and not harming others, that can conflict, leaving judgment to settle which one wins in a given case.

Deontology captures intuitions consequentialism struggles with. Most people think a surgeon shouldn't harvest one healthy patient's organs to save five, even though the numbers favor it. Rights, promises and the requirement of informed consent in medicine all have a deontological shape. Rawls's theory of justice drew heavily on Kant; see [[Veil of Ignorance]] and [[Social Contract]].

The theory has known weaknesses. Rules can conflict, and absolute rules can require letting disasters happen. Critics also note that following orders can masquerade as duty; see [[Obedience to Authority]]. A third major tradition, virtue ethics, going back to Aristotle, asks neither about rules nor outcomes but about what a person of good character would do.

The debate has practical stakes in AI. Systems can be trained to follow explicit principles rather than only to maximize a measured outcome, a rule-based approach with a deontological flavor; see [[Constitutional AI]] and [[AI Alignment]]. Language matters too, since promises and commands are acts that create obligations; see [[Speech Act]]. People also bend rules after doing good; see [[Moral Licensing]].
