The **fourth wall** is the imaginary barrier between a fictional world and its audience. The name comes from the box set of realist theater: a room with three walls on stage and an invisible fourth where the audience sits. The actors behave as if that wall were solid and never acknowledge the people watching. **Breaking the fourth wall** means a character or a work openly acknowledges the audience, or that it is fiction.

The break is older than the convention. In ancient Greek comedy the chorus stepped forward to address the audience directly, and Shakespeare's soliloquies and asides let characters confide in the house. In the 18th century Denis Diderot told actors to imagine a wall at the front of the stage. Bertolt Brecht later broke it on purpose, using direct address and visible stage machinery so that audiences would think critically instead of losing themselves in the story.

Breaks come in degrees:

- **Direct address.** A character speaks to the camera, as in *Ferris Bueller's Day Off*, *House of Cards*, or *Fleabag*, which used a glance at the lens to make the audience a confidant.
- **Self-awareness.** Characters comment on the conventions of their genre or know they are fictional, as Deadpool does in comics.
- **Metafiction.** The work makes its own construction a subject, as in Italo Calvino's *If on a winter's night a traveler*, written partly in the second person about the reader reading it; see [[Unreliable Narrator]].

Games can break the wall in ways other media can't, because the player is present and the software can reach outside the story. In *Metal Gear Solid* (1998), a villain reads the player's memory card and comments on the other games saved there, and the fight is won by plugging the controller into the second port so he can no longer "read your mind." *Undertale* and *Doki Doki Literature Club* remember actions across restarts and deleted saves, making the player complicit; see [[Interactive Fiction]] and [[Immersive Sim]].

A related distinction in games is between what exists in the world and what exists only for the player, such as a health bar. Designers choose between showing information inside the fiction, on a wristwatch or a dashboard, and overlaying it on the screen; see [[Diegesis]].

Other forms depend on keeping the wall up. Professional wrestling built a whole culture around never admitting the show is staged; see [[Kayfabe]]. Film editing relies on the audience not noticing its cuts; see [[Kuleshov Effect]]. Breaking the wall too often cheapens it.

Performers who talk straight to the camera get some of the same effect without any fiction at all, and audiences come to feel they know them; see [[Parasocial Relationship]].
