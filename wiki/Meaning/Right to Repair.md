**Right to repair** is the movement to guarantee owners and independent repair shops the parts, tools, schematics, and diagnostic software that manufacturers already give their own authorized technicians. The claim is narrow on paper — access, not a right to any particular design — but it runs against several manufacturer practices built up over decades.

**Parts pairing** is the sharpest current obstacle: a replacement component, even a genuine one, is serialized to the specific unit it came from, and firmware refuses to fully recognize a swapped part until authorized software re-pairs it. **Firmware locks** restrict what a repaired device will do at all, independent of the physical fix. **Adhesive construction** — batteries and screens bonded rather than fastened — trades a thinner device for a repair that risks damaging the part it replaces. **Warranty threats**, including stickers warning that opening a case voids coverage, have long pressured owners toward authorized channels even where such conditioning is legally unenforceable.

Legislative movement has been real: several jurisdictions now require manufacturers to sell parts, tools, and documentation to owners and independent shops on fair terms, following advocacy that functioned as sustained [[Collective Action]] against a well-resourced industry. Manufacturers' objections deserve a fair hearing: some repairs on lithium batteries or laser assemblies carry genuine safety risk done wrong, and firmware locks are sometimes framed as security against counterfeit components entering the supply chain, the same logic used to justify [[Code Signing]] elsewhere. Critics respond that these justifications also protect a repair monopoly, an incentive structure close to [[Rent-Seeking]], and that the resulting e-waste is an [[Externality]] borne by nobody who profits from it. Both things can be true of the same lock.
