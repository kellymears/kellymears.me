"The Death of the Author" is a 1967 essay by the French critic Roland Barthes arguing that a text's meaning doesn't belong to its author. Once written, a work is a weave of language and older texts, and its unity lies "not in its origin but in its destination," the reader. Barthes closed by declaring that "the birth of the reader must be at the cost of the death of the Author."

He was attacking a style of criticism that explained works through their authors' lives and intentions, asking what the writer meant, what happened to them, and what they believed. For Barthes, this put an artificial limit on a text, closing it to a single correct reading.

He had predecessors. In 1946 the American critics W. K. Wimsatt and Monroe Beardsley called it the **intentional fallacy** to judge a poem by what its author meant rather than by what it says. In 1969 Michel Foucault asked "What Is an Author?" and described the author as a function that readers and institutions use to group, value, and police texts.

The idea fits how communication works generally. Listeners always build meaning from what is said plus context, often inferring more than was intended; see [[Implicature]]. Misheard lyrics show audiences producing meanings the singer never put there; see [[Mondegreen]]. Film editing shows viewers supplying meaning between shots; see [[Kuleshov Effect]]. Narrators who can't be trusted make the reader's own judgment part of the work; see [[Unreliable Narrator]].

The argument now plays out in fandoms:

- **Word of God.** Fans use this term for an author's statements outside the work, in interviews or on social media, about what happened or what characters are like. When such statements add information that the text never showed, many readers resist treating them as binding; see [[Retcon]].
- **Canon and fan interpretation.** Readers build readings, and sometimes whole fan works, that diverge from the author's, and debate how much authority the creator has over a shared world.
- **Separating art from artist.** When creators behave badly, audiences ask whether the work can be judged apart from its maker. The author's reputation colors a reading whether or not critics think it should; see [[Halo Effect]].

Critics of Barthes point out that authors exist and that intentions are evidence, often useful, about meaning. Ignoring context can produce readings that are clever but wrong, especially for older texts whose words have shifted; see [[Semantic Change]]. Most practicing critics take a middle position: the author's intent is one piece of evidence, not the final word.

The essay itself has become a shorthand far beyond literary theory, much as a strongly felt relationship with a creator can obscure the work; see [[Parasocial Relationship]].
