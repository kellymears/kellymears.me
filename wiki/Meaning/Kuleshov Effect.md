The **Kuleshov Effect** is the finding that alternating a neutral shot of a face with different second shots makes an audience read a different emotion into the same face each time — the meaning is manufactured by the juxtaposition and exists in neither shot alone.

The founding experiment is attributed to Kuleshov in the early 1920s, but the standard account of it comes from one source: Pudovkin's *Film Technique* (1929), written years after the fact and the secondhand basis for essentially everything known about the demonstration. Pudovkin gives an unchanging close-up intercut with a plate of soup, a girl in a coffin, and a little girl playing with a toy bear, read by viewers as hunger, sorrow, and joy. Other retellings substitute a woman on a divan and read the third as desire — and that divergence is itself evidence of how thin the record is. The footage does not survive, and no audience response was measured at the time. Repeating a claim this old as settled fact is worth flagging on its own.

Later attempts to test the effect under controlled conditions found something real but smaller and more conditional than the legend implies: the illusion depends heavily on how ambiguous the face already is, and weakens with a face that already reads as clearly emotional. The strong version — any face, any context, reliably reprogrammed — has not survived replication.

What the effect demonstrates regardless is that editing is authorship, not assembly: a cut doesn't merely join footage, it manufactures an inference the audience supplies and attributes to the footage rather than to the cut. [[Environmental Storytelling]] runs on the same mechanism with objects instead of shots, and both depend on the trust a viewer extends to [[Ground Truth]] — that a camera, or a room, is showing them something real. Sequence, not content, does the interpretive work here, meaning built from [[Diegesis]] rather than added to it, and a standing caution about [[Plausible Mechanism]] explanations that outrun their evidence.
