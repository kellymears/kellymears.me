A **dog whistle** is coded language that sounds ordinary to most listeners but carries an extra, usually more extreme, message for a particular group, much as a dog whistle's pitch is audible to dogs but not to people. The speaker reaches supporters who know the code while keeping plausible deniability with everyone else.

It works through implication rather than statement. The words literally say something acceptable, and the second meaning comes from context and shared history that the hearer must supply; see [[Implicature]]. Because the coded meaning is never said aloud, accusing the speaker of it looks paranoid to outsiders, and that asymmetry is the point.

Common forms:

- **Historical echoes.** Phrases tied to past movements, such as "states' rights" in US politics, which evoked segregation for audiences who remembered how it had been used.
- **Insider references.** Numbers, symbols, and phrases that circulate within a subculture and mean nothing elsewhere; see [[Shibboleth]].
- **Euphemism.** Neutral-sounding words standing in for groups of people, so that criticism of an abstract category is heard as criticism of a specific people; see [[Euphemism Treadmill]].

Dog whistles let a politician hold together a coalition with different views. The general public hears a moderate message, and the in-group hears loyalty. The political scientist Tali Mendelberg's work on implicit racial appeals found that such messages moved voters precisely because they weren't recognized; once pointed out explicitly, they lost much of their effect.

Detecting dog whistles is genuinely hard, and the concept is easy to misuse. Since a dog whistle is defined by hidden intent, almost any phrase can be accused of being one. People on every side of a debate use the charge to dismiss opponents, so careful use asks for evidence that the code is actually shared, not merely imaginable.

The internet changed the pattern in two ways. Subcultures coin codes faster than outsiders can learn them, so moderators and their filters are always behind. And because anything said online can be screenshotted and shown to any audience, codes leak and get decoded in public, a form of [[Context Collapse]]. Once a code is widely known it stops working as a whistle and becomes an open signal. Publicizing an obscure symbol to condemn it can also spread it further; see [[Streisand Effect]] and [[Moral Panic]].

Neighboring practices lack the hidden intent. Speakers shift registers for different audiences all the time; see [[Code-Switching]]. Diplomats and companies use strategic ambiguity, leaving a statement open to several readings on purpose so each party can hear what it needs; see [[Speech Act]].

The tactic lives at the edges of acceptable speech, and it tends to move them; see [[Overton Window]].
