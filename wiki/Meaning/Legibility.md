**Legibility** is the property a system has when it can be counted, mapped, and administered from a distance. James C. Scott's account in *Seeing Like a State* traces the mechanism through cases that share a shape: a cadastral survey turns overlapping customary land claims into a single owner and a parcel number; permanent surnames turn a village's nicknames and patronymics into a taxable, conscriptable roster; scientific forestry turns a forest into even rows of one fast-growing species, countable per hectare; standard weights and measures replace units that varied by trade and locality with ones a capital can audit. Each move makes the thing visible to whoever governs it, and each discards something Scott calls *metis* — a term he takes from classical Greek, crediting Detienne and Vernant, for the tacit, local, practice-based knowledge that made the original arrangement work.

The failure is not the simplification itself but mistaking it for understanding. A scientific forest that ignores undergrowth, soil fungus, and windbreak looked, on the map, like an improvement on the tangled original, and the first crop grew excellently. With rotations of about eighty years, the damage showed only in the second generation, roughly a century on: marked retrogression, production down 20 to 30 percent. The lag matters as much as the loss, because the failure arrives too late to indict the plan. High-modernist planning fails hardest when this blindness pairs with the unchecked authority to act on it at scale.

The pattern recurs below the level of the state. A [[Taxonomy]] built purely for administrative retrieval can do to a body of knowledge what the survey did to land: easy to query, silently missing the [[Naming]] a practitioner relies on. Once such a system exists, reversing it is costly and rare — see [[Path Dependence]] — and a registry nobody maintains drifts from the reality it describes, [[Documentation Rot]] at the scale of a country rather than a codebase.

This is the social-scale sibling of [[Goodhart's Law]]: the same simplification that makes a system governable is what makes it gameable, because the map, not the territory, is what gets optimized against. [[Information Architecture]] and [[Plain Language]] are legibility used well, when the simplification is checked against what the reader needs rather than only what the administrator needs.
