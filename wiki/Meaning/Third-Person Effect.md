The **third-person effect** is the tendency to believe that persuasive or harmful media content — advertising, propaganda, violent content, disinformation — has a much stronger effect on other people than it has on oneself, even when exposure is identical. Sociologist W. Phillips Davison named and formalized it in 1983, noting the asymmetry as a consistent finding across surveys: nearly everyone rates themselves as more resistant to influence than "the average viewer," which is a statistical impossibility if taken as a claim about the actual population.

The mechanism is a comparison built on unequal information: a person has direct, felt access to their own reasoning process — they can point to the specific reasons they weren't swayed — while other people's reactions are known only from the outside, as inferred, generic vulnerability. This pairs with a self-serving motivation, since believing oneself unusually savvy is flattering, and with a straightforward status hierarchy in how the belief distributes: people typically rate themselves as least affected, people close to them as somewhat more affected, and a socially distant "general public" as most affected of all, a gradient that tracks perceived similarity rather than any real measurement of susceptibility.

The effect has real behavioral consequences precisely because policy and censorship debates get argued in exactly the terms it predicts: support for restricting pornography, violent video games, or political advertising correlates strongly with how much influence a person attributes to it on *others*, not on any measured influence on themselves — so the case for a content restriction is often made entirely from a belief that everyone besides the person making the case needs protecting. It sits close to the [[Halo Effect]] in structure — both substitute a flattering, effortless judgment for a harder, evidence-based one — but the third-person effect specifically concerns *susceptibility to influence* rather than any other trait.
