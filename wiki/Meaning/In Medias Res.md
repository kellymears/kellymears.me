**In medias res** is Latin for "into the middle of things." A story told this way opens in the midst of events, with the backstory supplied later through flashback, dialogue, or inference. The reader arrives late to a situation already in motion and has to catch up.

The phrase comes from Horace's Ars Poetica, a verse letter on writing from around 19 BCE. Horace praised Homer for hurrying the listener into the middle of the story, and contrasted it with beginning **ab ovo**, from the egg. The egg was Leda's, from which Helen hatched; a poet starting there would cover the whole background of the Trojan War before reaching the fighting. The Iliad instead opens in the war's tenth year, with a quarrel between two Greek commanders. The Odyssey begins near the end of its hero's travels, and much of the earlier journey is told later as a story within the story; see [[Diegesis]].

The technique works on curiosity. A scene whose causes are hidden raises questions, and questions pull a reader forward; see [[Curiosity]]. An unresolved situation also tends to stay on the mind; see [[Zeigarnik Effect]]. Viewers infer a great deal from context, filling in relationships and stakes from a few cues; see [[Kuleshov Effect]].

It also solves a practical problem. Opening with background, the history of a kingdom or a character's childhood, asks for patience before the reader has any reason to care. Writers who know their world well tend to overestimate how much of it the audience needs up front; see [[Curse of Knowledge]]. Starting late lets background arrive only when it matters, a principle interface designers apply to information too; see [[Progressive Disclosure]].

Film and television use it constantly. Many crime films open on the body and then rewind. Television's **cold open**, a scene before the opening titles, often drops viewers into a crisis or a moment of comedy with no setup. Games often begin with a dramatic scene from late in the story and then jump back, giving players a taste of what they are working toward; see [[Environmental Storytelling]].

Starting in the middle has costs. If the opening is too confusing, readers may not stay long enough to be rewarded. Heavy reliance on flashbacks can drain momentum, and a flash-forward can deflate tension by revealing who survives. Some stories exploit the gap, letting what the reader assumes about the opening scene prove wrong once the background arrives; see [[Unreliable Narrator]] and [[Rashomon Effect]].

Where a story starts shapes what it seems to be about. The same events told from the egg read as a chronicle; told from the middle, they read as a mystery; see [[Hero's Journey]].
