**Kayfabe** is professional wrestling's maintained fiction that scripted rivalries, injuries, and outcomes are genuine contests, sustained collectively by performers who stay in character outside the ring and by an audience that agrees to receive the performance as real.

For most of the twentieth century, kayfabe was enforced as an industry-wide practice rather than a narrative device: performers gave interviews in character, feuds were denied as staged even to the press, and the business's workings were treated as a trade secret rather than a spoiler, immune to outside [[Falsifiability]]. The audience's participation was less naivety than consented suspension — enough spectators understood the matches were choreographed that the fiction functioned as [[Diegesis]] maintained by mutual agreement rather than by successful deception.

Kayfabe eroded gradually — through insider journalism and eventually the promotions' own marketing — but the pivotal admission was official and dateable: Vince McMahon's 1989 testimony to the New Jersey state senate, seeking exemption from athletic-commission regulation by conceding that the matches were entertainment rather than sport. That was a legislative move, not litigation, and after it the staging was an open secret. What replaced it is closer to the frame television now uses for competition generally: audiences track "worked" storylines and "shoot" moments as genre knowledge, enjoying the artifice as craft rather than believing the premise — knowing a trick is a trick without its ceasing to be worth watching.

The term migrated outward to describe any arrangement where insiders know a contest is staged for effect while the audience is invited to read it as sincere, used loosely of political theatre and marketing campaigns that stage opposition or urgency. The migration flatters everyone involved more than it should: unlike wrestling, most of what gets called kayfabe outside it was never openly acknowledged as performance by its own participants, which lets the performers claim craft and the audience claim savvy, when what's happening may be closer to a [[Confidence Trick]] than a knowing show, or indistinguishable in practice from [[Astroturfing]] staged to look like organic reaction.
