A **dialect** is a variety of a language associated with a particular region or social group, with its own vocabulary, grammar and pronunciation. An **accent** is narrower, referring only to pronunciation. Everyone speaks a dialect and has an accent, including speakers of the variety considered standard. Linguists treat all dialects as equally systematic and rule-governed; the ranking of some as correct and others as sloppy is social, not linguistic.

Dialect differences are easy to map. In the United States, the words people use for a sweet carbonated drink, such as soda, pop or coke, divide the country into regions. Lines on such maps, called isoglosses, mark where one feature gives way to another, and bundles of them tend to follow old settlement patterns and geographic barriers. Social dialects cut across regions, tied to class, ethnicity or age. African American English, for example, has consistent grammatical rules of its own, such as a habitual "be" for repeated actions, which outsiders often mistake for errors.

The line between a dialect and a language is famously blurry. A saying popularized by the Yiddish linguist Max Weinreich holds that "a language is a dialect with an army and a navy." **Mutual intelligibility**, whether speakers understand one another, doesn't settle it. Speakers of Norwegian, Swedish and Danish can largely understand each other but speak separate national languages, while Mandarin and Cantonese, both called Chinese, are not mutually intelligible in speech. In a **dialect continuum**, neighboring villages understand each other but the ends of the chain don't, as once stretched across the Dutch and German speaking regions; see [[Prototype Theory]].

Which dialect becomes the standard is usually a matter of power. The speech of the capital, the court or the printing industry gets codified in dictionaries and taught in schools, then treated as the language itself; see [[Diglossia]], [[Linguistic Prescriptivism]] and [[Imagined Community]]. Britain's Received Pronunciation, once the accent of public schools and the BBC, was spoken natively by only a small minority.

People adjust their dialect depending on audience, shifting toward the standard at work and back at home; see [[Code-Switching]]. Dialect also marks belonging, which is why a single pronunciation can reveal an outsider; see [[Shibboleth]] and [[Social Identity Theory]]. Accent bias affects hiring and how credible speakers are judged, and technology can repeat it. Speech recognition systems have historically made more errors on dialects underrepresented in their training data; see [[Automatic Speech Recognition]].

Dialects change and die. Contact with other languages brings borrowed words and sometimes new languages altogether; see [[Loanword]] and [[Creole Language]]. Mass media and migration have leveled many regional varieties, and the small dialects of isolated communities are disappearing along with endangered languages; see [[Language Death]].
