**Sortition** is choosing people for public office by lot rather than by election. It sounds like a curiosity, but for much of history it was considered the most democratic way to fill positions, and versions of it are used in many countries today.

Ancient Athens relied on it heavily. In the fifth and fourth centuries BCE, most Athenian officials, including the Council of 500 that prepared business for the assembly, were chosen at random from citizens who put their names forward, and juries were selected by lot as well. Athenians used a stone device called a kleroterion, which assigned citizens' tokens to slots and used colored balls to pick rows. Elections were reserved for roles needing particular expertise, such as generals. Aristotle described choosing officials by lot as democratic and electing them as oligarchic, since elections favor the prominent and wealthy.

Later republics mixed methods. Medieval Venice elected its doge through an elaborate sequence alternating lotteries and votes, designed to make bribery and factional control impractical. Several Italian city-states used lots for some offices.

Modern sortition survives most visibly in juries, whose members are drawn at random from the population. Since the 1970s and 1980s, researchers and reformers have experimented with **citizens' assemblies**: randomly selected, demographically balanced groups that hear evidence, deliberate, and make recommendations. Notable cases:

- **British Columbia, 2004.** An assembly proposed a new electoral system. It won 57.7 percent in a 2005 referendum, short of the 60 percent required; see [[Ranked-Choice Voting]].
- **Ireland, 2016 to 2018.** A citizens' assembly recommended liberalizing the constitutional ban on abortion, and voters approved a change in a 2018 referendum. An earlier convention, two-thirds of whose members were chosen at random, had recommended allowing same-sex marriage, approved by referendum in 2015.

Supporters argue that random selection:

- produces bodies that resemble the public, not just the politically ambitious;
- resists capture by donors and lobbies, since members can't be predicted in advance; see [[Regulatory Capture]] and [[Rent-Seeking]];
- frees members from campaigning and reelection; see [[Iron Law of Oligarchy]];
- draws on varied knowledge and perspectives; see [[Wisdom of the Crowd]].

Critics worry about competence, accountability to no electorate, and how much influence the organizers and expert witnesses exert. Assemblies' recommendations also depend on whether elected officials choose to act on them.

Selecting by lot needs a trustworthy random process; see [[Pseudorandom Number Generator]]. Well-run assemblies also guard against the pressures that afflict any group deliberating together; see [[Groupthink]] and [[Delphi Method]].

Theorists note that elections face mathematical limits of their own; see [[Arrow's Impossibility Theorem]]. Sortition sidesteps voting on representatives entirely, though not the question of how a group decides; see [[Collective Action]].
