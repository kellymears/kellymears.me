The **Matthew effect** is the tendency for advantage to compound: those who already have recognition, resources, or attention tend to gain more, while those with little fall further behind. Sociologist Robert Merton named it in 1968 after a line in the Gospel of Matthew: "For to every one who has will more be given, and he will have abundance; but from him who has not, even what he has will be taken away."

Merton was describing science. He and Harriet Zuckerman found that well-known scientists get disproportionate credit for work they share with lesser-known colleagues. Famous researchers get more citations, more grants, and better students, which produce more work and more fame. Small early differences in luck or timing grow into large gaps in reputation.

The general mechanism is called **cumulative advantage**, and it shows up widely:

- **Wealth.** Returns on capital accumulate, so those who start with more grow it faster.
- **Education.** Children who read well early read more and learn faster, widening the gap each year.
- **Culture.** In a 2006 experiment, Matthew Salganik, Peter Dodds, and Duncan Watts built online music markets where listeners could see download counts. Popularity snowballed unpredictably: the same songs became hits in one world and flops in another, while in a control world without visible counts outcomes were more even. [[Social Proof]] turned small early leads into large ones.
- **Networks.** In the 1999 model of Albert-László Barabási and Réka Albert, new nodes link preferentially to already well-connected ones. This **preferential attachment** produces scale-free networks with a few huge hubs and a long tail, the [[Power law]] distributions behind the [[Pareto Principle]]. It describes web pages, which rank higher in search because they already have many [[Backlink]] references, and social media accounts.
- **Platforms.** Products with more users attract more users, the [[Network Effect]], so markets tip toward one winner.

Margaret Rossiter named the **Matilda effect** in 1993, after suffragist Matilda Joslyn Gage, for the related pattern in which women scientists' contributions are credited to men or overlooked. Cumulative advantage tends to amplify existing biases, so whoever gets the first boost, for whatever reason, keeps it.

The effect has an uncomfortable implication: success in such systems is partly luck amplified. That challenges the assumption that the most successful people or products are simply the best, an error close to [[Survivorship Bias]] and the [[Halo Effect]]. It also bears on how research gets published, since prominent labs get easier review, a contributor to [[Publication Bias]].

Counterweights exist. Blind review, lotteries for grant funding among qualified applicants, anti-monopoly rules, progressive taxation, and early-years education all try to limit runaway advantage. [[Path Dependence]] describes the larger family: early events lock in outcomes that would be hard to reach from scratch.
