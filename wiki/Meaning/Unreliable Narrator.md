An **unreliable narrator** is a storyteller whose version of events can't be fully trusted. The critic Wayne C. Booth named the device in *The Rhetoric of Fiction* (1961), describing narrators whose values or account differ from what the work as a whole suggests. The reader has to read past the narration to work out what actually happened, and much of the pleasure lies in that gap.

Narrators can be unreliable in different ways:

- **Liars.** They deliberately mislead the reader, often to hide their own guilt.
- **Self-deceivers.** They believe what they say but misunderstand themselves. Stevens, the butler in Kazuo Ishiguro's *The Remains of the Day* (1989), describes his loyal service in dignified terms while readers gradually see what it cost him.
- **The naive.** Children or innocents report accurately but miss the meaning, like Huck Finn describing slavery without seeing its cruelty.
- **The disturbed.** Narrators whose perception is distorted by madness, drugs, or grief, like the narrator of Edgar Allan Poe's "The Tell-Tale Heart," who insists on his sanity while describing a murder.

The most notorious case is Agatha Christie's *The Murder of Roger Ackroyd* (1926), in which the narrator turns out to be the murderer. Every statement he makes is technically true, but he leaves out the crucial moments. Some readers and critics called it cheating, and the novel became central to debates over whether mysteries should give readers a fair chance to solve them; see [[Fair-Play Mystery]].

Film and games add their own versions. Akira Kurosawa's *Rashomon* (1950) shows the same killing told by four witnesses with incompatible accounts. Flashbacks in films can lie, which audiences tend to resist because they read images as objective; see [[Kuleshov Effect]] and [[Diegesis]]. Games can make the player complicit: the story may reveal that the player character, whose view the player shares, has misunderstood or hidden something, and the rules can even work to hide it; see [[Ludonarrative Dissonance]] and [[Interactive Fiction]]. [[Disco Elysium]] voices the main character's own mental skills as narrators, some of them confidently wrong.

The device works because readers normally trust narrators. Stories cue readers to doubt through contradictions, details that don't fit, other characters' reactions, or a voice that protests too much. Planted clues pay off on rereading; see [[Chekhov's Gun]].

Unreliable narration resonates because everyday memory is unreliable too. People reconstruct events each time they recall them, filling gaps with expectation, and sincerely report things that never happened; see [[False Memory]]. They also explain their own behavior generously and others' harshly; see [[Fundamental Attribution Error]]. Pretended reliability can even be part of a shared game, as in wrestling storylines; see [[Kayfabe]].
