**Context collapse** is the flattening of many separate audiences into one. Offline, people speak differently to friends, parents, coworkers, and strangers, and those audiences rarely overlap. On social media, a single post can reach all of them at once, along with people the author never imagined. There's no way to choose the right tone for everyone, so people either speak to one audience and risk offending another, or speak blandly to all of them.

Researcher danah boyd developed the concept in her work on teenagers and social media in the 2000s, and in 2011 she and Alice Marwick described how users write for an **imagined audience**, a mental picture of who is reading that is usually narrower than who actually reads.

The idea builds on sociologist Erving Goffman's 1959 *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life*, which described social life as performance. People present different selves on different "stages," with a backstage where they relax. Context collapse removes the walls between stages, so the backstage joke ends up in front of the boss. Adjusting to audience is ordinary, described as [[Code-Switching]], but it depends on knowing the audience.

Common consequences include:

- **Misreading.** Irony, in-jokes, and community slang are read literally by outsiders. [[Poe's Law]] observes that sincere extremism and its parody can be impossible to tell apart without context.
- **Time collapse.** Old posts are read years later by people with different norms, sometimes ending careers.
- **Pile-ons.** A post meant for a few hundred followers is shared to millions and judged by people who know nothing of its author, and attempts to delete it can backfire through the [[Streisand Effect]].
- **Self-censorship.** Knowing anyone might read, people post less or more cautiously, a pressure related to the [[Spiral of Silence]].

Privacy scholar Helen Nissenbaum offers a related framework, **contextual integrity**: information flows carry norms from the context where they arose, and privacy is violated when information moves into a context with different norms. A medical detail shared with a doctor is fine; the same detail sold to an employer is not. Much of the harm from collecting [[Personal Data]] is information moving across contexts.

People find ways to rebuild walls. Private accounts, close-friends lists, alternate accounts, and the shift from public feeds to group chats all restore separate audiences. Smaller, bounded communities may be easier to speak in, consistent with [[Dunbar's Number]] and the limits on how many people we can know well. The opposite problem, a public figure's audience feeling they know them personally, is the [[Parasocial Relationship]].

For autistic and other neurodivergent people, who may already work hard at reading audiences, context collapse adds pressure to keep up [[Masking]] across every audience at once.
