A **writing system** is a set of visible marks used to record a language, so that a message can outlast speech and travel without the speaker. Unlike speech, which every human community develops, writing has been invented from scratch only a handful of times. Most scholars accept at least three independent inventions: in Mesopotamia, in China and in Mesoamerica. Egyptian hieroglyphs may be a fourth, or may have been inspired by Mesopotamian contact.

The oldest known writing, Sumerian **cuneiform**, emerged around 3200 BCE from accounting. Clay tokens and tallies tracking grain, livestock and debts developed into impressed signs on clay tablets, and only later came to record names, laws and literature; see [[Double-Entry Bookkeeping]]. Chinese writing appears on oracle bones from around 1200 BCE and remains in use today in a continuous line. Maya script recorded dynastic histories and astronomical calendars.

Writing systems fall into rough types. A **logogram** stands for a word or meaningful unit, as in Chinese characters, which is why readers of mutually unintelligible Chinese dialects can share a written language; see [[Dialect]]. A **syllabary** has a sign for each syllable, as with Japanese kana or the Cherokee syllabary created by Sequoyah around 1821, one of the few writing systems invented by a single person. An **alphabet** represents individual sounds. The Phoenicians wrote only consonants, a system called an abjad and still used for Arabic and Hebrew. Greeks added letters for vowels, giving the first full alphabet, ancestor of the Latin and Cyrillic scripts. Many scripts of South and Southeast Asia, such as Devanagari, mark vowels as modifications of consonant signs. Most real systems mix types: English spelling preserves pronunciations that changed centuries ago.

Lost scripts have been deciphered by careful inference. The Rosetta Stone, carrying the same decree in Greek, demotic and hieroglyphs, let Jean-François Champollion crack Egyptian writing in 1822. Michael Ventris showed in 1952 that Linear B recorded an early form of Greek. Others, such as the Indus script, remain unread.

Writing reshaped thought and memory. In Plato's Phaedrus, Socrates warns that writing will weaken memory, a complaint echoed in debates about every later technology that moves knowledge out of the head; see [[Cognitive offloading]]. The forms of letters were shaped by tools, from reed pens to chisels, then by printing; see [[Calligraphy]], [[Movable Type]] and [[Typeface]]. Scripts that run right to left or vertically complicate software, and today the Unicode standard assigns codes to well over a hundred scripts; see [[Character Encoding]] and [[Internationalization]]. Picture characters have even returned in digital form; see [[Emoji]].

Many languages have never been written, and some first acquire a script only as they near extinction; see [[Language Death]].
