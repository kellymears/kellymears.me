A **retronym** is a new name given to something old because a newer version has taken over its original name. Before electric guitars, a guitar was just a *guitar*; once electric guitars became common, the original needed a qualifier, and *acoustic guitar* was born. The term was coined by Frank Mankiewicz, a journalist and political aide, and popularized by the columnist William Safire in 1980.

Retronyms are a record of technological and social change. Each marks the moment a new thing became the default:

- **Landline.** A phone was a phone until mobile phones arrived.
- **Analog watch, analog clock.** Needed only after digital displays.
- **Snail mail.** Coined once email became normal.
- **Paper book, print book.** After e-books.
- **Silent film.** Before sound films, films were simply films.
- **World War I.** Called the Great War until there was a second.
- **Brick-and-mortar store.** After online shopping.
- **Natural turf, real Christmas tree, cloth diaper, whole milk, film camera.**

Computing generates them quickly because it replaces things quickly. *Desktop computer* became necessary with laptops; *wired* with wireless; *physical keyboard* with touchscreens; *on-premises* with the cloud; *bare metal* with virtual machines; *native app* with web apps. *Human translator* and *human-written* are recent additions, as machine-produced text became common enough to need distinguishing.

Retronyms reveal what a culture assumes. The unmarked word names the default, and the modifier marks the exception. When *mail* starts to mean email and paper mail needs the qualifier, the default has shifted. Linguists call this *markedness*: the plain form is unmarked and expected, the qualified form marked and unusual. Watching which form needs a modifier is a way to track what has become normal.

Some retronyms carry judgment. *Natural*, *real*, and *organic* imply the original is more authentic, a framing used heavily in marketing. Others are neutral labels for coexisting options.

Related naming phenomena:

- **Backronyms** invent an expansion for an existing word, as if it had been an acronym all along; see [[Backronym]].
- **Skeuomorphs** carry old forms into new technology, such as a floppy-disk save icon; see [[Skeuomorphism]]. Retronyms are the linguistic counterpart: the old thing gets renamed while the new thing keeps the old name.
- **Semantic change** is the broader process by which words shift meaning; retronyms are one of its traces; see [[Semantic Change]].

In software, the same pattern shows up in naming versions. When a successor takes the main name, the original gets a suffix such as *classic* or *legacy*, and documentation, APIs, and teams have to adjust; see [[Naming]] and [[Deprecation]].
