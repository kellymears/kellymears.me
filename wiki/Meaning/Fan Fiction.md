**Fan fiction** is writing by fans that borrows the characters, settings, or premises of a story someone else created. It might continue a series past its ending, explore a minor character, imagine a romance the original never allowed, or move familiar characters into a different world entirely. Most of it is written for free and shared openly.

Retelling other people's stories is ancient; much of world literature is reworked myth. The modern form took shape in science fiction fandom. In 1967, fans of a television space series produced a printed fanzine containing stories about its characters, and similar zines circulated by mail through the 1970s and 1980s. Internet archives arrived in the late 1990s, and in 2007 fans founded a nonprofit to build an archive of their own, opened in 2009, run by volunteers and governed by its users; see [[Commons-Based Peer Production]]. That archive later won a major science fiction award for best related work.

Fan fiction has its own vocabulary. **Canon** is the source material. An **alternate universe** story places the characters in a different setting, such as a coffee shop instead of a starship. **Shipping** imagines romantic pairings. **Fix-it** fic rewrites an ending the fans disliked. Short pieces, long novels, and everything between share the same archives. Large archives rely on fans tagging works with characters, relationships, and tropes, a crowd-built classification system maintained by volunteer editors; see [[Folksonomy]].

The culture runs mostly on gifts. Writers post work for free, and readers repay with comments and recognition; see [[Gift Economy]] and [[Social Capital]]. Communities form around shared tastes, often across continents, held together by imagined ties to people they'll never meet; see [[Imagined Community]]. Attachment to the characters themselves fuels much of the writing; see [[Parasocial Relationship]].

Its legal status is unsettled. Copyright protects characters and stories, and authors' attitudes vary widely, from encouragement to cease-and-desist letters. Advocates argue that noncommercial, transformative work should count as fair use. Once a work enters the public domain, derivative writing becomes plainly legal and sometimes commercially successful; see [[Public Domain]]. Fan fiction occasionally crosses into publishing; a bestselling erotic romance trilogy of the 2010s began as fan fiction for a vampire series.

Fan fiction treats the reader as a co-creator, an idea literary theory had already advanced; see [[Death of the Author]]. Games have a parallel tradition, where players remake and extend the works they love; see [[Game Modding]]. Many professional writers began by writing in someone else's world, learning structure and voice with characters already in hand. Fans often scrutinize source material closely enough to catch every contradiction a later sequel introduces; see [[Retcon]].
