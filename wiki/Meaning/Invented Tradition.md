An **invented tradition** is a practice that presents itself as ancient but was created recently, often within living memory, and claims continuity with a past that it largely reshapes. The historians Eric Hobsbawm and Terence Ranger collected case studies in *The Invention of Tradition* (1983), which made the phrase standard.

The book's most famous chapter, by Hugh Trevor-Roper, concerns the Scottish Highlands:

- **The kilt.** The short kilt, separate from the earlier belted plaid, appears to have been popularized in the 1720s by an English industrialist, Thomas Rawlinson, whose ironworkers found it practical.
- **Clan tartans.** The idea that each clan had its own pattern was largely systematized in the nineteenth century, partly through a book of supposedly ancient clan patterns published in 1842 by two brothers who claimed royal Stuart descent; it was largely a forgery.
- **The royal visit.** King George IV's 1822 visit to Edinburgh, staged by the novelist Walter Scott, filled the city with tartan pageantry that fixed the image of Highland dress for Scotland as a whole.

Other chapters showed that much British royal ceremony, which looks ancient on television, was elaborated in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries as the monarchy lost political power and gained a symbolic role. Many familiar Christmas customs, including the decorated tree, spread through Britain and America in the Victorian era. Tarot's reputation as an ancient occult text began with an eighteenth-century French writer; see [[Tarot]].

Hobsbawm argued that traditions get invented most often when societies change quickly. New nations, industrial workforces, and colonial administrations needed rituals that made recent arrangements feel natural and permanent; see [[Imagined Community]]. Traditions also invent their own origin stories, much as words acquire false histories; see [[Folk Etymology]] and [[Backronym]].

Being invented doesn't make a tradition fraudulent or worthless. Every tradition started somewhere, and a practice can be meaningful to the people who keep it regardless of its age; see [[Ship of Theseus]]. What distinguishes the invented kind is the claim of antiquity, which lends authority and discourages change. The claim often feeds on a longing for a past that never quite existed; see [[Nostalgia]].

The idea suggests some caution with arguments from custom. "We've always done it this way" may describe something a few decades old. Yet a practice that has lasted has passed some test, and it is worth knowing why it exists before abolishing it; see [[Chesterton's Fence]] and [[Lindy Effect]]. Fictional worlds revise their histories in the same way, presenting new backstory as if it had always been there; see [[Retcon]].

Rituals that mark major life changes are among the most commonly reinvented; see [[Rite of Passage]].
