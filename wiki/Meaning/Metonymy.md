**Metonymy** is referring to something by naming a thing associated with it. "The White House announced" means the president's staff. "Hollywood" means the American film industry. "The crown" means the monarchy, and "the press" means journalists. A server in a diner might tell a colleague that the ham sandwich at table six wants the check.

**Synecdoche** is the narrower case in which a part stands for the whole, or the whole for a part. "All hands on deck" asks for sailors, not hands. "Nice wheels" praises a car. "England won" means eleven players did. The Latin label **pars pro toto**, part for whole, is used for the same move, especially in naming places, as when "Holland" is used for the whole Netherlands.

Metonymy is easily confused with metaphor, and the difference is in the link. Metaphor connects things through similarity, calling an argument a battle because the two share a structure; see [[Conceptual Metaphor]]. Metonymy connects things that are found together, so the reference travels along an existing association. The linguist Roman Jakobson argued in 1956 that these are two basic poles of language, observing that some people with aphasia lost the ability to substitute similar words while others lost the ability to combine words in sequence, and suggesting that literary styles lean toward one pole or the other.

George Lakoff and Mark Johnson, in Metaphors We Live By, from 1980, treated metonymy as a habit of thought as well as speech. Saying "we need some new faces around here" picks out people by the part used to recognize them. Saying "Wall Street is nervous" puts institutions in place of the people in them, which can obscure who is actually acting. Interpreting such phrases relies on the listener inferring the intended reference from context; see [[Implicature]].

Metonymy is one of the main forces behind changes in word meaning; see [[Semantic Change]]. "Bureau" comes from a French word for the coarse cloth covering a desk; it came to mean the desk, then the office, then the agency. "Board" went from a plank to the table a council sat at to the council itself. Polite words for awkward subjects are often metonymies, naming the room rather than what happens in it, and they tend to wear out; see [[Euphemism Treadmill]].

Interface design leans on the same move. An envelope stands for email, a magnifying glass for search, a gear for settings, each naming a task by an object involved in it; see [[Iconography]]. Some of these symbols outlive their objects, like the floppy disk that still means save to people who have never seen one; see [[Skeuomorphism]]. Emoji often become metonyms as well, standing for things beyond their literal picture; see [[Emoji]].
