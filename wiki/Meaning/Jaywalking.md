**Jaywalking** is the offense of crossing a street outside the places and manners the law prescribes — mid-block, away from a marked crosswalk, or diagonally through an intersection. The category barely existed before automobiles arrived. On the standard account, argued by the historian Peter Norton in *Fighting Traffic*, it was manufactured largely in the United States during the 1920s to move blame for pedestrian deaths from cars onto the people they killed — an interpretation of motive rather than a settled finding, though the campaign it rests on is well documented.

Before the law caught up with the automobile, streets were shared space, and a driver who killed a pedestrian was treated as at fault, the way any operator of dangerous machinery would be. The automobile industry, facing public anger and city ordinances that restricted car speed, funded safety councils that ran organized campaigns mocking pedestrians who crossed mid-block, even staging events where children jeered at offenders — a coordinated appearance of grassroots outrage closer to [[Astroturfing]] than to any spontaneous shift in public feeling. The industry promoted the word without inventing it: "jay" was period slang for a rube, "jay driver" is attested around 1905, and "jay walker" appears in print by 1909, well before the campaign that moralized it and made it stick. The insult cast the pedestrian as the fool for not deferring to a car's right of way. That [[Naming]] did most of the persuasive work, reassigning the [[Externality]] of a car crash from the driver's mistake to the walker's carelessness.

The campaign succeeded well beyond its immediate goal. It produced the ordinances themselves — mid-block crossing, crossing outside a designated crosswalk, and diagonal crossing all became offenses — and helped build a [[Moral Panic]] around reckless walking rather than reckless driving. A century on, the [[Path Dependence]] is intact: street design, traffic law, and casual blame still default to the pedestrian being at fault, and the underlying [[Legibility]] shift — a crash rendered legible as an individual's bad choice rather than a system's design — has long outlasted anyone's memory of where the word came from.
