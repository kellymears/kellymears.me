**Baumol's cost disease** explains why some services keep getting more expensive relative to everything else, even when nothing is wrong with them. The economists William Baumol and William Bowen described it in 1966, studying why performing arts organizations kept running deficits.

Their example was a string quartet. Playing a Beethoven quartet took four musicians about 40 minutes in 1800, and it takes four musicians about 40 minutes today. The productivity of musicians hasn't changed. But the productivity of factory workers has risen enormously, and wages across the whole economy have risen with it. Musicians must be paid enough that they don't leave for other jobs, so their wages rise too, without any matching increase in output. The cost of a concert therefore climbs relative to the cost of manufactured goods.

The same logic applies to any work where human time is the product:

- **Education.** A teacher can only teach so many students well; class sizes haven't fallen in proportion to economic growth.
- **Healthcare.** Nursing, surgery, and care for elderly people remain labor-intensive.
- **Personal services.** Haircuts, therapy, childcare, and restaurant service.
- **Craft and repair.** Fixing things by hand often costs more than buying new, manufactured replacements; see [[Right to Repair]].

Meanwhile, goods whose production improves with technology get steadily cheaper: televisions, clothing, and computers cost a fraction of what they once did, measured in hours of work.

A crucial point is that cost disease isn't a disaster. Society as a whole is richer because manufacturing became more productive, so it can afford the more expensive services. The relative price rises, but people still get more of both. The problem is perception and budgets: public spending on schools and hospitals rises steadily, which can look like waste or mismanagement even when it's mostly this effect. Some rising costs do come from inefficiency or [[Rent-Seeking]], and separating the two is a real policy challenge.

Consequences and responses:

- **Shift in spending.** Over time, more of the economy is spent on stagnant-productivity services, which drags down overall productivity growth.
- **Automation pressure.** Industries try to make services more productive with technology: recorded music, online courses, automated checkout. Sometimes quality survives; sometimes the service just gets worse.
- **Policy debate.** Arguments over funding public services, and over measures such as [[Universal Basic Income]], often turn on how fast these costs will grow.

The idea raises live questions as AI systems take on tasks once limited by human time, such as tutoring, writing, and diagnosis. If they make such services more productive, cost disease could ease in some sectors, while the remaining human-only work could become relatively more expensive still; see [[Amdahl's Law]] for a similar logic of bottlenecks.
