A **third place** is an informal public gathering spot separate from home, the first place, and work, the second. The sociologist Ray Oldenburg introduced the term in *The Great Good Place* (1989), pointing to cafés, pubs, barbershops, diners, bookstores, and community centers as places where people spend time with each other for no particular purpose, and where neighborhoods become communities.

Oldenburg listed the traits that make a place work this way:

- **Neutral ground.** Nobody is a host, so people come and go freely without obligation.
- **A leveler.** Status from outside matters little; a laborer and an executive meet as equals.
- **Conversation as the main activity.** The talk is lively and playful, and it is the point of being there.
- **Accessibility.** The place is open long hours, close to home, and cheap or free to use.
- **Regulars.** A core of familiar faces sets the tone and welcomes newcomers.
- **A low profile.** The place is plain and unpretentious, which makes people feel at ease.
- **A home away from home.** People feel a sense of belonging and ownership.

Third places matter because they produce the casual, repeated contact that turns strangers into acquaintances. Those loose ties spread news and opportunities, build trust, and help people feel part of somewhere; see [[Strength of Weak Ties]] and [[Social Capital]]. They also give people a place to discuss public affairs, which Oldenburg considered important for democracy.

He argued that American third places were disappearing. Suburbs separated homes from shops and required driving everywhere; chain businesses favored quick turnover over lingering; and entertainment moved into the home with television. The decline is often linked to rising loneliness, although the causes are debated.

Some third places have moved online. Researchers in the 2000s argued that multiplayer games could serve as third places, since players gathered in taverns and guild halls to chat as much as to play; see [[Live Service Game]] and [[Cozy Game]]. Chat channels, forums, and community servers played a similar role, and rules about how to behave often came from regulars; see [[Internet Relay Chat]]. A sudden influx of newcomers can overwhelm a community's habits; see [[Eternal September]].

Critics note that online spaces differ in important ways. Many are run by companies designed to maximize engagement rather than conversation, and algorithmic feeds replace the regulars and familiar faces that give a place its character; see [[Doomscrolling]] and [[Context Collapse]]. Some audiences replace real two-way relationships with one-sided ones; see [[Parasocial Relationship]].

Libraries are often cited as the last free third places, open to everyone without buying anything, though they are rarely places for conversation. Group size matters too: regulars can only know so many people; see [[Dunbar's Number]].
