**Social identity theory** holds that part of a person's sense of self comes from the groups they belong to, such as nation, team, profession, religion, or fandom, and that people seek a positive identity partly by viewing their own groups favorably compared with others. The psychologists Henri Tajfel and John Turner developed it in the 1970s.

The theory grew from a surprising finding. In the **minimal group** experiments, Tajfel divided schoolboys into groups on trivial grounds, such as whether they preferred paintings by Klee or Kandinsky, and in some versions at random. The boys never met their groups. Asked to allocate small amounts of money between anonymous others, they favored their own group. Strikingly, they often preferred allocations that maximized the difference between groups over ones that gave their own group more in absolute terms. Belonging, it seemed, was enough to produce bias, without any conflict of interest or history.

Several processes follow:

- **Categorization.** Sorting people into groups exaggerates differences between groups and similarities within them. Outsiders seem alike ("they're all the same") while insiders seem varied.
- **Identification.** People adopt the norms of groups they identify with, which can drive conformity and obedience; see [[Conformity]].
- **Comparison.** People compare their group with others in ways that favor their own; see [[Social Comparison]].

Group membership also shapes interpretation. Good acts by one's own group are credited to character and bad ones to circumstance, while for other groups the pattern reverses; see [[Fundamental Attribution Error]]. Group loyalty shapes which facts people accept; see [[Motivated Reasoning]]. Markers of belonging, such as words, pronunciations, and codes, police the boundary; see [[Shibboleth]], [[Code-Switching]], and [[Dog Whistle]].

Muzafer Sherif's **Robbers Cave** study of 1954 placed two groups of boys at a summer camp, generated hostility through competitions, and then reduced it with goals requiring cooperation. It is widely taught, though later archival research found an earlier, unpublished attempt in which the boys refused to turn on each other, a reminder that the hostility was partly engineered.

The **contact hypothesis**, proposed by Gordon Allport in 1954, holds that contact between groups reduces prejudice under the right conditions: equal status, shared goals, cooperation, and support from authorities. A 2006 meta-analysis of over 500 studies found that contact generally reduces prejudice, even when not all conditions are met.

Social identity helps explain nationalism, sports rivalry, political polarization, and online tribes; see [[Imagined Community]] and [[Filter Bubble]]. Threats to a group can intensify identification, and reminders of mortality strengthen attachment to one's own worldview; see [[Terror Management Theory]] and [[Moral Panic]]. Group identity can also enable cooperation, solidarity, and collective action; see [[Collective Action]].
