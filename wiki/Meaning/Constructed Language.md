A **constructed language**, or conlang, is one consciously designed by a person or group rather than developed naturally. People have invented hundreds, for reasons ranging from world peace to fictional worlds.

**Philosophical languages** came first. In the 17th century, thinkers including John Wilkins designed languages whose words would encode the classification of all concepts, so that a word's form would show its place in an ordering of the world. Wilkins published his in 1668. The project foundered on the impossibility of a single agreed classification of everything; see [[Taxonomy]] and [[Ontology]].

**International auxiliary languages** aim to be easy second languages for everyone. Volapük, published in 1879, briefly attracted a large following before splitting over reforms. **Esperanto**, published in 1887 by L. L. Zamenhof, an eye doctor in a multilingual city of the Russian Empire, became the most successful. Its grammar is regular, with no irregular verbs, and its vocabulary draws mainly on European languages. It has speakers worldwide, literature, and even a small number of native speakers raised in it. Yet no auxiliary language has become a global standard; adoption depends on others adopting it first, and English spread instead; see [[Network Effect]] and [[Schelling Point]].

**Logical languages** test ideas. Loglan, begun in 1955 by James Cooke Brown, aimed partly to test whether language shapes thought; its successor Lojban has an unambiguous grammar that computers can parse; see [[Linguistic Relativity]].

**Artistic languages** serve fiction. J. R. R. Tolkien, a philologist, invented Elvish languages with histories and sound changes, and said his stories were made to provide a world for the languages. Marc Okrand created Klingon for *Star Trek* films in the 1980s, and fans have translated Shakespeare into it. David J. Peterson built Dothraki and other languages for television in the 2010s. Such languages add depth to imagined worlds; see [[Diegesis]].

**Minimalist languages** explore simplicity. **Toki Pona**, created by Sonja Lang in 2001, has only about 120 to 140 words, so speakers must describe things by combining basic ideas, a practice that resembles some approaches to clear writing; see [[Plain Language]].

Constructed languages illuminate natural ones. Designers must make decisions natural languages make by drift, such as whether nouns have gender, how sounds combine, and how words are ordered; see [[Grammatical Gender]]. Words in heavily used conlangs come to show patterns of frequency like natural ones; see [[Zipf's Law]]. When Esperanto gained native speakers, their speech began to drift from the textbook rules, as natural languages do; see [[Creole Language]] and [[Semantic Change]].

Unicode has declined to encode the Klingon script in its standard, citing little real use in text, though fans use it in private encodings; see [[Character Encoding]].
