An **information cascade** happens when people, deciding one after another, rationally ignore their own private information and copy the choices of those ahead of them. Each person is being sensible, yet the group as a whole can settle firmly on the wrong answer. The economists Sushil Bikhchandani, David Hirshleifer, and Ivo Welch formalized the idea in 1992, alongside Abhijit Banerjee's parallel model of herd behavior.

The classic illustration uses two restaurants side by side. Each diner has a weak private hint about which is better, but can also see how full each one is. If the first two diners happen to pick restaurant A, the third diner, whose own hint favors B, reasons that two signals for A outweigh one for B, and goes to A. From then on every diner faces the same logic, and the crowd at A grows regardless of what anyone privately believes; see [[Bayes' Theorem]].

The trap is that once the cascade starts, choices stop carrying information. A hundred people in restaurant A tell you only what the first two thought. The crowd looks like overwhelming evidence while resting on almost none. Copying isn't irrational here; it just stops pooling what people know, which is the opposite of the condition that makes crowds accurate; see [[Wisdom of the Crowd]] and [[Social Proof]].

Properties that follow from the model:

- **Fragility.** Because the cascade rests on so little, one piece of public news or one visibly confident dissenter can reverse it abruptly. Fads appear and collapse for this reason.
- **Accident.** Small chances in the first few choices decide the outcome; see [[Path Dependence]].
- **Silence.** People who doubt the herd keep quiet, so the doubt never becomes visible; see [[Pluralistic Ignorance]] and [[Spiral of Silence]].

Examples are everywhere. Bestseller and chart lists feed on themselves. In a 2006 experiment, Matthew Salganik and colleagues let thousands of people download songs in separate "worlds" that each showed their own download counts, and the same songs became hits in one world and flops in another; see [[Matthew Effect]]. Citation counts, app store rankings, and bank runs show the same pattern, and investors buying because others are buying is one engine of a [[Speculative Bubble]].

Related ideas apply to beliefs rather than choices. An [[Availability Cascade]] spreads a belief through repetition in public discussion, and [[Groupthink]] describes a small team suppressing dissent for the sake of harmony.

Remedies aim at keeping private signals visible. Juries and committees can vote secretly, or have members write down their estimates before discussion starts, and reviewers can rate an item before seeing anyone else's rating. Recommendation systems that display popularity counts tend to amplify early luck instead; see [[Recommender System]].
