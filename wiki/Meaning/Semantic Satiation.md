**Semantic satiation** is the experience of a word losing its meaning after being repeated many times in a row — spoken or subvocalized ten or twenty times, until it turns into pure sound. The effect was noted early in the twentieth century as a "lapse of meaning" and given its current name by the psychologist Leon Jakobovits in a 1962 McGill doctoral dissertation. It sits beside the [[Mondegreen]], the [[Eggcorn]], the [[Snowclone]], and the [[Backronym]] in a broader [[Taxonomy]] of word-level oddities, unique among them in erasing meaning rather than replacing or repurposing it.

The standard account is reactive inhibition at the level of a single neural representation, not fatigue of the ear or mouth. Accessing a word's meaning repeatedly fires the same lexical-semantic pathway, and its activation threshold rises with each firing, the way a muscle briefly resists a movement just performed. On that account phonological processing — recognizing and producing the sound — is a separate system that stays intact throughout, which is what produces the experience: the word is heard perfectly and says nothing. The locus is genuinely unsettled, though. Pilotti, Antrobus and Duff (1997) argued the adaptation is presemantic, sited in the very perceptual layer the standard account holds constant; Kounios and colleagues (2000) pushed back on perceptual-impoverishment accounts in turn, favouring a semantic locus.

Psychology experiments use the effect both as a probe and as a tool. Reaction-time studies show slowed semantic judgment on a satiated word while perceptual identification stays fast, isolating meaning access — the kind of category judgment [[Prototype Theory]] describes — from perception. It has also been used therapeutically, repeating a word past the point of felt meaning to blunt the emotional charge it carries. Part of the loss is structural too: a word said alone, outside the [[Collocation]] that ordinarily carries some of its sense, is missing half of what fixes its meaning — the [[Ground Truth]] meaning has not changed, only its momentary accessibility. Whatever the locus, the effect is brief and reverses once repetition stops.
