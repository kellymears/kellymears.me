A **gift economy** is a system in which things circulate as gifts rather than through sale or barter. No price is set and nothing is paid on the spot, yet gifts are rarely free: accepting one creates an obligation, and the ongoing exchange of gifts builds and maintains relationships. Anthropologists have found gift exchange in nearly every society, often alongside markets rather than instead of them.

Marcel Mauss's essay *The Gift* (1925) laid out the classic analysis. He argued that gift exchange rests on three obligations: to give, to receive, and to give back. Refusing a gift, or failing to return one, is an insult that breaks a relationship. Gifts carry something of the giver with them, so they bind people together in a way that a completed sale, which ends the relationship, does not.

Two cases shaped the theory:

- **The Kula ring.** Bronisław Malinowski described how people in the Trobriand Islands sailed long distances to exchange shell necklaces and armbands, which traveled in opposite directions around a ring of islands. The objects had no practical use and were always passed on; their value lay in the partnerships and prestige the exchange created.
- **The potlatch.** Among peoples of the Pacific Northwest coast, such as the Kwakwaka'wakw, hosts gave away or even destroyed large amounts of goods at feasts to mark births, deaths, and changes in rank. Status came from giving, not keeping; see [[Veblen Good]]. Canada banned potlatches from 1885 to 1951 as part of efforts to suppress Indigenous culture.

Gift and market logic can conflict. Richard Titmuss's *The Gift Relationship* (1970) compared Britain's voluntary blood donation with paid donation in the United States and argued that paying donors produced worse blood and weakened the sense of civic duty. Later psychology found that adding payment to an activity people do for its own sake can reduce their motivation; see [[Overjustification effect]].

Gift economies reappeared in digital life. Open-source software, Wikipedia, and answers on help forums are given freely, with contributors rewarded mostly in reputation and community; see [[Commons-Based Peer Production]] and [[Wiki]]. Eric Raymond described open-source culture as a gift culture in 1998, in which status comes from what one gives away. Licenses that require sharing improvements turn the obligation to give back into a legal rule; see [[Copyleft]].

These systems have known weaknesses. Many people take without giving, and a few contributors carry the load; see [[Free Rider Problem]]. Gift exchange works best in groups small enough that people know who gave what, and it struggles at scale; see [[Dunbar's Number]]. Repeated exchange depends on trust that the other side will reciprocate, a dynamic modeled in [[Prisoner's Dilemma]].
