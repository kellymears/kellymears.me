**Supply and demand** is the basic model economists use to explain how prices form in a market. Buyers generally want less of something as its price rises, the **law of demand**. Sellers generally offer more as the price rises, since higher prices cover higher costs and attract new producers. Drawn on a chart, the demand curve slopes down and the supply curve slopes up, and the point where they cross is the **equilibrium price**, at which the quantity buyers want matches the quantity sellers offer.

The model was refined by Alfred Marshall in 1890, who compared supply and demand to the two blades of a pair of scissors: asking which one sets the price is like asking which blade does the cutting. Above equilibrium, unsold goods pile up; below it, shortages appear.

The model's power is in predicting what happens when conditions change. A frost that destroys orange groves shifts supply and raises juice prices. **Price elasticity** measures how much quantity responds to price. Demand for insulin or gasoline barely changes when the price rises, while demand for a particular brand of cereal collapses, because substitutes exist. When something gets cheaper to use, total consumption can rise enough to outweigh the savings; see [[Jevons Paradox]].

Interfering with prices has predictable side effects. A ceiling below the equilibrium, as in strict rent control, tends to create shortages and waiting lists. A floor above it creates surpluses, and whether minimum wage increases reduce employment has been one of the most argued empirical questions in economics, studied with natural experiments; see [[Natural Experiment]] and [[Causal Inference]]. Ticket scalping exists because events price below what the market would bear, and surge pricing on ride-hailing platforms is an explicit attempt to let prices clear in real time; see [[Two-Sided Market]].

The model has well-known exceptions. Some luxury goods sell more as they get more expensive, because the price itself signals status; see [[Veblen Good]]. Markets with hidden quality, such as used cars, can unravel; see [[Information Asymmetry]]. Prices can detach from any underlying value when buyers purchase only because they expect prices to keep rising; see [[Speculative Bubble]]. And a single seller facing no competition can hold prices above the level competition would set; see [[Monopoly]]. Costs that fall on outsiders aren't reflected in market prices at all; see [[Externality]].

Friedrich Hayek argued in 1945 that prices are a communication system, summarizing scattered knowledge that no planner could collect, a version of the [[Wisdom of the Crowd]]. The same logic governs in-game economies, where designers tune the flow of currency and goods to keep prices stable; see [[Faucets and Sinks]]. Rising prices across the whole economy, rather than in one market, are a different problem; see [[Inflation]].
