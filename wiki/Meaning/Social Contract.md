The **social contract** is the idea that political authority is legitimate because the people subject to it have agreed, or would agree, to accept it. Rather than deriving the right to rule from God, inheritance or conquest, social contract theories ask what rational people would sign up for, and judge governments by whether they honor the terms.

The theories usually begin with a thought experiment, the **state of nature**, a picture of life without government. Thomas Hobbes, writing in Leviathan in 1651 after the English Civil War, described it as a war of all against all, in which life was "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short." Rational people would escape it by handing almost unlimited power to a sovereign. John Locke, in 1689, pictured a milder state of nature in which people already had natural rights to life, liberty and property. Government exists to protect those rights, rests on the **consent of the governed**, and may be resisted when it violates them. That argument fed directly into the American Declaration of Independence. Jean-Jacques Rousseau's The Social Contract, in 1762, located sovereignty in the "general will" of the people as a whole, an idea that inspired democrats and alarmed critics worried about the [[Tyranny of the Majority]].

Game theory gives the old argument a modern form. Without enforcement, individuals who each act sensibly can end up worse off together, as in the [[Prisoner's Dilemma]] and the [[Tragedy of the Commons]]. A state that enforces agreements solves the problem of [[Collective Action]] and limits the [[Free Rider Problem]], at the price of concentrating power that can itself be abused.

The obvious objection, made by David Hume in the eighteenth century, is that nobody actually signed. Most people are born into a state and have little practical choice about leaving it. Defenders reply that the contract is hypothetical, a test of legitimacy rather than a historical event. John Rawls revived the tradition in 1971 on exactly that basis, asking what principles of justice people would choose if they didn't know their own place in society; see [[Veil of Ignorance]].

Critics have also asked who was party to the contract. Carole Pateman's The Sexual Contract, in 1988, and Charles Mills's The Racial Contract, in 1997, argued that classic social contract theory quietly excluded women and non-white people, whose subordination the agreement assumed rather than questioned.

The idea also runs through everyday politics. Claims that citizens owe taxes or military service, and that a government which fails its people has broken faith, are social contract arguments. So are justifications of [[Civil Disobedience]], which accept the authority of law in general while refusing a particular unjust one. Even small groups rely on shared, often unwritten agreements about what membership requires; see [[Schelling Point]] and [[Imagined Community]].
