**Information asymmetry** exists when one party to a transaction knows more than the other. The seller of a used car knows its history; the buyer doesn't. The applicant knows how hard they work; the employer doesn't. These gaps shape markets in ways classical economics, which assumed everyone had the same information, missed. George Akerlof, Michael Spence, and Joseph Stiglitz shared the 2001 Nobel Prize in economics for showing how.

Akerlof's 1970 paper "The Market for 'Lemons'" used used cars. Suppose some cars are good and some are lemons, and only sellers know which. Buyers, unable to tell, will pay only an average price. Owners of good cars find that price too low and stop selling, so the average quality drops, buyers lower their offers, and more good sellers leave. In the extreme, only lemons remain. A market where both sides would gladly trade can fall apart because one side can't verify what's on offer.

Two related problems recur:

- **Adverse selection** happens before a deal. People who know they're high risk are most eager to buy insurance, so insurers who can't tell them apart raise prices, which drives off low-risk customers. Mandates and group plans exist partly to counter this.
- **Moral hazard** happens after. Once insured, people may take less care, since someone else bears the cost. It is a close cousin of the [[Principal-Agent Problem]], where one party acts on another's behalf and can't be fully observed.

Markets develop tools to close the gap:

- **Signaling.** The informed side sends a credible signal. Spence argued that education partly works this way: a degree signals ability and persistence to employers, whatever it teaches. Signals work only when they're **costly** to fake, the logic biologists see in the peacock's tail.
- **Screening.** The uninformed side designs choices that make people reveal themselves, such as insurance plans with different deductibles.
- **Guarantees.** Warranties, return policies, and money-back offers shift risk to the party who knows more.
- **Reputation and certification.** Brands, reviews, inspections, and licensing let strangers trust each other. On the web, a [[Certificate Authority]] vouches for who runs a site.

Each tool invites gaming. Reviews can be faked through [[Astroturfing]]; credentials can be pursued for their own sake, as [[Goodhart's Law]] predicts; a good brand can lend undeserved trust through the [[Halo Effect]]. Con artists exploit the gap directly, as in the [[Confidence Trick]].

The idea reaches well beyond economics. Software users can't inspect what's in their dependencies, which makes [[Supply Chain Security]] an information problem. Platforms know far more about users than users know about them, a gap at the center of debates over [[Personal Data]]. And when one side can't verify the other's claims, cooperation itself gets harder, as in the [[Prisoner's Dilemma]].
