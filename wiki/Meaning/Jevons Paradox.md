The **Jevons paradox** is the observation that improving the efficiency with which a resource is used can increase, rather than decrease, total consumption of that resource. The English economist William Stanley Jevons described it in *The Coal Question* (1865). Britain worried its coal would run out, and many assumed more efficient steam engines would make supplies last longer. Jevons argued the opposite: James Watt's efficient engine made coal-powered work so cheap that it spread into countless new industries, and total coal use soared.

The mechanism is price. Efficiency lowers the effective cost of whatever the resource provides, whether heat, light, travel, or computation. Lower cost raises demand. If demand responds strongly enough, the extra use outweighs the savings. Economists call the general phenomenon the **rebound effect**; the Jevons paradox is the extreme case where the rebound exceeds 100%, so consumption rises overall.

Examples:

- **Lighting.** The cost of a unit of artificial light has fallen by thousands of times since the 1800s. People didn't respond by spending less on light; they lit far more spaces, far more brightly, for far longer.
- **Roads.** Adding lanes to relieve congestion makes driving faster, which attracts more drivers until traffic returns, a pattern called **induced demand**. Studies across many cities have found vehicle miles grow roughly in proportion to new road capacity.
- **Computing.** Each gain in hardware efficiency has been met by software that uses more of it, and by entirely new uses that weren't affordable before. Data centers became more efficient per computation and still consume more electricity each year.
- **Language models.** As the price per token of model output falls, applications use far more tokens, generating long reasoning chains and running agents for hours; see [[Token]].

The paradox doesn't mean efficiency is bad. Efficiency makes people better off: more light, more travel, more computation for the same cost. But it means efficiency alone doesn't reliably reduce total resource use or environmental harm. If the goal is lower consumption, efficiency usually needs to be paired with something that keeps the price of use from falling, such as a tax or a cap; see [[Externality]] and [[Tragedy of the Commons]].

The rebound is often partial. More efficient home heating leads people to keep homes somewhat warmer, but total energy use still usually falls. Whether rebound exceeds 100% depends on how much unmet demand exists and how big a share of costs the resource represents.

Related patterns appear in personal life: a faster commute often leads to living farther away, keeping commute time roughly constant. Rising income raises expectations rather than satisfaction; see [[Hedonic Treadmill]]. In software, faster machines invite heavier programs, so perceived speed changes little; see [[Performance Budget]].
