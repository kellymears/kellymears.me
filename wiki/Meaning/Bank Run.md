A **bank run** happens when many depositors try to withdraw their money at the same time because they fear the bank will fail. Because a bank doesn't keep most deposits on hand, a run can bring down a bank that was healthy until it started. The fear causes the failure it predicts; see [[Self-Fulfilling Prophecy]].

The fragility is built into what banks do. They take deposits that customers can withdraw at any moment and lend most of the money out for years, as mortgages and business loans. This works as long as only a few depositors want their money on any given day. If everyone asks at once, the bank must sell loans quickly at a loss or close its doors.

The sociologist Robert Merton used an imaginary run on a fictional bank as the opening example in the 1948 essay that introduced the idea of the self-fulfilling prophecy. In 1983, the economists Douglas Diamond and Philip Dybvig gave the logic a formal model: a bank has two stable outcomes, one where depositors trust it and leave their money, and one where everyone runs because everyone else is running. If you expect others to withdraw, withdrawing first is the sensible move.

That makes a run a coordination problem, in which the outcome depends on what each person expects the others to do; see [[Schelling Point]]. Rumors and visible queues push expectations toward the bad outcome, and each withdrawal is a signal to others; see [[Information Cascade]]. The pattern resembles a server failing because every client retries at once; see [[Thundering Herd]].

Two remedies emerged. Walter Bagehot argued in 1873 that a central bank should act as **lender of last resort**, lending freely to solvent banks against good collateral during a panic. The United States created federal **deposit insurance** in 1933, after thousands of banks failed in the early years of the Great Depression; if small deposits are guaranteed, there's no reason to run. The cost is that insured depositors stop watching how carefully their bank lends; see [[Information Asymmetry]].

Runs haven't disappeared. In 2007, depositors queued outside branches of Northern Rock in Britain, the first such scene there in over a century. In March 2023, Silicon Valley Bank failed after customers, many of them connected through the same investors and group chats, requested tens of billions of dollars in withdrawals within roughly a day. Most of its deposits were above the insured limit, so the reason to run was intact. Money market funds in 2008 and some cryptocurrency platforms and stablecoins since have suffered the same dynamic outside traditional banking; see [[Blockchain]].

Runs often follow the bursting of a bubble, when doubts about asset values become doubts about who can pay; see [[Speculative Bubble]].
