**Prototype theory** is Eleanor Rosch's finding, from experiments in the 1970s, that everyday categories are graded resemblance to a central example rather than sets fixed by necessary and sufficient conditions. A robin is a better bird than an ostrich, and speakers agree closely on the ranking. Membership is a degree, which makes ordinary categories a poor fit for the crisp trees that [[Taxonomy]] assumes.

The evidence is behavioral. People verify typical members faster, list them first, acquire them earlier as children, and generalize new facts from them more readily — the results collectively called typicality effects. Rosch also identified a *basic level*, "chair" rather than "furniture" or "swivel chair", at which things are named by default. That default is what [[Plain Language]] means by the ordinary word.

Boundaries are fuzzy in a demonstrable way. William Labov asked people to name drawings of vessels and found the cup/bowl/vase line moved with width, with the presence of a handle, and with whether the vessel was said to hold coffee or mashed potatoes. There is no edge to locate, so no [[Ground Truth]] to appeal to.

This is why arguments about whether a hot dog is a sandwich never resolve. Each side reasons from a different prototype and neither is reporting a fact; the dispute is about category structure, settled by convention rather than discovery, which is why [[Naming]] a category is a consequential act. The same graded structure operates on individuals as readily as on categories: [[Flanderization]] describes a recurring character written, over a long run, down to their own most typical trait, until the atypical detail that made them a person rather than a type has been pruned for fitting the prototype too loosely. Ludwig Wittgenstein made the point earlier without experiments, describing *game* as held together by overlapping family resemblances rather than a shared essence.
