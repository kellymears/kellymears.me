A **loanword** is a word adopted from one language into another. English is full of them: "ballet" from French, "algebra" from Arabic, "tsunami" from Japanese, "kindergarten" from German, "ketchup" probably from a Chinese or Malay source. Borrowing is one of the main ways languages acquire words, usually for new things, ideas, or prestige.

English borrowed on an unusual scale. After the Norman Conquest of 1066, French became the language of the court, law, and administration for centuries, and thousands of French words entered English. The pattern survives in pairs where the animal kept a native name and the meat a French one: cow and beef, pig and pork, sheep and mutton. Latin and Greek supplied much of the vocabulary of science and scholarship. Estimates vary, but a majority of the words in a large English dictionary come from other languages, even though the most frequent everyday words are mostly native; see [[Zipf's Law]].

Borrowed words are reshaped to fit. Their sounds adapt to the new language, their meanings narrow or shift, and their origins fade; see [[Semantic Change]] and [[Folk Etymology]]. Japanese adapts English words into its sound system, so "television" becomes terebi. Some apparent loans are invented in the borrowing language: German uses "Handy," which looks English, for a mobile phone.

A **calque**, or loan translation, borrows the structure of an expression rather than its sound, translating each part. "Skyscraper" became Wolkenkratzer in German, "cloud-scraper," and gratte-ciel in French. English took "flea market" from French marché aux puces and "superman" from German Übermensch.

Borrowing follows contact and power. Trade, conquest, religion, and technology carry words along with them. Languages in close contact may develop simplified mixed forms; see [[Creole Language]]. Bilingual speakers switch between languages mid-sentence, which can be a route for words to cross; see [[Code-Switching]]. When one language dominates, borrowing can turn into replacement; see [[Language Death]].

Some communities resist. The Académie française, founded in 1635, recommends French alternatives to English terms, with limited success. Icelandic has long preferred to coin new words from native roots, so its word for computer, tölva, combines words for number and prophetess. Purism often expresses national identity; see [[Imagined Community]].

Within a language, borrowed and native words can carry different social weight, with Latinate vocabulary sounding formal and native words plain; see [[Plain Language]] and [[Diglossia]]. Loanwords also show who influenced whom: a language's vocabulary is a record of its contacts.

Borrowing isn't the only source of new words; speakers also fuse existing ones; see [[Portmanteau]]. Whether a borrowed word changes how speakers think about its subject is debated; see [[Linguistic Relativity]].
