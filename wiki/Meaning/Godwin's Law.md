**Godwin's law** holds that as an online discussion grows longer, the probability of a comparison involving Nazis or Hitler approaches one. The lawyer Mike Godwin formulated it in 1990 on Usenet, the discussion network where much early internet culture formed; see [[Usenet]].

Godwin didn't intend it as a neutral observation. He had noticed Nazi comparisons turning up in arguments about everything from gun laws to software, and he thought they trivialized the Holocaust. So he wrote the law down and posted it wherever such a comparison appeared, an experiment in spreading an idea on purpose to make a habit look tired. It worked. Within a few years other users were citing it before he could, and it became one of the best-known internet adages, alongside rules about parody and wrong answers; see [[Poe's Law]] and [[Cunningham's Law]].

Several conventions grew up around it:

- **The thread is over.** On many forums, the first person to bring up Nazis was treated as having lost the argument, and the discussion was considered finished. Godwin's original wording says nothing of the kind.
- **Reductio ad Hitlerum.** The philosopher Leo Strauss coined this phrase in the early 1950s for the error of rejecting an idea because Hitler held it. Hitler was a vegetarian; that says nothing about vegetarianism. It is a form of guilt by association; see [[Logical Fallacy]].
- **Invoking the law itself** to dismiss any comparison, whether or not it fits.

The law works because Nazi comparisons are the maximum-strength insult in Western argument, a shortcut that ends reasoning rather than advancing it. Using the strongest charge for small offenses also wears it down, so that the word loses force when it is needed; see [[Semantic Satiation]] and [[Euphemism Treadmill]]. Arguments that open with the worst possible reading of an opponent rarely persuade anyone; see [[Steelmanning]].

Godwin himself has stressed that the law was never meant to forbid comparisons that fit. In 2017, after white supremacists marched with torches in Charlottesville, Virginia, he wrote publicly that comparing them to Nazis was entirely appropriate. The point was to make people think before reaching for the comparison, not to protect anyone from an accurate one. Treating every comparison as automatically out of bounds can shift what counts as acceptable to say; see [[Overton Window]].

The law also reflects a property of long threads. Online arguments draw in more participants over time, often strangers with no shared context, and they escalate toward the most extreme available terms; see [[Context Collapse]]. Long threads and heated subjects have historically fueled waves of public alarm; see [[Moral Panic]].

Its template has been endlessly reused, with variants for other topics and other forums, in a pattern familiar from phrase templates; see [[Snowclone]].
