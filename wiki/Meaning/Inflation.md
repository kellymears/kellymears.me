**Inflation** is a sustained rise in the general level of prices across an economy, which means that each unit of money buys less over time. It differs from a price rise in one market, such as a bad harvest raising the price of coffee; see [[Supply and Demand]]. Inflation is usually measured with a price index that tracks the cost of a fixed basket of goods and services over time, such as the consumer price index.

Economists distinguish several sources. Demand-pull inflation happens when spending grows faster than the economy can produce goods. Cost-push inflation comes from rising input costs, such as oil. Expansion of the money supply plays a central role in monetarist accounts; Milton Friedman called inflation "always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon." Expectations matter as well. If workers and firms expect prices to rise, they demand higher wages and set higher prices, which makes the expectation come true; see [[Self-Fulfilling Prophecy]]. Most central banks therefore target low, stable inflation, often around 2 percent a year, and try to keep the public's expectations anchored there.

Mild inflation has costs, but extreme inflation is catastrophic. **Hyperinflation**, often defined as prices rising more than 50 percent a month, usually follows governments printing money to cover debts they can't otherwise pay. Germany in 1923 saw prices double every few days. Hungary in 1946 holds the record, with prices doubling roughly every fifteen hours. In such episodes people abandon the official currency for foreign money or barter, and bad money pushes good money out of circulation; see [[Gresham's Law]].

**Deflation**, falling prices, sounds pleasant but can be worse than mild inflation. If prices are expected to fall, people delay purchases and debts become heavier in real terms, which can deepen a slump, as in the Great Depression and Japan's long stagnation from the 1990s. **Stagflation**, high inflation together with high unemployment, defined the 1970s after oil price shocks and undermined the belief that the two always move in opposite directions.

Critics of large public spending, including some proposals for a [[Universal Basic Income]], warn that it will drive prices up, while advocates of [[Modern Monetary Theory]] argue that inflation, not debt, is the real limit on government spending. Services such as health care and education have tended to grow more expensive faster than manufactured goods; see [[Baumol's Cost Disease]]. Bitcoin's fixed supply was pitched partly as a defense against inflation; see [[Bitcoin]]. Episodes of runaway prices can also trigger a rush to withdraw savings; see [[Bank Run]].

Goodhart's Law originated in this field, as a warning that money supply targets stop working once policy leans on them; see [[Goodhart's Law]]. Online game economies suffer inflation too, when currency enters faster than it leaves; see [[Faucets and Sinks]].
