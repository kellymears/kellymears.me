A **shibboleth** is a word or pronunciation that reliably marks who belongs to a group and who does not — not by its meaning but by the way a speaker produces it. The term comes from the Book of Judges: Gileadites guarding the fords of the Jordan made fleeing Ephraimites say *shibboleth* — chiefly "ear of grain", secondarily "flood" or "torrent" — and killed those who answered *sibboleth*. How the test worked has been argued over for a century, and the popular gloss — that Ephraimite dialect simply lacked an "sh" sound — is not scholarly consensus: Speiser proposed a distinct phoneme in that dialect, Emerton a different articulation of the same phoneme that Gileadite ears took for /s/, Marcus a piece of polemical satire rather than a phonetic record; the standing objection is that speakers across the region could produce both sounds. What the account fixed permanently was the word's double sense — a test with lethal stakes — placing it in a broader [[Taxonomy]] of identity signals bundled inside ordinary [[Naming]].

Wartime versions kept the lethal stakes with the same mechanism: passwords chosen because an enemy's native phonology could not reproduce them convincingly, where the giveaway was how the mouth shaped a sound, not what was said. The test works because native phonology is acquired early and changes slowly — the durability behind the graded categories [[Prototype Theory]] describes — so an adult can master every word of a second language and still fail its accent, which is what makes the marker reliable rather than gameable, the same acoustic confidence [[Automatic Speech Recognition]] scores against expected native patterns.

Most shibboleths today are soft rather than lethal, and still do real sorting work. A regional vowel, a word for a soft drink, getting a loanword's [[Grammatical Gender]] wrong, all place a speaker's origin before a sentence finishes. Inside professions, the marker is often a [[Collocation]] rather than a pronunciation — a pairing of words a practitioner reaches for without thinking, harder to fake than vocabulary alone, since [[Ground Truth]] fluency shows up in habits, not a glossary. The same gap that turns an accent into a listener's [[Mondegreen]] turns it into a speaker's shibboleth.
