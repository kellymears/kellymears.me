**Duverger's law** holds that electoral systems where each district elects one representative by simple plurality, often called first-past-the-post, tend to produce two dominant political parties. Proportional systems, which award seats in line with each party's share of the vote, tend to support several. The French political scientist Maurice Duverger set out the idea in a 1951 book on political parties, though others had noticed the pattern earlier.

Duverger identified two forces behind it:

- **The mechanical effect.** A party that comes second or third in many districts can win a substantial share of the vote and almost no seats. The counting rule itself punishes parties whose support is spread evenly.
- **The psychological effect.** Voters who see a favorite party as unlikely to win switch to whichever major candidate they dislike least, to avoid wasting their vote. Donors, activists, and candidates make the same calculation, and the expectation that only two parties can win makes itself true; see [[Self-Fulfilling Prophecy]].

The result is a coordination problem. Voters on one side benefit from rallying behind a single candidate, and two big parties become the obvious focal points; see [[Schelling Point]] and [[Collective Action]]. A minor candidate who draws votes mostly from one side can hand the election to the other, the spoiler effect. Because established parties attract more support by being established, the pattern reinforces itself; see [[Matthew Effect]] and [[Network Effect]].

The United States, with two parties that have dominated for over 150 years, is the textbook case. But the law is a tendency, not a rule, and its exceptions are instructive:

- **Regional parties** can thrive under plurality voting if their support is concentrated, as in Canada, India, and the United Kingdom, where nationalist parties win seats in their home regions.
- **Party systems** shaped by history, religion, or language can persist despite the incentives.
- **Proportional systems** don't guarantee many parties; a few large ones can still dominate.

Political scientists today treat the relationship as a strong regularity running in both directions: electoral rules shape party systems, and established parties choose electoral rules that suit them.

Reformers who want more room for smaller parties have proposed changes such as ranking candidates, so that a vote for a long shot transfers to a second choice instead of being wasted; see [[Ranked-Choice Voting]]. Theory suggests no voting method avoids every such distortion; see [[Arrow's Impossibility Theorem]]. With two parties, competition can push both toward the center of public opinion, at least in simple models; see [[Median Voter Theorem]] and [[Overton Window]]. Whoever draws district boundaries can also shape which party benefits from the mechanical effect; see [[Gerrymandering]].
