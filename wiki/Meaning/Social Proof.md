**Social proof** is the tendency to look at what other people are doing, or believe, as evidence about the correct thing to do or believe — treating a crowd's behavior as information rather than just noise. Psychologist Robert Cialdini popularized the term in his 1984 book *Influence*, framing it as one of several reliable levers of persuasion, alongside reciprocity and authority, that work precisely because they're normally decent heuristics.

The mechanism is a rational shortcut that becomes irrational at the edges: in genuinely ambiguous situations, other people's behavior really is useful evidence, since a crowd running from a building probably knows something you don't. This is why the effect is strongest exactly where individual judgment is weakest — an unfamiliar restaurant, an unfamiliar app, a decision under time pressure — and why "as seen on," star ratings, and "most popular" labels are effective independent of any argument about the product's actual merits. The classic demonstration is Muzafer Sherif's 1935 autokinetic-light experiment, where subjects alone gave wildly different, idiosyncratic estimates of how far a stationary point of light appeared to drift, but after hearing others' estimates converged onto a shared group norm that then persisted even when tested alone again later.

The pathological end of the same mechanism is a pluralistic ignorance cascade: if everyone privately doubts something but assumes everyone else's calm confidence reflects real certainty, a crowd can unanimously act on a belief that not one member of it actually holds — the same structure behind the [[Bystander Effect]], where each onlooker reads the others' inaction as evidence there's no real emergency. Social proof is also the load-bearing mechanism behind an [[Availability Cascade]], which is essentially social proof compounding across a whole population rather than within one room.
