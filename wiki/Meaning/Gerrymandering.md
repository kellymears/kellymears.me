**Gerrymandering** is drawing electoral district boundaries to give one party or group an advantage. Where each district elects one representative, the same votes can produce very different results depending on where the lines fall, so whoever draws them can shape the outcome before anyone votes.

The name comes from Massachusetts in 1812. Governor Elbridge Gerry signed a redistricting plan that included a long, twisting state senate district north of Boston. A newspaper cartoon added claws, wings, and a head to its outline and called it the "Gerry-mander," after the salamander it resembled. The word stuck, along with the idea that strange shapes are the giveaway, though skilled modern maps can look quite ordinary.

The two basic techniques:

- **Cracking** spreads the other side's voters thinly across many districts, so they fall short of a majority in each.
- **Packing** concentrates them in a few districts they win overwhelmingly, wasting their surplus votes.

Combined, they can turn a narrow statewide majority into a large majority of seats, or let a minority of voters elect a majority of representatives.

Redistricting usually follows each census, when populations are recounted; see [[Census]]. Software and detailed voter data have made the practice far more precise. Mapmakers can test thousands of plans, predict each district's partisan lean, and choose the one that best survives shifting turnout; see [[Geospatial Data]].

Measuring unfairness turned out to be hard. A map isn't biased just because seats don't match votes, since natural clustering, such as one party's voters concentrated in cities, produces lopsided results on its own. Researchers proposed measures like the efficiency gap, which compares how many votes each party wastes. Others generate large ensembles of neutral maps by random sampling and ask whether an enacted map is an extreme outlier among them; see [[Monte Carlo Method]] and [[Outlier]]. Any single metric, once written into law, invites maps engineered to pass it; see [[Goodhart's Law]].

Courts and legislatures have responded unevenly. In the United States, racial gerrymandering can be challenged under federal law, but in 2019 the Supreme Court ruled in Rucho v. Common Cause that claims of partisan gerrymandering couldn't be decided by federal courts, leaving them to states. Several states now assign redistricting to independent or bipartisan commissions. Many other democracies give the job to nonpartisan boundary bodies, or use proportional systems where district lines matter less.

The deeper problem is a conflict of interest: representatives choosing their voters instead of the reverse; see [[Principal-Agent Problem]] and [[Regulatory Capture]]. Safe districts decided in primaries can reward candidates who appeal to their own side's base over the general electorate, one proposed contributor to polarization; see [[Median Voter Theorem]].
