**Pluralistic ignorance** is a situation where most people in a group privately reject a belief or norm but wrongly assume that most others accept it. Because everyone acts on their guess about the majority, they go along publicly, which confirms everyone else's wrong guess. The norm survives though almost nobody supports it.

Psychologists Daniel Katz and Floyd Allport coined the term in 1931. A well-known demonstration came from Deborah Prentice and Dale Miller's research at Princeton in the 1990s: students, on average, were less comfortable with campus drinking habits than they believed their peers were. Each student saw others drinking and assumed they were comfortable with it, while hiding their own discomfort, and many shifted their attitudes over time toward the imagined norm.

Classic examples include:

- **The classroom.** A lecturer asks if there are questions. Nobody raises a hand, each assuming they're the only one confused, and each reading the silence as evidence that others understood.
- **Emergencies.** Bystanders who see others looking calm assume there is no emergency, which is one mechanism behind the [[Bystander Effect]].
- **Unpopular regimes.** Political scientist Timur Kuran described **preference falsification**: people under repressive governments publicly support what they privately oppose. Because private opinion is invisible, regimes can look stable until a small trigger reveals the hidden majority, and collapse comes suddenly, as in Eastern Europe in 1989.
- **The emperor's new clothes.** Hans Christian Andersen's tale is the archetype. Everyone praises invisible clothes, each fearing they alone can't see them, until a child says what all of them know.

The **Abilene paradox**, described by management expert Jerry Harvey in 1974, is a workplace version. A family drives hours to Abilene, and afterward discovers that nobody wanted to go: each agreed because they thought the others did. Teams do the same with projects everyone privately doubts. Unlike [[Groupthink]], where members genuinely converge, the Abilene paradox involves people who disagree but never say so.

It's related to the [[Spiral of Silence]], in which people who believe their view is unpopular stay quiet, making it seem more unpopular still. [[Social Proof]] supplies the reasoning, "others are doing it, so it must be fine," but in pluralistic ignorance the proof is an illusion created by everyone's silence. The [[Overton Window]] can shift abruptly when hidden opinion becomes visible.

The remedy is making private views visible. Anonymous surveys that report actual norms reduce problem drinking on campuses, an approach called social norms marketing. In teams, a [[Pre-mortem]], anonymous voting, or asking each person to speak before the most senior person does helps surface doubts. A single dissenting voice often has outsized effect, since it shows others they are not alone. [[Blameless Postmortem]] practice and psychological safety exist partly to keep this channel open.
