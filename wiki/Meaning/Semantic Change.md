**Semantic change** is the way a word's meaning shifts over time. It's constant and ordinary: almost every common word in English once meant something different. A *nice* person was once foolish; a *girl* was once a child of either sex; *awful* once meant full of awe, worthy of reverence.

Linguists sort the common patterns into types:

- **Broadening.** A word comes to cover more. *Dog* once named a particular breed; *holiday* was a holy day.
- **Narrowing.** A word comes to cover less. *Meat* once meant any food; *deer* once meant any animal, as German *Tier* still does.
- **Amelioration.** A word gains status. *Knight* once meant a servant boy; *nice* rose from "foolish" to "pleasant."
- **Pejoration.** A word loses status. *Silly* once meant blessed; *villain* meant a farm worker. Euphemisms for unpleasant things sink this way repeatedly; see [[Euphemism Treadmill]].
- **Metaphor and metonymy.** A *mouse* becomes a pointing device; *the crown* stands for the monarchy.
- **Reversal.** Some words come to mean their opposite. *Literally* is widely used for emphasis in figurative statements, a use found in Dickens.

Causes include technology (a *computer* was a person who computed), social change, borrowing between languages, mishearing and reinterpretation (see [[Folk Etymology]] and [[Eggcorn]]), and the endless pressure for fresh, expressive language that wears out intensifiers. *Very* comes from a word for "true"; *terribly* and *awfully* became intensifiers as their original force faded.

Change creates practical hazards:

- **Reading old texts.** Readers bring modern meanings to older writing without noticing. When Shakespeare's characters say *presently*, they usually mean "immediately."
- **Legal interpretation.** Courts must decide whether a statute's words mean what they meant when written or what they mean now.
- **Skunked terms.** While a word is mid-change, either use draws criticism, so careful writers avoid it; *decimate* and *begs the question* are examples.
- **Technical vocabulary.** Jargon drifts as it spreads. *Agile*, *DevOps*, and *AI* each mean different things to different people, causing quiet misunderstandings; see [[Naming]] and [[Plain Language]].

Computational linguistics now measures semantic change directly. Word embeddings trained on texts from different decades show a word's neighbors shifting: *gay* moving from "cheerful" to its modern sense, *broadcast* from sowing seeds to radio; see [[Embedding]]. Language models trained on older text carry older meanings, a quiet source of error.

Semantic change also connects to how categories work, with central meanings shifting as typical examples change; see [[Prototype Theory]]. Repeating a word until it sounds strange is a different, momentary effect; see [[Semantic Satiation]]. Prescriptive arguments about what a word "really" means are usually arguments about which stage of a change to freeze.
