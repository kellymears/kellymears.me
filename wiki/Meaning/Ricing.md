**Ricing** is the hobby of customizing a computer desktop's look and feel in depth, usually on Linux, into a coherent aesthetic: a chosen color palette applied across the terminal, editor, status bar, notifications, and wallpaper, along with matched fonts, gaps between windows, transparency, and animations. A finished setup is a *rice*. The practice centers on sharing: a screenshot posted to communities such as the long-running r/unixporn subreddit, usually with the configuration files linked so others can copy it.

The word came from car-modification slang, where "rice" described cosmetic upgrades to Japanese import cars, often with the implication that the changes were for looks rather than performance. The term carried ethnic connotations in that context. Its tech usage has mostly lost them, and a later [[Backronym]], "race-inspired cosmetic enhancement", tried to launder it after the fact. Some people prefer simply "desktop customization" for that reason.

A typical rice is assembled from parts:

- **A [[Tiling Window Manager]]** or a compositor with configurable gaps, borders, and animations, often running under Wayland.
- **A bar** for workspaces, clock, and system status, frequently custom-built.
- **A palette** applied consistently across every program. Tools now generate a palette from the wallpaper and render templates for each application, which is the same idea as [[Design Token|design tokens]]: name the colors once and derive every surface from them.
- **[[Dotfiles]]** in a public repository, so the whole setup is reproducible and shareable.

The culture is mostly aesthetic play, and that is the point. It combines craft, identity, and a [[Shibboleth]]-like literacy in tools that most computer users never see. It also has recognizable failure modes. Ricing makes an excellent rabbit hole, and a common joke is spending a weekend perfecting a setup instead of using it, a form of [[Yak Shaving]]. Showcase screenshots invite [[Social Proof]] dynamics, and the latest popular look spreads quickly through a community. The more practical upside is real, though: someone who has riced a desktop understands, in detail, how its parts fit together.
